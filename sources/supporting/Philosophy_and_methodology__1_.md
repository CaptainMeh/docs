# Philosophy and methodology

# Brand philosophy

## Digital Infrastructure Stewardship

When people build digital systems, they have a lot of positive intentions: both for the organisation they work in and for society. But while they build them, lots of different forces pull them into other directions and perhaps the best case scenario is that they end up shipping something fast which falls short of the initial intentions. Very few actually end up being competitive, not to mention being competitive in a lasting way.

We treat digital systems as infrastructure, not as disposable products. Like physical infrastructure, performance is key. They must work under real conditions, handle failure gracefully, and remain viable over time. When they do not, the cost is paid quietly through downtime, workarounds, stalled teams, and strategic drag.

We work with organisations that do serious work and depend on their systems performing reliably under real conditions, so they need a design method they can rely on. That means building for performance in reality, not in demos. Including edge cases, not just ideal users. And this performance of the product must be sustained over years of operation, not just on launch day.

Our responsibility is threefold:

- **To users**, we build systems that hold up under pressure
- **To organisations**, we build systems that remain economically and operationally sustainable
- **To the broader digital ecosystem**, we leave the infrastructure stronger than we found it

We make the sacrifice, because this approach is not scalable in the usual sense. We take on fewer projects by design. But the alternative is contributing more fragile systems to an already brittle landscape and we don't do that.

But we are intrinsically motivated to make the effort, because the result is not just better software. It is organisations that outperform competitors who rely on shortcuts, patches, and surface level solutions.

We think it is a no-brainer: **If the system you depend on matters, how it is built matters too.**

**We treat every system as infrastructure that must be developed with responsibility.**

This shapes everything:

- We build for long-term viability, not just launch
- We transfer understanding so organisations can steward their systems
- We create foundations that support growth without collapse
- We make systems that fit the reality of operations, not idealized workflows

**We work with organisations that take their domain seriously.**

The best clients:

- Are genuinely committed to their domain, whether they are early-stage or long-established
- Serve their users with substance, not just marketing
- Face real constraints and complex realities
- Want their systems to actually work, not just look modern

## Why This Matters More in 2030

**Our work begins where established knowledge, best practices, and automated synthesis stop being sufficient.**

AI can accelerate what is already understood and recombine known patterns. It cannot determine what matters when signals conflict, constraints evolve, and the problem itself is still forming. In that space, judgment becomes the bottleneck. Our role is to structure uncertainty, surface tensions, and make trade-offs explicit until coherent systems emerge.

**As automation increases, coherence becomes cheaper and more dangerous.** Systems can appear internally consistent while drifting away from reality. What matters is not just that a system works, but that it continues working as both the system and its context evolve.

**As systems become easier to build, they become harder to trust.** Automation accelerates structure faster than meaning can adapt. The main risk shifts from technical failure to sense decay: a slow erosion of meaning between intent, behaviour, and interpretation. Systems will function, comply with specifications, and remain internally coherent, while drifting away from operational reality.

Decisions that follow procedure yet worsen outcomes. Post-incident reports that conclude "everything behaved as expected." Systems that do exactly what they were designed to do, long after the context they were designed for has changed.

**Reasoning becomes a competitive capability.** As automation lowers the cost of building and explaining products, competitive advantage shifts to the ability to make decisions that remain valid longer than those of competitors. Valuable reasoning will mean the capacity to delay closure when reality has not yet stabilized—preventing premature commitments that lock products into weak positions.

**Our method protects against these risks.** By preserving meaning under change, maintaining coherence that remains anchored in operational reality, and exercising disciplined judgment over time, the systems we build stay legible to users, workable for teams, and defensible as long-term business assets rather than fragile accumulations of optimisations.

This is infrastructure work. It requires patience, discipline, and the willingness to build fewer things better. But **for organisations whose systems actually matter, whose competitive position depends on performance that endures, there is no alternative.**

# **Critical Systems Design Method**

## What Critical Means

Critical Systems Design is Creative Navy's method for designing software products whose interfaces, workflows and operating logic have real consequences for the organisations and people that depend on them.

**Critical does not mean only safety-critical.** The method applies wherever poor design produces real costs — not just user frustration. A system is critical in this sense when:

- Users are professionals whose work depends on the system performing reliably
- Errors, ambiguity, or poor information structure cause operational failure, financial loss, competitive disadvantage, or safety risk
- The system must hold up under real conditions — pressure, interruption, edge cases, degraded states — not just in controlled demonstrations
- The quality of the interface and workflow directly affects the quality of the work being done

This covers a wide spectrum. At one end: aviation systems, medical devices, industrial control rooms, and other regulated environments where design standards like IEC 62366-1, DO-178, and ISA-18.2 exist because the cost of interface failure is measured in safety incidents. At the other: enterprise SaaS platforms, operational management tools, AI-enabled products, and complex internal systems where the cost of interface failure is measured in operational drag, lost revenue, stalled decisions, and competitive disadvantage.

What these share is not a regulatory classification. It is that design quality produces measurable consequences. A logistics platform where dispatchers cannot understand system state under load is a critical system. A financial operations tool where exception handling is ambiguous is a critical system. A clinical workflow platform where handoff information is structured for the ideal case but breaks on the common one is a critical system. In each, the design of the interface, the structure of the workflow, and the clarity of the information architecture determine whether the organisation performs well or poorly under real conditions.

**The distinction that matters** is not between regulated and unregulated industries. It is between systems where interface quality has real operational consequences and systems where it does not. Critical Systems Design addresses the former.

---

## Core Philosophy

Performance, when it happens, is always concrete: anchored in specific conditions, constraints, and operational realities.

Products must set up people to achieve performance in those real conditions when the moment is right. They cannot be derived from the abstract. They must be grown from reality to enable competitive performance.

Most UX work treats design as pattern application: taking established UI conventions, research rituals, and "best practices" and applying them to new contexts. This produces interfaces that look like design was done, but lack substance. The flows are generic. The features feel hollow. The system fragments under real use.

Our approach is different. We believe that **competitive performance emerges from understanding the terrain of reality in which systems operate:** the actual workflows, the technical constraints, the unspoken mental models, the systemic dysfunction, the limits of what we can know.

Critical Systems Design is Creative Navy's method for making products more competitive by growing them from the concrete realities where performance actually happens—while competitors derive theirs from abstract patterns, feature checklists, trends, and automated certainty.

This requires:

- **Evidence-aware thinking** (understanding what we know and what we don't)
- **Multi-perspective synthesis** (seeing the situation from all necessary angles)
- **Lateral exploration** (learning through the work itself, not rushing to solutions)
- **Tension-driven reasoning** (examining what drives conflicts to find the competitive vector)
- **Growing systems organically** (building coherence from observed reality, not imposing artificial structure)

## The Five Principles

### **1. Evidence-Aware Thinking**

We don't collect evidence to validate decisions. We use evidence to **understand the limits and terrain of what we know**.

- **What constitutes evidence depends on context.** Sometimes it's user interviews. Sometimes it's learning the domain ourselves. Sometimes it's observation. Sometimes it's participation.
- **We're explicit about uncertainty.** Better to understand the voids than fill them with absurd assumptions.
- **Observed behaviour over stated needs.** "User needs" are often wrong assumptions. Observed behaviour reveals reality.

**What this means in practice:** We spend time understanding rather than rushing to solutions. We do whatever it takes to actually learn: find the right people, take courses, observe in the field, participate in the work. **Domain learning** means our team often becomes productive users of the systems we're redesigning—studying manuals, performing tasks ourselves, understanding constraints from inside the work. This phase takes longer than clients expect because they don't realize their understanding has blanks.

**Competitive value:** Evidence-aware thinking prevents premature commitment to weak product frames. It protects differentiation by ensuring decisions are grounded in operational reality rather than plausible but incorrect assumptions.

---

### **2. Multi-Perspective Synthesis**

Complex systems require understanding from multiple angles simultaneously. Clients usually see their domain deeply but from one perspective. We synthesize:

- **What people are actually trying to do** (not what they say they need)
- **How the status quo is dysfunctional** (systemic issues, not just user complaints)
- **Unstated mental models** (the structure of understanding users aren't aware of)
- **Technical and operational constraints** (the reality that determines what's possible)
- **Where organisational assumptions diverge from field reality** (what headquarters believes vs. what happens on the ground)
- **The gaps between formal process and actual practice** (how work really gets done when procedures don't fit)
- **What gets lost in translation between roles** (where coordination breaks down because perspectives don't align)
- **The cost structures that shape decisions invisibly** (why certain choices feel impossible even when they're correct)
- **Where the product's history constrains its future** (legacy decisions that still matter but nobody remembers why)
- **Which constraints are real and which are assumed** (what actually must stay fixed vs. what just feels that way)

**What this means in practice:** Our team brings different ways of seeing. We extract the structure of the client's intuition—which is often half-right but blank of specifics—and fill those **blanks with substance** rather than generic patterns. This produces interfaces where every element has specific value and the overall structure reflects the actual situation of use.

**This synthesis is ongoing, not front-loaded.** As we build and test, new perspectives emerge. A constraint that seemed absolute reveals itself as negotiable. A workflow that appeared clear turns out to mask deeper dysfunction. We continuously integrate what we learn across all perspectives throughout the engagement.

**Why human judgment remains essential:** AI can collect perspectives and identify patterns, but it cannot hold multiple conflicting realities in mind simultaneously and determine which matters more in this specific context. That requires experienced judgment—the kind that comes from having seen similar tensions resolve in different ways across many projects, understanding not just what people say but what they mean, recognising when a technical constraint is actually a political one, sensing when assumed impossibilities are really just unfamiliarity.

The quality of this synthesis depends directly on the experience of who's doing it. Senior practitioners recognise patterns that take years to internalize: how cost structures constrain design choices in ways that aren't obvious, how legacy decisions create invisible boundaries, how different roles see the same system through incompatible frames. This isn't knowledge that can be automated or transferred through documentation—it's judgment built through sustained exposure to complex realities.

**Competitive value:** Multi-perspective synthesis prevents the kind of local optimisations that erode strategic intent. It maintains product coherence that competitors struggle to replicate through feature accumulation. More importantly, it produces solutions that account for forces most teams don't even see, creating differentiation that appears effortless but is actually the result of seeing the full terrain clearly.

---

### **3. Lateral Exploration**

We don't separate research from making. We learn by building, testing, and observing how solutions perform against real constraints.

- **Design as inquiry.** Each prototype is a question we're asking reality, not a proposal we're validating.
- **Multiple paths simultaneously.** We explore 4-8 solutions for key challenges in parallel, treating each as a way to understand the problem space.
- **Learning compounds.** Early experiments teach us what matters, which shapes later experiments, which reveal deeper patterns.
- **Ambiguity is productive.** The unclear space before solutions crystallize is where we discover what the system actually needs to be.

**What this means in practice:** We build rapidly and repeatedly during early phases, not to show progress but to learn. We observe how designs behave under realistic conditions, what breaks, what holds, and what reveals itself only through making. This looks inefficient to clients used to linear processes, but it prevents the costly pivots that happen when assumptions fail late.

**Competitive value:** Lateral exploration prevents premature convergence on weak solutions. While competitors commit early based on assumptions, we've already tested multiple directions and understand which paths lead nowhere. This produces differentiation that's hard to replicate because it's grounded in discovered reality rather than applied patterns.

---

### **4. Tension-Driven Reasoning**

We don't smooth over conflicts between what works locally and what works systemically. We examine what creates the tension, because that's where strategic direction lives.

- **Tensions reveal forces.** When local optimisation conflicts with system coherence, it exposes fundamental dynamics: market shifts, unresolved strategic questions, misalignments between business model and user reality.
- **We go meta.** We don't just resolve the tension—we understand what's driving it and where it's pointing.
- **Trade-offs become explicit.** We document why we chose this configuration over alternatives, making the reasoning available for future decisions.
- **The competitive vector emerges.** Understanding what drives conflicts shows where the product needs to evolve and where competitors will struggle.

**What this means in practice:** When we discover that the best solution for one challenge conflicts with system coherence, we don't compromise blindly. We investigate: Why does this conflict exist? What would have to change for it not to exist? What does this tension tell us about where the market is moving? The product concept that emerges articulates not just what we're building, but the strategic position it occupies.

**Competitive value:** Tension-driven reasoning finds defensible positions competitors can't easily copy. Most teams resolve conflicts tactically, optimising locally or forcing consistency. We use tensions as strategic intelligence, positioning products where market forces, user needs, and organisational capability align to create lasting advantage.

---

### **5. Growing Systems Organically**

Most agencies create designs as solutions. We create designs as **learning experiences** that teach us what the solution should be.

- **We work from both ends:** Micro-level user actions AND system-level coherence
- **We explore laterally:** Understanding and learning, not just pushing toward a final design
- **We tolerate ambiguity:** The space before clarity is where real solutions form
- **We build systems organically:** Coherence emerges from reality, not from imposed frameworks
- **Constraint respecting:** We treat legacy systems as constraints to work within, not obstacles to overcome—preserving what works while restructuring what doesn't

**What this means in practice:** We develop multiple options not as "concepts to choose from" but as ways to understand the problem space. Most of our designs exist to teach us, to reveal what works and what doesn't. Designs for production grow out of this exploration rather than being created from templates.

**Competitive value:** Organic growth prevents fragmentation and preserves meaning under change. As competitors add features that make their products harder to use, systems built through this approach maintain clarity and stay defensible as long-term business assets.

## Critical Systems Design Method: Phase Explanations

The Critical Systems Design method grows solutions through embedded experimentation, resolves tensions between local optimisation and system coherence, and stewards implementation until organisations gain independence. Unlike conventional approaches that separate research from design, we learn by making from day one, navigating the messy reality where what works for individual features often conflicts with what works for the whole.

Our engagement structure adapts to what each project needs, but these elements consistently appear:

---

### Phase 1: Sandbox Experiments

**Purpose:** Identify and explore the critical challenges that will determine system success or failure.

**What happens:**

We begin by identifying 4-8 key platform challenges—the points where design decisions have the highest impact. These aren't features to build, but fundamental questions the platform must answer. For example: How should multi-role workflows coordinate without creating bottlenecks? How can complex data become readable under time pressure? How should the system behave when constraints conflict?

For each challenge, we develop multiple lateral solutions through rapid prototyping and testing. This isn't concept generation followed by research. It's design-as-research: we learn by building, observing how solutions perform against real constraints, and understanding the terrain through making.

**Option space mapping** is central to this phase. We systematically explore multiple solutions for each challenge—often 3-6 variants per challenge—before converging on an approach. Each experiment produces evidence about what actually works in context. Some solutions reveal unexpected problems. Others expose assumptions that don't hold. The best ones teach us something we couldn't have learned by asking or analysing alone.

**Research is embedded, not separate.** We interview users while testing prototypes. We observe workflows while exploring alternatives. We benchmark competitors while building our own approaches. Learning happens through the work, not before it.

**What this produces:**

- Library of tested approaches for each key challenge
- Evidence about what works, what fails, and why
- Understanding of the terrain we're working in
- Clarity about which problems are hard and which just seemed hard

**Competitive protection:** Sandbox Experiments prevent early commitment to weak product frames. By exploring the terrain before converging, we avoid the costly pivots competitors face when their initial assumptions fail under real use.

**Duration varies:** From 2-3 weeks for focused problems to 2-3 months for complex platforms.

---

### Phase 2: Concept Convergence

**Purpose:** Resolve the tension between local optimisation and system coherence.

**What happens:**

We now face a design problem that most methods ignore: what works best for each individual challenge often conflicts with what works best for the system as a whole.

For each challenge, we determine which solution performs best when considered in isolation. This is local optimisation—choosing the strongest approach for that specific problem without worrying about how it fits with everything else.

Then we step back and look at configurations: which combination of solutions creates the most coherent system? This almost always reveals tensions. The workflow that's clearest for role A creates blind spots for role B. The layout that works for simple cases makes complex cases illegible. The pattern that feels intuitive in one module behaves differently in another.

**We don't hide these tensions. We make them explicit.**

**Then we go one level deeper: we examine what drives the tension itself.** Why does what works locally conflict with what works systemically? Often, this reveals fundamental forces shaping the product—shifts in how the market operates, unresolved strategic questions, or misalignments between business model and user reality.

**This is where competitive advantage lives.** The tension between local and system optimisation isn't a problem to solve away—it's a signal. It shows where the product needs to evolve, where competitors will struggle, and where strategic decisions will matter most over the coming years. Understanding what creates the tension tells us which direction the product should move to maintain its position as the landscape shifts.

The product concept we produce articulates the chosen direction and documents the trade-offs: why we accepted slightly less optimal local performance to gain system-wide coherence, or where we chose to preserve local excellence even though it creates systemic complexity. More importantly, it identifies the **competitive vector**—the strategic direction where market forces, user needs, and organisational capability align to create defensible advantage.

This isn't compromise; it's conscious navigation of real constraints toward a position competitors will struggle to reach. The concept explains not just what the solution is, but why this configuration works better than alternatives we explored, and how it positions the product to maintain advantage as the forces driving the tension continue to evolve.

**This deep understanding of tensions is what allows us to create design systems that enable adaptation rather than enforce rigidity.** Without examining what drives conflicts, design systems become traps—collections of patterns that crystallize one moment's compromise and prevent teams from responding as context shifts. But when we understand the forces creating tensions, we can build systems that remain responsive to those forces. The design system documents not just components and rules, but the reasoning behind them: which decisions protect the competitive vector, which constraints are temporary, where flexibility must be preserved, and how patterns should evolve as the underlying tensions shift. This transforms the design system from a fence that keeps teams inside safe boundaries into a map that helps them navigate changing terrain while maintaining coherence. Teams can extend the system intelligently because they understand what they're protecting and why, making decisions that preserve strategic intent even as surface implementations change.

**What this produces:**

- Product concept showing the chosen configuration
- Explicit documentation of tensions and trade-offs
- Rationale for why this approach beats alternatives
- Shared understanding across stakeholders about what we're building and why

**Competitive protection:** Concept Convergence preserves differentiation while resolving complexity. Competitors who optimise locally end up with feature sets that don't cohere. Our approach maintains the unique value proposition while making it operationally viable.

**Duration varies:** From 1-2 weeks for straightforward systems to 4-6 weeks when many stakeholders need alignment.

---

### Phase 3: Iterative System Building

**Purpose:** Develop the complete system, letting detail and coherence strengthen each other.

**What happens:**

We build the full system as interactive wireframe prototypes, working through divergent-convergent cycles that reveal and resolve tensions at increasing levels of detail.

This isn't linear execution of the concept from Phase 2. As we add detail, new tensions emerge. An interaction that seemed simple becomes complicated when we consider edge cases. A layout that worked in theory creates problems when populated with real content. Patterns that felt coherent at high level show inconsistencies when implemented across contexts.

**We use divergence and convergence deliberately.** When we hit a tension, we diverge—exploring 2-4 ways to resolve it. We build enough to see how each approach performs. Then we converge, choosing the resolution that maintains system coherence while solving the immediate problem.

As this process continues, two things happen simultaneously:

**More nuance emerges.** Details that seemed like decoration reveal themselves as essential. Edge cases that seemed rare turn out to define the system's character. The specifics that make features actually useful become clear.

**Coherence strengthens.** Each resolved tension makes the system more internally consistent. Patterns crystallize. The logic that connects features becomes evident. What felt like separate parts begins to work as a whole.

The final design doesn't exist at the start of this phase. It emerges from this iterative resolution process.

**What this produces:**

- Full interactive prototype demonstrating system behaviour
- Resolved tensions with documented resolution logic
- High-fidelity wireframes showing complete flows
- Demonstrated coherence, not just claimed coherence

**Competitive protection:** System Building prevents local optimisations from eroding strategic intent. While competitors accumulate features that fragment the user experience, this approach maintains coherence that becomes harder to replicate over time.

**Duration varies:** From 6-8 weeks for focused systems to 4-6 months for complex platforms with many interconnected parts.

---

### Phase 4: Organizational Integration

**Purpose:** Prepare the system and the organisation for long-term partnership.

**What happens:**

The design now needs to enter organisational reality. This isn't just "delivery"—it's preparing both the system and the organisation so they can work together effectively.

**Refinement with design system.** We translate the interactive prototypes into a design system that captures decisions, rationale, and behaviour rules. This isn't a component library alone. It documents why things are the way they are, what constraints shaped them, and how to extend patterns without breaking coherence.

The design system reflects the reality of the product—the constraints, the trade-offs, the logic behind decisions. It becomes the reference that helps teams maintain substance as the system evolves.

**Structured dissemination.** Different parts of the organisation need different things to work with the design effectively:

- Product teams need to understand strategic decisions and trade-off logic
- Design teams need component libraries, usage rules, and extension guidance
- Developers need specifications, interaction details, and implementation priorities
- Marketing needs to understand what makes the system valuable and how to talk about it

We tailor activities to how this specific organisation works. Large workshop sessions for some. One-on-one pairing for others. Written documentation. Video walkthroughs. Live Q&A. Whatever helps knowledge transfer effectively.

The goal isn't just that people understand the design. It's that they understand enough to maintain it, extend it, and defend the substance we built together when pressures arise.

**What this produces:**

- Design system with components, patterns, and rationale
- Documentation tailored to different organisational roles
- Internal capability to work with and extend the system
- Shared understanding of what matters and why

**Competitive protection:** Organizational Integration ensures the product can be extended without dilution. Competitors often lose their differentiation during growth because internal teams don't understand what made the product work. We prevent that erosion.

More specifically, this phase develops the organisation's intangible resources: **judgment about what matters** (the ability to distinguish essential from superficial when making product decisions), **shared product intuition** (a collective sense of what fits the system and what doesn't), **reasoning capability** (understanding not just what decisions were made but why, and how to apply that logic to new situations), and **strategic coherence** (the capacity to maintain the product's competitive position across many small decisions made by different people over time). These capabilities compound—teams that internalize this understanding make better decisions faster, extend the product without fragmenting it, and resist pressures that would dilute what makes it work. While competitors train their teams on processes and patterns, we transfer the understanding that makes those patterns meaningful.

**Duration varies:** From 2-3 weeks for small teams to 2-3 months for large organisations with many stakeholders.

---

### Phase 5: Implementation Partnership

**Purpose:** Actively support the design through production reality until the organisation can steward it independently.

**What happens:**

The design now enters development and roll-out. This is where most agencies disappear. We stay.

**Active oversight during development.** We monitor how the design performs as it's implemented. Engineers discover edge cases we didn't anticipate. Technical constraints require adjustments. Product priorities shift. We're present to help navigate these realities while preserving the substance of what we built.

This isn't policing—it's partnership. When developers ask "can we change this?", we don't just say yes or no. We explain what the decision was trying to achieve and help find solutions that meet both their constraints and the user's needs.

**Mentorship through roll-out.** As features launch, we watch how they perform in reality. We help teams interpret feedback, distinguish signal from noise, and decide what to adjust. We support them through the anxious early period when the system meets real use.

**Gradual retreat.** As the organisation gains confidence and capability, we become less involved. We move from active participation to advisory role. From weekly sessions to monthly check-ins. From hands-on problem-solving to answering questions when asked.

The endpoint isn't when the system is "done"—systems are never done. The endpoint is when the organisation can maintain, extend, and defend the system without us. When they've internalized the thinking enough to make good decisions independently.

**What this produces:**

- Design that survives contact with implementation reality
- Organisation that can maintain and extend the system
- Fortified team with embedded capability
- Knowledge that lives beyond our engagement

**Competitive protection:** Implementation Partnership prevents erosion during scale and rollout. Many products lose their essential character when they scale because no one is protecting what made them work. We ensure the competitive advantages built into the design survive contact with organisational reality.

**Duration varies:** From 2-3 months for straightforward implementations to 12-24 months for complex platforms being rolled out gradually.

---

### How the Phases Connect

These aren't waterfall stages. They flow into each other, and we often return to earlier phases when new information emerges.

**Sandbox Experiments** teaches us the terrain and prevents weak commitments.
**Concept Convergence** navigates tensions while preserving differentiation.
**Iterative System Building** prevents fragmentation as complexity increases.
**Organizational Integration** ensures capability to maintain competitive advantages.
**Implementation Partnership** protects meaning during the vulnerable transition to scale.

The method is less like a linear process and more like progressive crystallization—solutions become clearer and more specific as we move through phases, but each phase builds on the understanding developed in previous ones.

---

### Adaptations

Not every engagement includes all five phases:

**Audit or research-focused work** might use Phase 1 (Sandbox Experiments) to explore key challenges without proceeding to full system design.

**Small tactical projects** might combine Phases 3-4 (Iterative System Building + Organizational Integration) when the concept is already clear.

**Large transformation programs** might repeat Phases 1-3 for different parts of the system before moving to organisation-wide integration.

The phases describe the complete method. Real projects adapt based on what the situation requires.

Critical Systems Design applies to any software product where interface and workflow quality has real operational consequences — not only to safety-critical or regulated industries. - The method only applies to industries where lives are at stake
- The method requires regulatory compliance as a precondition
- The method is only relevant to embedded, hardware-adjacent, or industrial software

### Cadence (Sprint vs Phase):

These two terms operate at different levels and must never be equated in writing.

- **Sprint** — the working and delivery cadence. Creative Navy runs **one-week sprints universally, across every engagement**. The sprint is the fixed rhythm at which work is produced and reviewed; its length does not vary by project, domain, or phase. Only "one-week sprint" describes cadence.
- **Phase** — a stage of the Critical Systems Design method (Sandbox Experiments, Concept Convergence, Iterative System Building, Organizational Integration, Implementation Partnership). A phase is **not** a sprint; it is a run of several sprints whose total length varies with the scope and complexity of the engagement.

**Writing rule:** never call a phase a "sprint," and never imply the sprint cadence varies. The phase durations stated in this document (for example, "2–3 weeks to 2–3 months" for Sandbox Experiments) are ranges of elapsed time that span multiple one-week sprints; they describe how long a *stage* takes, not the cadence. Where it aids clarity, a phase length may be expressed in sprint counts — e.g. "Sandbox Experiments typically runs 2–3 sprints for a focused problem and 8–12 for a complex platform" — but the word "sprint" is reserved for the cadence and must not be used as a synonym for "phase."
