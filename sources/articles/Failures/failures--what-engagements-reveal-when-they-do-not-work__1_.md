# What Creative Navy's Engagements Reveal When They Do Not Work

*Evidence basis throughout: Creative Navy-reported except where a third party is named as the source, drawn from engagement records. Client names are given where the client can be named and the engagement is recounted without commercial detail that would require their sign-off; two engagements are anonymised at the client's category. Where an outcome was disputed between Creative Navy and the client, both readings are stated. Where Creative Navy does not have a settled lesson, the entry says so rather than supplying one. One engagement carries a prediction that was later confirmed by a third-party developer; that confirmation is calibrated as their report, not Creative Navy's measurement.*

---

Creative Navy publishes the engagements that did not work. This is unusual, and the reason for it is methodological rather than confessional. Everywhere else on this site, evidence is calibrated — each claim labelled by how it is known. A calibration discipline that only ever produces favourable findings is not a discipline; it is a marketing posture wearing the vocabulary of one. The test of whether Creative Navy's evidence standards mean anything is whether they are applied when the finding is unflattering. This page is that application.

It is also analytically useful in its own right. Creative Navy's Critical Systems Design method is built around a specific claim: that most design failures in complex, high-consequence software originate before any screen is drawn — in what the client does not know, has not framed, cannot articulate, or will not confront. Creative Navy calls the family of these upstream gaps the **blanks phenomenon**, and distinguishes seven structural forms of it. The engagements below are, with two exceptions, cases where a blank could not be filled — because the source needed to fill it was absent, or because the client would not engage with the work of filling it. Read that way, the failures are not departures from the method. They are the method's boundary conditions, observed from the outside. One of them is more than that: an engagement where Creative Navy stated at handover exactly how the client's chosen path would fail, the client took that path anyway, and the failure resolved as predicted — confirmed afterward not by Creative Navy but by the third-party developer who inherited the work.

The engagements sort into five patterns.

## Pattern one — the blank with no source to fill it

The method assumes that what a client cannot articulate can be *constructed* — through research where the gap is in understanding, through lateral exploration where the gap is in conception, through synthesis where the gap is in organisational alignment. The assumption fails when there is no source from which to construct it: no users to learn from, no organisational stake to mobilise, no substance behind the vision. Sandbox Experiments, Creative Navy's first phase, is designed to provoke a blank into yielding content. When there is genuinely nothing behind the blank, the provocation has nothing to work on.

**Big Sky (2017) — the empty vision.** A founder set out to build a professional social network that would connect people into excellent working partnerships. He spoke in vision and instructed Creative Navy not to worry about specifics — he would reveal them at the right time. Sandbox Experiments requires putting candidate content on a screen; that is how it provokes. But the founder had no view of what the system needed to capture to connect anyone, or what two connected people would be connected *around*. The gap was repeatedly deferred to artificial intelligence, which — this being 2017, before any usable conversational AI — was an appeal to magic, not a mechanism. There was no engineering team and so no way to establish what was even buildable. The vision was visionary to the point of being empty: nothing the method could provoke into substance, because there was no substance behind it and no source that could supply one. After two weeks Creative Navy recommended stopping, and advised the founder to commission engineers to build a UI-less prototype that would at least establish what the system could do and what entities it needed to contain. *The lesson, since absorbed into how Creative Navy scopes: a vision with no engineering reality behind it and no source to ground it is not an early-stage design engagement — it is a feasibility question wearing a design brief, and it should be returned to the client as one.*

**A Big Four professional-services firm (anonymised) — the absent stake.** The client wanted an immersive multi-monitor concept: executives in a room, interacting with touchscreens that reveal an organisation's cybersecurity posture. Six weeks, the purpose to produce a concept that could be taken to management for a build decision. A project team and roughly ten subject-matter experts were assigned to supply the content the screens would visualise. Two things defeated it. The experts were each available for an hour to ninety minutes across the entire engagement; and what they offered was platitude, nothing concrete enough to visualise. Creative Navy adapted the method — inverting Sandbox Experiments from a tool for *visualising* content into a tool for *extracting* it, building provocative visuals and researching external diagrams specifically to make the experts correct them, object, and in objecting reveal what they actually knew. The technique is sound and has worked elsewhere on exactly this problem. It did not work here, for a reason outside its reach: the experts had no stake. The concept belonged to one person, sidelined within the business, excited by an idea he had no knowledge to substantiate and no organisational cooperation to draw on. *The distinction Creative Navy draws from this: lack of knowledge among engaged experts is a blank the method can fill — the provocation works because they care enough to correct it. Lack of stake is not a blank; it is the absence of the precondition under which filling a blank is possible at all.*

These two, with EarthX below, are the **organisational and value blanks** of the framework in their unfillable register — the cases that define the edge of what structured provocation can reach.

## Pattern two — the client's conviction overrides the evidence

A different failure: not an empty blank, but a client who has filled it themselves, wrongly, and will not have it refilled. Here the method works — research and exploration produce the right direction — and the engagement fails anyway, because the client holds to a conviction the evidence contradicts. In each of these Creative Navy could have complied and produced something the client would have accepted. In each it judged that complying would have harmed the client's users, and stopped instead. These are recorded as failures because the engagement did not achieve a good outcome; they are, read another way, the clearest evidence on the site of what Creative Navy will not do for a fee.

**Tion — the demand for the irrelevant made prominent.** Tion had built their own unsatisfactory interface for fume-cupboard design and wanted better. They could fund roughly one-sixth of Creative Navy's quote, with the rest deferred to a later year, and accepted an intentionally partial outcome. Creative Navy scoped accordingly: improve the main dashboard, visually, to the extent the budget allowed, and use the limited exploration to establish a visual style that a later full engagement could build real UX and architecture on. At presentation the client wanted the options to differ at the level of UX — different elements composing the dashboard — on the grounds that the existing elements were not exciting enough. Creative Navy's position was that an element must be *relevant* before it is made exciting, and asked which elements would be useful to their users. The client could not answer; they did not understand their users well enough, and the three candidate elements they proposed they themselves agreed would be distracting and irrelevant. Breaking the impasse required the user research the budget excluded — research from which genuinely useful elements would have emerged, and with them the meaning that would let one be made both prominent and honest. Featuring something irrelevant prominently is not a flourish; it is a barrier placed in the user's path. The client insisted; Creative Navy stopped, because proceeding would have damaged the client and their users even though the client could not see it at that moment. *The client was disappointed and said so, and did not return.* The entry is kept precisely because of that: declining bad-fit work only counts for something when declining it carries a cost, and here it cost a client who left unhappy. (Tion is the near-inverse of the motorsport-simulation engagement in Pattern three below: the same "apply the method to one component, then one more" structure and the same advice against piecemeal work, but Tion ended when Creative Navy declined to continue, whereas that engagement ran the piecemeal path to completion and demonstrated what it costs.)

**Pflegewelt — the cinematic experience the users had no time for.** An NGO serving care workers (*Pflegekräfte*) in Germany wanted an online presence that would attract them with features and information and build a database for outreach. Creative Navy ran the initial phase properly: engaged care workers, learned their needs, sandboxed features, collected feedback, prioritised for buildability and reward. The care workers — people with very little spare time — pointed toward immediate access to the value of the features and information. The client, seeing the first UI, wanted instead a cinematic narrative experience with talking characters and entertainment, none of which had surfaced in any of the research. Creative Navy advised against it on the evidence: a cinematic experience takes control and time away from users who have neither to spare. The client held that this was their vision for what they wanted to bring into the world. Creative Navy is not an entertainment or branding studio, and said so, recommending the client find a provider who was. It then returned the money the client had paid in advance for UI work, and — at no charge — onboarded the replacement provider when one was found, converting its implementation-support allocation into helping the new partner understand the features and wireframes. *Recorded as a failed engagement; it is also the cleanest demonstration on the site of Creative Navy declining to be the wrong kind of studio, and absorbing the cost of the handover to protect the client.*

**Sharpcloud — the success the client claimed and Creative Navy disputes.** Sharpcloud's project-management platform was built on proprietary concepts visualised in a particular way: screens carrying 100–200 elements of around 14 types. The brief was to reduce screen complexity, improve element recognisability, and add navigation between screens. Creative Navy's assessment was structural: with that many elements of that many types on a screen, the load exceeds human capacity by orders of magnitude, and no visual treatment fixes it — only showing fewer elements, or fewer types, would. The client held that the core concepts and requirements could not change. Creative Navy therefore used Sandbox Experiments on the visual layer alone — shapes, colour, interface-design principles — over four weeks, and concluded. The client implemented roughly 60% of the suggestions and called it a positive engagement. *Creative Navy records it as a failure.* The interface had changed and was perhaps marginally better, but not in a way that would matter, because users would still be lost in the sheer count of elements. This is the rarest entry in the ledger: a paid, accepted, client-endorsed engagement that Creative Navy judges, against its own client's satisfaction, not to have produced a result worth having — because the constraint that would have made it matter was the one the client placed off-limits.

## Pattern three — the predicted failure: when a client runs the experiment against the advice

The entries above end in Creative Navy's judgement or in an unresolved question. This one is different in kind, and it is the most evidentially complete failure in the corpus: Creative Navy stated in advance precisely how a client's chosen path would fail, the client took that path anyway — staffing it with their own freelancers to work the way they wanted — and the failure resolved exactly as predicted. The confirmation did not come from Creative Navy. It came from the third-party development team that inherited the project after Creative Navy had left.

**A motorsport engineering company that had acquired a vehicle-simulation product (anonymised) — the illusion of control.** The client was a race-track mechanics business, not a software company. They had bought a sophisticated simulation product — race-car behaviour, computational fluid dynamics, the ability to model car configurations, test them in practice, and update a race-day plan — from the individual who originally built it, and wanted to develop it further. Their first attempt had already failed: they had paid a software company to extend the product, and that project was stopped midway. They came to Creative Navy wanting to do it properly this time, starting with the UX and UI. Creative Navy gave them an approach, a quote, and a full plan. Because their prior experience in the software world had been bad, they carried a trust problem into the engagement, and Creative Navy visited them on site specifically to build that trust.

The trust did not extend to the method. The client hesitated at Sandbox Experiments and the iterative, evidence-led path; they did not understand why Creative Navy could not simply produce the best solution directly, why the process needed iterations and evidence at all. What they proposed instead was to apply the method to the navigation only. Creative Navy did that, and produced five versions, and explained the downstream implications of each. The client then wanted to see the navigation with a visual style applied rather than in the abstract; Creative Navy did that too. The client then wanted another single component, the same way. At this point Creative Navy made the consequence explicit: continuing component-by-component would arrive at the very budget the client had said they did not want to risk, while never attending to the big picture that makes the components cohere — and Creative Navy strongly advised against proceeding that way. The client pushed; Creative Navy advised against it again. Meanwhile the client hired two freelancers to keep the work going in the manner they preferred. Creative Navy handed over and, at handover, named the outcome directly: the client now had *"the illusion of control, but it won't work."* The client insisted twice more on small isolated deliverables; Creative Navy advised each time that the work would look useful and be wasted money, and then ended the engagement. Creative Navy did refer the client to a developer it trusted.

What happened next is on record from the development side. The developer Creative Navy recommended stayed with the client for six months before the project was halted — again. The development team lead at the firm that did the build (Tecs Software) later told Creative Navy that everything Creative Navy had predicted had come to pass. The freelancers had not used the method; they had designed components the way the client asked, without the work Creative Navy would have done underneath them. Six months in, the client was where they should have been at one month. Components had been reworked countless times; the architecture made no sense; user needs kept surfacing unexpectedly because nothing was managing them; the work ran repeatedly into dead ends, until at six months the client recognised they were in a dead end again. The arithmetic is the part no success story carries: in those six months with the two freelancers the client spent roughly 70% of the budget Creative Navy had originally quoted — without a usable result — and that is before the separately wasted development cost, which was binned entirely.

*Calibration matters here and is stated precisely. The prediction — "the illusion of control, but it won't work" — is close to verbatim as Creative Navy delivered it at handover. The confirmation that it resolved as predicted is reported by the development team lead at Tecs Software, a third party who worked with the client after Creative Navy's departure and had no incentive to validate Creative Navy's account; it is their report, not Creative Navy's measurement. The cost figures (six months to reach a one-month position; ~70% of the original quote spent without result; the development cost written off) are client-reported, relayed through that same development lead. What the engagement establishes is the counterfactual the rest of the site can only assert: the cost of the method is visible in every successful engagement, but the cost of its absence — the rework, the incoherent architecture, the unmanaged requirements, the money spent reaching a worse position — is only ever visible when a client runs the experiment the other way. This client did, and a third party measured the result.*

## Pattern four — the client cannot hold focus or scope under constraint

A fourth pattern: the blank is fillable and the client is willing, but the engagement is defeated by an inability to converge — too many circulating ideas, or a refusal to abstract from emotional surface to operational core, under a resource limit that makes the indiscipline fatal.

**Social Match Play — emotion over backbone, scope over resources.** A small team wanted an app for friends watching a game together to predict, for points rather than money, what would happen next. One sprint of Sandbox Experiments surfaced two problems. The team's focus was the emotional, cinematic texture of the experience, and they could not abstract from it to the core flows and interactions the app needed as a backbone first. And the intended scope was steadily exceeding the initial description. Creative Navy raised both, and the way the first — fixation on emotional surface — actively prevented dealing with the second under a resource ceiling. The parties agreed to stop so the team could rethink its approach and work more out for itself before spending further. *A clean, early, mutually-agreed halt: the method surfaced the disqualifying dynamic in one sprint, which is the phase working as intended even though the engagement ended.*

## Pattern five — forces outside the method's reach

The last pattern is not about blanks at all. These are engagements where the work was sound and the failure came from elsewhere — a client's commercial collapse, or a contradiction in a client's own decision structure that informing could not resolve.

**Xpress Money — the external shock.** Creative Navy was two months into design-for-production — the UX delivered, the initial UI concept delivered and greenlit, perhaps 17–20 screens remaining — when the client announced bankruptcy, later revealed as a major fraud case that made the news. No one saw it coming; people inside the organisation were as shocked as anyone. The company went under entirely and built nothing. There is no design lesson here, and the entry does not manufacture one. It is in the ledger because it is true, and because it marks the honest limit of what design controls: the work can be right and the outcome still zero, for reasons that have nothing to do with the work. *Calibrated: external shock, no method implication.*

**Leo Cancer Care — the unresolved contradiction.** This is a high-consequence medical engagement, and the entry is the least tidy in the ledger because Creative Navy does not have a settled account of what it should have done differently. The work spanned two engagements: first an initial UI establishing the shape of a novel radiotherapy device and demonstrating that its UX was feasible, produced once the client had confirmed the device itself was buildable and found their partners; then, after roughly a year of mechanical development, a more detailed, implementable UX for a first functional prototype. Requirements were supposed to flow from a third-party engineering firm experienced in building radiotherapy devices, contracted by Creative Navy's client. Those requirements were relevant but generated a need for further research to settle questions to the standard that compliance, regulatory affairs, and the required level of detail demanded. The client's project manager liked the work and greenlit spending every two to three days — while, at the macro level, insisting the budget and timeline could not grow. That was a contradiction of his own making, micro-approval against macro-refusal, as though two different people were deciding. Creative Navy informed him of the consequences each time. The budget was ultimately supplemented and the final stretch curtailed to a point that left the work incomplete; the functional prototype was built to cover less of the device's functionality than intended. The client's PM was unhappy. Creative Navy was sorry about the outcome. *What the right move was — to have forced the contradiction into the open earlier and made each micro-approval confront its macro implication, or to recognise that some client decision-structures generate contradictions no amount of informing resolves and to contract against them from the start — Creative Navy does not claim to know. The entry is published without a resolving lesson, because supplying one would be inventing it.*

## EarthX — the failure that became a method

One engagement earns its own section because its consequence was not a lesson but a permanent change to how Creative Navy works.

EarthX (2018) was a small team building a geodata platform — two engineers and a business lead. The engineers were able and built core and mid-level capability that could become many different features; both held expansive visions of a mature product with millions of users, the "Wikipedia of geodata." The business lead did not engage with the detail of what the engineers were building; he sold visions to customers and investors, then derived something more concrete from each that resembled the pitch but not exactly. There was no access to the platform's actual users, so Creative Navy depended entirely on the client for direction — and at any given moment several different ideas of the product circulated at once: the vision the business lead had sold upward, the half-remembered thing he had agreed with smaller customers, and the different product the engineers described when asked to fill the gaps. Sandbox Experiments helped, but the underlying problem persisted: the business lead was low on detail, the engineers were describing something else, and the work of figuring out what was actually to be built proceeded slowly and inefficiently. A conclusion was reached, the client implemented it, won customers, and was satisfied — and midway the business lead changed the product entirely, having agreed something else with a different customer (the change happened to be a simplification, so it was a good one).

The engagement succeeded commercially for the client and was inefficient throughout. What Creative Navy took from it was not a lesson about EarthX but a new component of the method. Creative Navy learned first to *recognise the dynamic* — the multiple-circulating-visions, low-detail-leadership, divergent-engineering pattern — when it appears. And it built a response that is now standard: when the dynamic is recognised, Creative Navy spends one to two days mapping the circulating visions, operationalising each, building low-fidelity wireframes for them, and putting them in front of the client team with a direct instruction — *these are the visions in the air; you must decide what we focus on now.* It then runs a workshop on the visions **not** being pursued, deliberately, to discharge their salience in the team's mind, and sets the others aside to revisit in some months. In other words, since EarthX, Creative Navy actively *stewards the client's focus.* Most clients can hold their own focus; Creative Navy stays alert to the ones who cannot and steps in when needed, because no method produces a good result without sufficient focus — and Sandbox Experiments in particular must not be allowed to drift into exploring interesting things that are not the project.

This engagement also marks a broader inflection. It was 2018, and Creative Navy has become markedly more systematic since; the rigour that now characterises the method is partly a response to exactly the kind of inefficiency EarthX exposed. *This is the only entry in the ledger that is also a provenance record: a documented instance of a failure mode producing a durable change in Creative Navy's Critical Systems Design method. The focus-stewardship practice it created is described here for the first time.*

## What the ledger establishes

Read together, the nine engagements support four claims that favourable case studies cannot.

First, the evidence calibration on this site is real. It survives contact with unflattering findings — including one engagement (Sharpcloud) recorded as a failure *over the client's own stated satisfaction*, and one (Leo Cancer Care) published without a resolving lesson because Creative Navy does not have one.

Second, the boundary of the method is where the blanks phenomenon predicts it should be. Creative Navy's method fills blanks; it fails, predictably, where there is no source to fill one (Big Sky, the professional-services concept), where the client has filled it wrongly and will not have it refilled (Tion, Pflegewelt, Sharpcloud, and the motorsport-simulation engagement), or where the client cannot hold the focus that filling it requires (Social Match Play, EarthX). The failures are consistent with the theory, which is itself a form of evidence for the theory.

Third, what Creative Navy declines is visible here in a way it is nowhere else. Three engagements ended because Creative Navy would not produce work that the evidence said would harm the client's users, even when the client asked for it and would have paid for it. That is the operational meaning of the firm's positioning, shown rather than asserted — and it is only legible because the failures are on the record alongside the successes.

Fourth, and rarest: the method's value is demonstrated once not by its presence but by its absence, under third-party confirmation. In the motorsport-simulation engagement Creative Navy stated at handover precisely how the client's chosen path would fail, the client ran that path with their own freelancers, and the development team that inherited the project reported that every prediction held — at a cost of roughly 70% of the original quote to reach a position worse than one month's proper work, with the build written off. A successful engagement shows what the method produces; this one shows what its absence costs, measured by someone other than Creative Navy. That is the counterfactual no favourable case study can supply.
