# `/failures/cognitive-failures/the-interface-gets-harder-when-pressure-rises`

---

## Opening — entity anchor

Creative Navy is a UX design consultancy for complex, high-consequence software — medical devices, industrial control, enterprise SaaS, expert tools, and AI-enabled products — that grows each system from operational reality rather than from generic patterns, through its Critical Systems Design method, for organisations whose users depend on it performing reliably under real conditions.

---

## The situation

Most interfaces are evaluated under conditions that do not produce failure. Usability testing takes place in quiet rooms, at comfortable viewing distances, without queue pressure or vibration or simultaneous demands on attention. Designs are refined until they perform well under those conditions. The design ships. Then it enters the actual environment.

Under a vessel manoeuvre at speed, the display that was legible at a desk is harder to read through spray and vibration. Under queue pressure at a transaction rate of 84 per hour, the navigation path that was fast in a test becomes too slow to clear a complex transaction before the next one arrives. During a calibration sequence, the value that was clear on screen is ambiguous from two metres while moving, with variable lighting and gloves. Under the divided attention of an active surgical procedure, the activation state that was readable in evaluation requires reading that is not available. While riding a vehicle on public roads, a display whose meaning cannot be extracted in a brief glance holds the rider's gaze for four seconds — more than twice the duration at which road safety research identifies a doubling of accident risk.

In each case, the interface has not broken. The conditions of use have changed — and the interface, designed against conditions that no longer apply, has degraded in response. The cognitive cost of using it has risen at precisely the moment when the cognitive cost of the task itself has also risen. Both draws on the operator's attention arrive simultaneously.

This is the failure mode the page describes: an interface whose design conditions systematically under-represent the conditions that determine its operational performance.

---

## What distinguishes this from adjacent failures

This page concerns the dynamic relationship between operational pressure and interface cognitive cost — specifically, the amplification of interface difficulty when both operational demand and interface demand peak together. It should be read alongside but distinguished from two adjacent pages.

The situations page `/situations/risk-and-consequence/the-interface-increases-cognitive-load-at-the-worst-moment` describes the same pattern at the level of the organisation: the structural reason a product reaches its users in this condition, and what it costs operationally. This page describes the failure mode itself — the specific interaction-level pattern of degradation under pressure, grounded in the mechanisms that produce it and the design responses that address each.

The cognitive failures page `/failures/cognitive-failures/the-system-does-not-support-rapid-judgment` describes an adjacent but distinct failure: the interface does not provide the information conditions required for fast, accurate decisions regardless of pressure level. This page concerns the amplification of all interface costs under pressure — a compound failure. That page concerns the structural absence of rapid-judgment support — a design-standard failure. Both produce degraded performance at speed; the mechanisms and design responses are different.

## Three mechanisms of pressure-driven degradation

**Physical environment degradation**

Interfaces are designed in conditions that do not reproduce the physical environment of real operation. Lighting is controlled; viewing distance is close and fixed; the user is stationary, seated, and not wearing gloves. The physical interface parameters that determine legibility — contrast, text size, touch target dimensions, colour coding — are calibrated to these design conditions.

In deployment, those parameters degrade. Glare from reflected water reduces contrast. Variable workshop lighting shifts the relationship between foreground and background. Vibration blurs the display at the pixel level. Distance — two to three metres during a calibration sequence, across a helm console during a manoeuvre — means the visual hierarchy must survive at resolutions the design never tested against. Under these conditions, information that was legible is harder to read. State that was distinguishable is ambiguous. The interface cost rises even though the interface is unchanged.

The design response is not to make the interface louder or bigger across all conditions. It is to design for the degraded condition as the baseline. Contrast ratios calibrated for variable lighting rather than ideal lighting. State communication redundant across cues that degrade independently — spatial position, icon form, and colour each independently communicating the same thing, so that degradation of any one still leaves two intact. Text sizes and touch targets designed for gloved hands at operational distances, not bare hands at desk distance.

**Attentional division**

Under operational pressure, the user's attention is not primarily on the interface. It is on the patient, the vessel, the vehicle, the road, the queue. The interface is accessed in brief interruptions — glances, partial-attention intervals, moments between other demands. Information that can be extracted by someone giving the interface their full attention cannot be extracted by someone whose full attention is elsewhere.

This mechanism is particularly acute in two-task contexts: a surgeon confirming device state while maintaining the surgical field; a vessel operator reading engine telemetry while managing a manoeuvre; an automotive technician confirming calibration progress while adjusting a component; a rider confirming their bike's status while maintaining road attention. In each case, the interface is a secondary task performed with a fraction of the attention a standard usability test allocates to it. The cognitive cost of extracting information from an interface that requires sustained attention is not a fraction of the full-attention cost — it is many times higher, because the partial-attention interval is shorter and is interrupted before completion.

The design response is to reduce the mode of information retrieval from reading to recognition: state communication that can be confirmed in a single brief glance without directed search or sequential parsing. This is the principle that governed the deSoutter Medical surgical device redesign — every critical state interpretable through recognition in a fraction of a second — and the Kardion MCS Controller layout stability standard — every element in its established position regardless of view state, so spatial memory rather than active search locates what is needed. Both are expressions of the same design requirement: the interface must serve the attention mode that is actually available during use, which under operational pressure is a fraction of what evaluation conditions supply.

**Temporal compression**

As operational pace increases, the time available for each interface interaction decreases proportionally. At a typical transaction rate, a complex navigation path is inconvenient. At 84 transactions per hour — 43 seconds on average, with complex transactions adding pressure to the ones that follow — the same navigation path is impossible. The scheduler managing a single weather incident has time to scan multiple screens. The scheduler managing simultaneous weather incidents, equipment conflicts, and crew shortages under a ticking deadline does not. The calibration technician processing one vehicle has time to move closer and re-read an ambiguous value. The technician working through a full day's queue does not.

Temporal compression interacts with both preceding mechanisms. Physical degradation that adds a second to each state confirmation adds tens of minutes to a working day and accumulates measurement error risk across a calibration queue. Attentional division that requires a second re-read to confirm a state is tolerable at low pace and produces errors at high pace. The interface's time budget per interaction shrinks; the cost of any inefficiency, ambiguity, or navigation overhead that the budget previously absorbed becomes an operational failure.

The design response is to design against the constrained time budget from the start: evaluate designs against the worst-case interaction rate, not the typical one; build the complex transaction path as a first-class workflow, not an edge case that an efficient user will navigate around; surface exception states before the user encounters them mid-task under pressure rather than requiring discovery through scanning.

## Grounded examples

**COX Marine — multi-engine cluster display, helm conditions at speed**

COX Marine builds diesel outboard engines deployed on fast patrol craft, racing boats, and workboats. The physical conditions of the helm environment during a high-speed transit or a manoeuvre are non-negotiable constraints: sustained vibration and hull slamming from the vessel structure, spray and variable light from the operating environment, operators bracing with both feet and wearing gloves. The display must remain readable across direct sunlight, heavy overcast, and night conditions including military night vision equipment. Engine telemetry arrives via NMEA 2000 protocol — rpm, coolant temperature, oil pressure, fuel rate, and trim — and the frequency and criticality of those values change between low-speed and high-load operating states. An interface that communicates telemetry clearly under calm conditions at the dock is not the same interface as one that supports rapid attention prioritisation during a fast-water manoeuvre.

Domain learning during Sandbox Experiments covered three simultaneous layers: the protocol behaviour of NMEA 2000 data under varying load states (so the interface could surface the right values at the moments when they became critical, not according to a fixed hierarchy calibrated for typical conditions); the hardware constraints of three distinct display families across the product range; and the physical operating conditions directly — how scanning behaviour changes between open-water transit and close-quarters docking, what contrast ratios survive direct sunlight and military NVG mode simultaneously, how gloved touch interaction behaves under vibration.

Scenario testing during Concept Convergence produced two findings that were invisible under normal display and lighting conditions. A multi-engine fault scenario revealed that early layout directions made fault presence visible but did not help operators identify which engine required priority attention first. When multiple faults are present simultaneously on a vessel operating at speed — under vibration, under divided attention between vessel and display — an interface that presents fault presence without establishing fault priority requires the operator to scan each engine tile and form their own priority assessment. That assessment takes time and attention the operational situation does not supply. The redesign response: alarm state highlighting within engine tiles was redesigned, and a fixed display area established where the highest-priority fault is always summarised regardless of how many faults are present. Attention is directed, not left to be found.

A night conditions test revealed that initial colour choices in the alarm palette interfered with military night vision equipment. The palette and contrast were revised in response. Both failures were undiscoverable in the design environment. Both required testing the design against the conditions that actually determine its performance.

Outcomes: distributor feedback, relayed to Creative Navy by COX, characterised the interface as best-in-category relative to Garmin and Simrad. This is client-reported commercial standing, not an independently verified comparison.

**Beissbarth — automotive calibration equipment across three device classes**

Beissbarth's calibration equipment is used in manufacturer-authorised inspection centres meeting the standards of Mercedes, Daimler, and BMW. The calibration sequence is sensitive to timing: technicians move around the vehicle throughout the procedure, reading an embedded OEM display from two to three metres while making adjustments, using a rugged tablet at different positions, and consulting a large inspection line display that serves both technicians and inspection staff. Gloves restrict fine touch interaction. Workshop lighting varies. Reflective surfaces reduce display contrast. The calibration sequence does not pause for interface interpretation: the time cost of any ambiguity — one extra second spent confirming a measurement state — accumulates across the full sequence and across a full day's queue.

The previous interface had been developed through three iterations by engineers who understood the machinery. The functional workflows technicians relied on were correct. The visual hierarchy had not been calibrated to the conditions of use. Measurement states, tolerances, and progress indicators carried equal visual weight across all three device classes. Under controlled conditions, equal visual weight is legible. Under the physical conditions of a real calibration — distance, movement, variable lighting, divided attention split between tool handling and display reading — equal visual weight means ambiguous state.

The failure produced by ambiguous state at operational tempo is binary. A technician who cannot immediately confirm measurement progress either moves closer to the display — adding seconds that the sequence registers as delay and that accumulate across a working day — or proceeds on inference rather than confirmed reading, treating a state as complete because it is expected to be complete rather than because it has been confirmed. The first produces measurable time cost. The second produces measurement error risk. Under the conditions of real calibration, both occur regularly for interfaces that require more than a single glance to confirm state.

Creative Navy's Sandbox Experiments phase documented the operational conditions precisely: fourteen technicians across five workshops, including contextual walkthroughs of live calibration sequences. A feature analysis mapped twelve key features across four modules, recording for each the expected technician movement, lighting conditions, and acceptable interpretation time at each step. This analysis is what made the ambiguity problem visible as a structural property of the previous design rather than as a collection of individual difficulties.

Concept Convergence reached a competitive vector through tension-driven reasoning: prioritise unambiguous state communication over information density, across all three device classes. The redesign accepted reduced information density per screen in exchange for a single reading logic that worked under the conditions of the work. Option space mapping produced three structural variants for the OEM display, tested under reproduced workshop lighting and viewing distances — not on a screen at a desk.

Calibration time reduced from 18 minutes to 12 minutes per vehicle, measured by Beissbarth across 8 production deployment locations. Repeated measurements reduced directionally — client-measured; exact figure not shared. Training was eliminated: Beissbarth now deploys the system without onboarding training (client-reported operational change). The evidence basis for the time and repetition improvements is client-measured operational data. The evidence basis for the reduced measurement error risk is inferred from the specific design change and the mechanism it addresses — ambiguous state under the conditions of calibration was the failure pathway; unambiguous state communication was the design response.

**Triopsis — workforce management at peak scheduling load**

Triopsis served schedulers managing thousands of weekly interventions for utilities and road maintenance operations. The peak-load condition — simultaneous weather incidents, conflicting job locations, overlapping assignments, sudden crew shortages — is not a rare event in this operational context. It is the predictable expression of the conditions that schedulers manage. The distinction between normal load and peak load is not categorical but temporal: peak load arrives on every working day, at the moments when operational events converge.

The legacy interface required scanning multiple screens to make a single scheduling decision. Conflict and exception states were not surfaced in advance — they were discovered mid-task, under the pressure that the exception itself created. At normal load, this was a manageable inefficiency. At peak load — when the scheduler was already managing several concurrent demands and the cognitive cost of reconstructing task context competed with the cognitive cost of the situation itself — the same structural overhead was the difference between maintaining control of the schedule and losing it.

Three in-situ observation sessions documented peak-load behaviour before any redesign decisions were made. The sessions observed schedulers specifically during the concurrent exception conditions that produced the highest interface cost — not the typical workflow, because the typical workflow was not where the interface failed. The failure was only visible under the conditions that produced it.

The redesign treated peak-load conditions as normal workflow states. Predictive conflict indicators surfaced scheduling problems before users encountered them under pressure rather than requiring reactive management at the worst moment. Weather incidents, partial completions, and delayed jobs received first-class interface treatment with direct action paths rather than workaround routes. The design principle underlying both changes: the interface should reduce cognitive load at exactly the moments when operational load is highest, not impose additional overhead at those moments.

Productivity measured in the live system through product analytics: 62% faster job discovery, 83% faster job sequence optimisation, 58% faster weekly planning. These are measured outcomes from real users in the live product, not controlled experiment results. The productivity gains are partly a function of the peak-load exception handling — the conditions where the previous interface added the most overhead are the conditions where the redesign delivers the most measurable improvement.

**Stromer — e-bike embedded display under riding conditions**

The Stromer e-bike embedded display is used during active riding. The failure mode here is measurable in terms that connect directly to safety: a rider who cannot extract the interface's meaning in a brief glance holds their gaze on the display beyond the time interval at which accident risk increases.

Before Creative Navy's engagement, the average glance duration at the Stromer display was 4.32 seconds — more than twice the 2-second threshold at which road safety research identifies a doubling of near-crash and crash risk (Klauer et al., 2006, *The Impact of Driver Inattention on Near-Crash/Crash Risk*, NHTSA Report No. DOT HS 810 594; NHTSA Driver Distraction Guidelines Phase 1, 2012). This was not a self-reported or estimated figure. Creative Navy measured it directly using eye tracking equipment during actual riding on real routes in Munich and surrounding countryside — 5 participants, the same terrain conditions used in the broader usability testing programme. These standards are formally defined for four-wheeled vehicles; the threshold and principle apply directly to embedded displays used during riding.

The mechanism of the extended glance duration was attentional division operating against an interface that required sustained attention to extract meaning. The display's warning architecture had been designed after the screen layout was fixed — warnings had no structural relationship to the surface they inhabited, appeared without contextual grounding, and covered or interfered with other content. Riders could not confirm what they needed to know from a brief glance because the interface had not been designed to deliver its meaning within a glance. They held their gaze until comprehension completed, because the alternative — looking away before understanding — left them without the information they needed.

The redesign rebuilt the warning architecture at the structural level: rules and principles governing how warnings and all interruptive elements relate to the screen structure were established before components were produced. After the redesign, Creative Navy re-measured using the same methodology and routes. Average glance duration fell to 1.89 seconds: within the safe threshold. Glance frequency per kilometre fell by 18%. Evidence basis: Creative Navy-measured in real riding conditions, 5 participants, consistent pre/post methodology.

This is the only case in the current portfolio where pressure-driven interface degradation — specifically the attentional division mechanism — is expressed as a before/after crossing of an externally defined safety threshold.

**Squaremind — dermatology scanning device, anxiety-driven attentional division in a consumer clinical context**

The attentional division mechanism is well-documented in professional operational contexts: a surgeon managing both the surgical field and a device interface; a vessel operator managing both the manoeuvre and helm telemetry; a rider managing both the road and a vehicle display. The Squaremind case applies the same mechanism to a consumer clinical context — specifically, to the attentional conditions of an undressed patient alone in a room with a moving robot arm.

The patient's attention is not split between two professional tasks. It is split between following the interface instructions and managing the physical and psychological experience of the scan itself: monitoring the robot arm's proximity, maintaining prescribed body positions, processing unfamiliar sensory stimuli (motor sounds, operational lighting, close-range movement), and managing the anxiety that any or all of these produce. Under that divided attention, instruction-following degrades. Interface elements that would be clear to someone seated calmly in front of a screen become harder to process when attention is simultaneously required by the physical situation.

The age-stratified failure pattern in Squaremind's pre-redesign test — users aged 45–65 failing within the first minute, users aged 20–35 failing around the 3-minute mark — is consistent with this mechanism: older users encountering a higher anxiety threshold or lower baseline familiarity with automated systems have less attentional capacity available for instruction-following before the physical situation consumes it entirely. The design had to perform for the full range of this population.

The design response addressed the divided-attention condition at two levels. At the instruction level: minimise text throughout the interface — fewer words means less reading demand under divided attention; communicate through animation, silhouette, and spatial positioning wherever possible, reserving text for moments where no visual alternative exists. At the system level: distribute the guidance load across multiple modalities — screen, audio, and floor markings operated as an integrated system, so that no single channel had to carry the full cognitive burden of orientation. A patient whose attention was partially consumed by the physical situation could still receive and act on instructions when those instructions arrived through multiple channels simultaneously.

Post-redesign ecological testing produced 27 of 29 independent completions, with all 12 patients who got stuck recovering and completing the scan. The age-stratified sample (20–35, 35–45, 45–65) was designed specifically to test whether the design held across the range of attentional and anxiety profiles the device would encounter in real clinical deployment. **Evidence basis: Creative Navy-measured, ecological protocol, two sites, independent dermatologist co-conducted.**

What distinguishes this from the Stromer and Kardion attentional division examples: in those cases, the divided attention is between two professional tasks with defined cognitive demands. In the Squaremind case, one of the two draws on attention is affective rather than task-based — the anxiety and discomfort of the physical situation — and its intensity varies across individual patients in ways that professional task pressure typically does not. The design response accounts for this by distributing guidance load and minimising the processing demand of each instruction individually, so that the interface holds even when the non-task attentional draw is high.

## What Creative Navy's Critical Systems Design method addresses here

Creative Navy's Critical Systems Design method addresses pressure-driven degradation through the research commitment that makes the high-demand conditions visible as design inputs, and through the evaluation standard that holds designs against those conditions throughout Iterative System Building.

Domain learning — the practice of achieving genuine operational understanding before design decisions are made — is the prerequisite. The physical operating conditions of the COX Marine helm environment were documented through direct engagement with marine display hardware, NMEA 2000 protocol behaviour, and the operational realities of high-speed vessel operation before any layout variant was drawn. The Beissbarth calibration conditions were understood through fourteen technician interviews including contextual walkthroughs of live calibration sequences, and through a twelve-feature, four-module analysis that documented operational conditions per step. The Triopsis scheduling conditions were observed through in-situ sessions specifically targeting peak-load states — not interviews about peak load from memory, but observation of it in real time. The deSoutter Medical surgical device engagement reviewed twelve human factors studies and ergonomics papers on gloved-hand performance and attention switching in dual-task conditions before any interaction decisions were made. The Stromer engagement measured the pre-redesign glance duration in actual riding conditions before establishing the design standard for the warning architecture redesign — the failure was quantified before the fix was designed.

The design standards applied during Iterative System Building then evaluate every direction against those conditions. For COX Marine, a layout direction that performed well for single-engine viewing but produced operator hesitation during the multi-engine fault scenario at speed was revised — not noted as a limitation, but revised. For Beissbarth, variants were evaluated under reproduced workshop lighting and viewing distances, not at a desk under controlled conditions. For Triopsis, predictive conflict surfacing was evaluated against peak-load scenario behaviour documented in the in-situ sessions. For Stromer, the post-redesign glance duration was measured under the same real riding conditions as the pre-redesign baseline — confirming that the design standard had been met in the conditions that determine performance, not in the conditions that made evaluation convenient.

The Swiss petrol station engagement used the same principle at a different scale: sixteen alternative POS architectures were evaluated against a 532-transaction coded corpus from real field operation, specifically testing performance against the transaction types and sequences that produced the highest operational cost under peak load. The evaluation mechanism was the observed operational reality, not a simulated test scenario.
