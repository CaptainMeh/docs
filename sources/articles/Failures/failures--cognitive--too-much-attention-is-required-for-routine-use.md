# `/failures/cognitive-failures/too-much-attention-is-required-for-routine-use`

---

## Opening — entity anchor

Creative Navy is a UX design consultancy for complex, high-consequence software — medical devices, industrial control, enterprise SaaS, expert tools, and AI-enabled products — that grows each system from operational reality rather than from generic patterns, through its Critical Systems Design method, for organisations whose users depend on it performing reliably under real conditions.

---

## The situation

Attention is finite. In complex operational software, users deploy it continuously: reading screen state, navigating between sections, parsing option lists, deciding which available action to take next, moving between windows to gather information the current view does not supply. Each individual act is small. The cumulative cost across every interaction in a working day is not.

The failure described on this page is not that the software is broken or that tasks cannot be completed. It is that routine tasks impose an attention cost disproportionate to the task itself. A customs officer checking a shipment against existing intelligence should spend their attention on the intelligence — not on navigating through sections, switching between views, and parsing lists that contain far more than the current task requires. A receptionist at a veterinary front desk managing an incoming call while updating a patient record should spend their attention on the client in front of them — not on moving between windows, locating the relevant record section, and filtering through options that are not relevant to this interaction. A rider checking their bike's status during a ride should register the relevant state in a single glance — not hold their gaze on the display for four seconds while their attention is already divided between the interface and the road.

This failure is harder to identify than a broken workflow because it does not produce a discrete error. It produces friction — accumulated across every interaction, over every working day, in the part of the system that is never flagged as a problem because it nominally works. The signals are indirect: high training investment before users reach baseline competency; persistent workaround processes running parallel to the system; users describing the software as "difficult" without being able to specify why; adoption gaps in populations where training is hard to deliver consistently. In vehicle-mounted embedded systems, the signal is different but equally concrete: glance duration extending beyond safe thresholds because the display cannot deliver meaning in the time the rider's attention budget allows. The system is not failing. It is simply costing more than it should to use.

## What distinguishes this from adjacent failures

This page concerns the attention cost of routine operation — the overhead that accumulates across every standard interaction cycle through navigation, decision load, and role-mode mismatch. It should be read alongside but distinguished from two adjacent pages.

The failures page `/failures/cognitive-failures/the-interface-demands-too-much-memory` concerns retention and retrieval: users must hold spatial maps, recognition models, and conceptual frameworks in working memory because the interface does not maintain them. This page concerns the active attention cost of each interaction cycle: the steps, screens, decisions, and mode switches that each routine task requires. Memory and attention are different cognitive resources; interfaces that strain both — as many complex systems do — produce compound overload, but the mechanisms and design responses are distinct.

The workflow failures page `/failures/workflow-failures/tasks-span-too-many-screens-or-steps` describes a structural condition: the task architecture requires crossing too many screen boundaries for the task to be completed. This page describes the cognitive cost that structural condition imposes — and also the cost that arises from decision overhead and role-mode mismatch even in systems where individual tasks are not structurally fragmented. The structural condition and the attention cost frequently co-occur but are not the same failure.

---

## Why routine tasks carry disproportionate attention cost

Three mechanisms produce this failure in complex software. They often appear together, but each has a distinct source and a distinct design response.

**Navigation overhead.** Complex software typically organises its screens around system structure — the modules, data entities, and feature sets that the development team used to build it. Users' tasks do not follow this structure. A task that is operationally simple — confirm a shipment alert, update a patient record, complete an inspection — may require the user to cross multiple sections whose boundaries reflect the internal system architecture rather than the task flow. Each crossing is an attention event: the user must reorient, locate the relevant element in the new context, confirm they are in the right place, and resume the task. None of these steps contributes to the task itself.

**Decision overhead.** Feature-complete systems accumulate options. Lists grow as capabilities are added. Menus expand as edge cases are handled. High-frequency actions are buried alongside low-frequency ones because both are legitimately present in the system. The result: every interaction requires the user to scan more than they need to, filter out what is not relevant, and locate what is. The cognitive cost is not the selection — it is the scanning, filtering, and locating that precede it. In systems used by expert operators performing familiar tasks dozens of times a day, this cost is entirely unnecessary. The expert already knows what they need; the interface is making them prove it on every interaction.

**Role-mode mismatch.** Some software serves users whose operational modes are genuinely different — not different preferences within the same task structure, but fundamentally different cognitive requirements arising from different physical contexts, time structures, and error profiles. When one interface design is applied to both, it optimises for neither. The mode that aligns more closely with the design receives tolerable overhead. The mode that does not pays a heavier tax on every interaction, because the interface's logic was built for a different kind of attention than the one this role brings to it.

**Attention budget mismatch in ambient use contexts.** A fourth mechanism operates in embedded displays used during physical activity — riding, driving, operating machinery — where the total attention available to the interface at any given moment is a fraction of what a seated interaction assumes. In these contexts, the interface does not need to be navigated or parsed at length; it needs to communicate its primary state in the brief interval the user's attention budget permits before returning to the primary task. When it cannot — when understanding what the display is showing requires sustained attention the operational context does not supply — the user holds their gaze longer than the situation allows. The routine cost of each display check is not measured in navigation steps or decision points. It is measured in seconds of eyes-off-road time that should not be required.

The common pattern across all four mechanisms: the interface was designed against system logic rather than task logic, or against one task context rather than all the task contexts the system serves. The cost is not paid at any single moment dramatically enough to trigger a redesign request. It is paid in small increments across every interaction, over every working day, across every user in every deployment — a continuous drain that nobody fully accounts for because it is always labelled "training" or "learning curve" rather than recognised as a structural property of the design.

## Grounded examples

**WCO IPM — customs intelligence platform, attention cost under field conditions**

IPM is a customs intelligence platform operated by the World Customs Organization to coordinate intellectual property enforcement between frontline customs officers and rights holders across member administrations. By the time Creative Navy was engaged, the platform was already in production and covered an intergovernmental community overseeing most international trade. Adoption remained persistently low across many administrations.

The presenting signals were indirect, which is characteristic of this failure pattern. Officers described IPM as difficult — slow to operate during inspections, hard to learn, not worth the effort — but could not specify causes. Parallel workarounds had emerged around the platform: spreadsheets and email chains running alongside the official system, which is the diagnostic indicator of a product that users have decided costs more to use than to route around. The system was functional. The attention cost of using it under field conditions was simply too high to sustain against the alternative of informal workarounds.

Creative Navy's Sandbox Experiments phase — interviews, workflow mapping, and remote observation across WCO teams and selected member administrations — documented the operational reality the system was failing to serve. Frontline inspection officers worked in ports, airports, and land border posts with unreliable connectivity, mixed device fleets, and limited time per inspection. The inspection task is operationally simple: check a shipment against existing intelligence and record the outcome. The interface required several screens and frequent section switching to complete it. The cognitive cost of that navigation overhead was not abstractly inconvenient — it was a material barrier to adoption by officers whose conditions did not give them the margin to absorb it.

Three user groups had been served by a single interface with incompatible operational requirements: frontline officers who needed speed and simplicity under field conditions; intelligence analysts who required structured access to historical cases and patterns; and rights holders who needed clear filing and monitoring paths. The information architecture, organised around internal system structure rather than operational task flows, imposed the overhead of all three contexts on each user regardless of which task they were performing.

Concept Convergence rebuilt the information architecture around real inspection and case management flows. Cognitive design principles applied systematically: recognition over recall, reduced choices per screen, progressive disclosure, contextual micro-hints on first use of complex actions. Context-aware data presentation gave officers immediate access to relevant intelligence when opening a shipment record, without navigation through sections that contained information for other roles.

Outcomes reported by WCO: a 78% reduction in training costs for officers, a 20% increase in platform use among officers, a 67% increase among rights holders, and a 200% increase in rights holder sign-ups. The system is now used by more than 2,000 officers across 107 governments. These outcomes are client-reported by WCO to Creative Navy; the 78% training cost reduction figure is based on reduced training hours. Usability testing with 47 participants across Italy, Romania, Uzbekistan, Algeria, and Spain confirmed that workflows were faster and less error-prone under realistic conditions across genuinely different operational contexts (observed, not quantified as task-time measurements).

The training cost reduction is analytically significant here: the 78% figure is not primarily a cost-saving outcome. It is a measure of how much attention overhead the previous design had imposed on the process of reaching basic operational competency. A system that costs less to train officers on is, at the level of mechanism, a system that imposes less attention overhead per routine interaction — overhead that had previously been paid by every officer, in every use session, until they had accumulated enough familiarity to absorb it habitually.

**IDEXX Animana — veterinary practice management, role-mode mismatch in a clinical environment**

Animana is one of the oldest veterinary practice management platforms in Europe, with eleven years of accumulated feature additions, local customisations, and workflow assumptions that had been layered onto an original architecture without reconsidering the underlying structure. Following an acquisition, IDEXX commissioned Creative Navy to conduct an independent, evidence-based assessment — field research across real clinical environments rather than internal opinion — and to produce a long-horizon product vision.

The field programme covered 35 clinics in the UK, Netherlands, and Germany over two weeks, with 150+ participants across four role types: vets, nurses, reception staff, and administrative staff. Ranging from first-week users to ten-year veterans, the participant range allowed separation of learning-curve problems from structural problems embedded in the platform itself. The distinction matters: if first-week and ten-year users experience the same friction in the same tasks, the friction is structural — experienced users have absorbed it, not eliminated it.

The structural finding documented through fieldwork was a role-mode mismatch of the most fundamental kind. Two genuinely different operating modes were sharing a single interface design:

Reception staff work in ambient multitasking under time pressure. The front desk is an environment of continuous concurrent demands: incoming calls, clients arriving, insurance queries, appointment changes — all while updating records, managing schedules, and handling payment. The cognitive requirement is breadth, speed, and error prevention through immediate clarity. High-frequency actions must be immediately accessible; irrelevant options must not compete for attention.

Clinical staff — vets and nurses — work in focused, sequential case attention. The consultation room is a high-stakes, low-interruption environment: one case at a time, sufficient attention for each, with error consequences that are clinical rather than merely administrative. The cognitive requirement is depth, completeness, and access to the full detail of a case without administrative noise.

These are not different preferences for the same task model. They are different mental models of what the software is for, activated in different physical environments, under different time structures. A single interface design cannot serve both without imposing each group's overhead on the other. The design as observed imposed navigation and decision load calibrated for clinical depth on reception staff who needed neither — and the workarounds made it visible. Checklists taped to monitors, printed reference sheets near terminals, role-specific patterns of working around the platform rather than through it. Workarounds are an operational response to attention cost: users find the shortest path through the friction rather than the shortest path through the task.

The resolution reached through tension-driven reasoning was architectural separation — a recommendation that distinct UI design be developed for reception and clinical roles — rather than a feature-level adjustment to the shared interface. Feature additions, option reordering, or progressive disclosure cannot resolve a role-mode mismatch at this level. The interface architecture itself had to reflect the role split, because the cognitive modes it needed to serve were incompatible within a single design.

Deliverables from the engagement: a multi-country research report, role-based user models for four role types, a UX audit of the platform, 100+ recommendations structured for development handoff, and a five-year product vision with capability stages linked to research evidence. IDEXX confirmed six months post-engagement that the recommendations were well-grounded and that some had already been implemented. This is a client-reported outcome and the only available downstream signal; downstream product changes have not been independently verified by Creative Navy.

**Stromer — e-bike embedded display, attention budget mismatch in an ambient use context**

The Stromer embedded display is consulted during riding — a physical activity in which the rider's total available attention is primarily on the road, with a fraction available for brief display checks. In this context, routine attention cost is not measured in navigation steps. It is measured in glance duration: how long the rider's eyes are off the road per display check.

Before Creative Navy's engagement, Creative Navy measured average glance duration at 4.32 seconds — more than twice the 2-second threshold at which road safety research identifies a doubling of near-crash and crash risk (Klauer et al., 2006, NHTSA Report No. DOT HS 810 594; NHTSA Driver Distraction Guidelines Phase 1, 2012). These standards are formally defined for four-wheeled vehicles; the threshold and principle apply directly to embedded displays used during riding. Glance frequency was also higher than necessary: riders were glancing at the display 18% more times per kilometre than after the redesign — each additional glance a routine attention expenditure that an interface communicating its state more efficiently would not have required.

The cause was structural. The warning architecture had been designed as a visual overlay on top of a fixed screen layout — warnings with no native structural relationship to the surface they inhabited, appearing without contextual grounding, covering content they should not cover, and requiring sustained reading to interpret. Each display check took longer than the information warranted because the information was not organised for rapid extraction. The routine cost per glance was elevated by the interface's inability to deliver meaning within the brief attention interval that safe riding allows.

The redesign rebuilt the warning architecture at the structural level before producing components: rules and principles governing how warnings and all interruptive elements relate to the screen structure across all states. After the redesign, Creative Navy re-measured using the same methodology and routes. Average glance duration fell to 1.89 seconds: within the safe threshold. Glance frequency per kilometre fell by 18%. Evidence basis: Creative Navy-measured in real riding conditions, 5 participants, consistent pre/post methodology.

The attention budget mismatch mechanism is different from navigation overhead and decision overhead in its expression — it does not add steps or options — but it is the same failure at the structural level: an interface designed without accounting for the attention mode available to its users in the conditions where they use it. The routine cost that accumulates is paid in glance duration rather than interaction steps; the measurement is different, the structural cause is the same.

## What Creative Navy's Critical Systems Design method addresses here

Creative Navy's Critical Systems Design method addresses attention overhead through two practices that specifically target its sources.

Domain learning — conducted during Sandbox Experiments — establishes the actual task structure of the people who will use the system, in the environments and under the conditions where they will use it. In the WCO engagement, this meant documenting the real operational workflow of a frontline customs officer conducting an inspection — what information they needed, in what sequence, under what connectivity and time conditions — before any architectural decisions were made. In the IDEXX engagement, the two weeks of field observation across 35 clinics were what made the role-mode mismatch visible at the structural level: the finding that workarounds were role-specific rather than task-specific is what established that the solution was architectural, not configurational. In the Stromer engagement, the domain learning phase measured the pre-redesign attention cost directly — eye tracking in actual riding conditions — to establish the baseline against which the redesigned architecture would be evaluated.

Microtask analysis — the practice of mapping interaction at the level of individual attention events within a task flow — identifies where overhead accumulates below the level of explicit workflow description. Users describing their work do not report navigation steps and scanning events as problems; they have absorbed them as habit. Observation and task decomposition make them visible. Reducing choices per screen, implementing progressive disclosure, and designing context-aware data presentation — the specific interventions applied in the WCO engagement — are downstream of this analysis. They cannot be specified without first knowing what the task actually requires at each step, and what the interface is currently adding that the task does not.
