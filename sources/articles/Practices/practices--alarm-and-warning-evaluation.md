# `/practices/alarm-and-warning-evaluation`

---

## What the practice is

This page describes the evaluation activity. The discipline being evaluated — the alarm lifecycle, rationalisation, priority assignment and the standards that codify them — is described on the alarm-management page.

Alarm-and-warning evaluation examines the full set of alerts, warnings, and alarm states in a system and assesses each against the criteria that determine whether it will function correctly under operating conditions. Those criteria are not the same as the criteria that determine whether an alarm appears on screen. An alarm can be technically present — visible, accurately triggered — and still fail operationally because it does not communicate what to do, cannot be distinguished from lower-priority alerts, or relies on a communication channel that fails under the conditions the device is used in.

The evaluation is conducted against operating conditions, not controlled viewing conditions. The questions are: can this alarm be perceived in the environment where the device operates? Can it be interpreted quickly enough to act on it? Does it direct attention to the right place? Does it communicate both the problem and the next step?

---

## What the evaluation examines

**Priority hierarchy audit** — does the system have a defined priority structure for alarms, and is it visually expressed? Can a user looking at an active alarm state determine immediately whether it requires immediate intervention or whether it is informational? When multiple alarms are active simultaneously, does the highest-priority condition receive the most prominent treatment?

**Actionability assessment** — for each warning or alarm: does it communicate what to do, not just that something is wrong? An alarm that communicates a condition without specifying a next action is not actionable in time-pressured environments. The evaluation distinguishes alarms that close the action loop (condition + next step) from those that open it (condition only).

**Communication channel analysis** — what channels does each alarm use (colour, sound, icon, position, text, vibration)? Is any alarm dependent on a single channel that may fail under operating conditions? Variable lighting renders colour-only indicators unreliable. Noise renders sound-only alarms inaudible. Vibration makes text unreadable. The evaluation identifies where single-channel dependency exists.

**False positive and nuisance alarm rate** — how frequently do alarms trigger under conditions that don't require action? Nuisance alarms are the primary driver of alert fatigue: users learn to dismiss alerts habitually, and that dismissal extends to genuine alerts. The evaluation estimates false positive exposure and identifies the alarm types most likely to train dismissal behaviour.

**Mute behaviour review** — what happens when a user silences an alarm? Does it disappear entirely, or remain visually present at reduced prominence? An alarm that disappears when muted creates a pathway to a state where an active alarm is invisible. The evaluation identifies whether muting produces genuine silence or dangerous invisibility.

**Operating condition testing** — each significant alarm evaluated under the conditions of actual use: the lighting environment, the noise level, the attentional load the user is carrying, the physical position from which they interact with the device. In embedded systems, the evaluation must be conducted in or approximated to the real environment.

**Regulatory alignment check** — in regulated medical device contexts, the evaluation includes assessment against IEC 60601-1-8, the collateral standard for alarm systems in medical electrical equipment: priority tiering requirements, the prohibition on colour as the sole differentiating attribute between priority levels, mute behaviour requirements, and alarm state visibility requirements. IEC 62366-1 governs the usability-engineering process through which the use-related risks behind those requirements are identified and evaluated; it does not itself specify what an alarm must look or sound like.

---

## When it is used

During Sandbox Experiments when reviewing an existing system — the alarm-and-warning evaluation is part of the diagnostic evidence base established before design work begins.

As a component of complex product audits — alarm architecture failures are a recurring finding in audits of high-consequence systems, and the evaluation provides structured evidence for specific recommendations.

In regulated medical device contexts, the evaluation runs throughout Iterative System Building as part of the IEC 62366-1 formative evaluation process, assessing proposed alarm designs against the IEC 60601-1-8 alarm-system requirements before they are finalised.

The practice typically follows state-and-transition review (which identifies all system states including alarm states) and precedes the redesign work covered under the warning-and-alarm-clarity-improvement capability.

## Evidence from engagements

**deSoutter Medical / Zethon** — Competitive benchmarking as the primary evaluation method: **6 comparable surgical devices evaluated**; alarm and state communication assessed for each under operating theatre conditions (variable lighting, gloved hands, divided attention). The finding: **most of the evaluated devices** used colour as the primary — and often sole — indicator of device state and warning conditions. Under variable theatre lighting, colour-only communication is unreliable; the evaluation identified this as the primary failure mode across the competitive set. The evaluation produced the requirement that addressed it: redundant non-colour cues (spatial position + icon form + colour) for every critical state, so that the failure of any single channel under operating conditions does not remove the signal. The evaluation also assessed activation state communication — the transition from ready to active — as the highest-risk alarm category in surgical instruments.

**Kardion MCS Controller** — The IEC 62366-1 formative evaluation process structured the alarm-and-warning evaluation formally. For the cardiac support controller, the evaluation examined: whether priority tiering was expressed visually with sufficient differentiation between levels (required by IEC 60601-1-8); whether any alarm state relied solely on colour for differentiation (prohibited by IEC 60601-1-8); what happened visually when an alarm was muted (IEC 60601-1-8 requires continued visual presence); and whether the absence of alarm was as confirmable as its presence. Each of these was a formal evaluation criterion tied to identified use-related hazards. The evaluation produced specific alarm architecture requirements — not design recommendations but documented design requirements traceable to identified hazards — that shaped the alarm layer of the final design. **The design passed FDA evaluation as submitted, with no design changes required.**

**Cox Marine cluster displays** — The evaluation was conducted through scenario testing during Concept Convergence: a multi-engine fault scenario was simulated against proposed layout candidates to assess alarm communication under realistic fault conditions. The test revealed a specific failure: several candidate layouts surfaced fault presence at the tile level but did not direct attention to the priority engine. A user scanning multiple tiles for the source of a fault under time pressure was experiencing an alarm communication failure even though the alarm was technically visible. The evaluation produced the requirement for a two-level alarm architecture: a fault-summary area surfacing the highest-priority condition across all tiles, and per-tile alarm highlighting directing attention to the source.

**Elsner Elektronik / Cala Touch KNX** — The evaluation assessed alarm and notification architecture for a consumer product used by non-technical users across 54 countries. The primary evaluation question was different from high-consequence contexts: not "can this alarm be acted on in time?" but "can this alarm be interpreted correctly by someone with no technical background?" The evaluation identified the alert fatigue risk: sensor-range notifications occurring regularly in normal operation could, if given the same visual and auditory treatment as critical failure alerts, train users to dismiss all alerts including critical ones. The evaluation produced the requirement for two distinct alert levels: routine notifications (informational, low urgency, for the systems engineer) and critical notifications (requiring immediate action, distinct visual and auditory treatment). The calibration of each level's content for a non-technical audience was a specific evaluation output.

**Gexcon CFD simulation** — The evaluation addressed a specific alarm design challenge: how to warn users about configuration errors in an expert simulation tool where the errors produce valid-looking outputs and the consequences are deferred. The evaluation examined the existing warning architecture: were incomplete inputs flagged during scenario setup, or only after the simulation ran? Were contradictory parameter combinations identified before run, or discovered in outputs? For each warning point, the evaluation assessed whether the warning was actionable (did it specify what was wrong and what to do?) or merely informational (did it indicate that something was wrong without specifying what?). The evaluation produced requirements for three-point warning architecture: setup-stage warnings, pre-run contradictory-input warnings, and post-detection error specification.

**The industrial engagement (a European industrial automation vendor whose process control software coordinates machining cells)** — The expert review of the running system produced alarm-specific audit findings. **These are what the review found in that system, never a general claim about industrial software.** In the alarms table, an error row and a selected row were both solid red simultaneously, and one row duplicated another verbatim (E21). In the alarm list, severity was conveyed by a leading icon alone — no text, no row tint (E32). Alarm detail carried a real taxonomy — workpiece, pallet, timestamp, severity, numeric fault code, source classification, failed operation — poorly surfaced rather than absent (E33). **Designed responses describe what was designed; never imply they produced a result:** an operator instructions layer distinguishing notification from instruction, with informational items *acknowledged* and actionable items *completed*, grouped by day, with inline entity links, filtering by type, machine, timeline and location, and a batching panel grouping related interventions in one storage unit into a single instruction so the operator makes one trip (E42); and a pre-warning state specified for tool lifetime below a defined threshold (E48). **The product is in development, not shipped; nothing in this engagement is an outcome.**

**Streetec kntrl 2** — Regulation sets the alarm conditions: the vehicle's suspension certification specifies a permitted pressure range per axle — the client's worked example being roughly 3.4–4.0 bar front and 4.5–5.2 bar rear for one common hatchback — and operating outside that range must warn the driver; some regulations permit operation outside the range below 50 km/h (S25). The designed alert scheme is a two-level escalation: a first attempt at a blocked action produces an inline notification, a second attempt produces a modal. Colour stays neutral until a genuine alert state — raising and lowering pressure are both normal operations and neither is colour-coded as a warning (S43). **These are designed responses; no safety result was measured — regulatory constraints shaped the Streetec design, and no safety outcome may be claimed.**

**The MDU (ship motion monitoring; the manufacturer is not named)** — The maritime standards regime frames this material without covering it: IMO Bridge Alert Management (resolution MSC.302(87), implemented through IEC 62923-1 and IEC 62923-2) defines four alert priorities — emergency alarm, alarm, warning, caution — and has been mandatory for new navigation and communication installations on SOLAS ships since 30 August 2021. **The MDU is not SOLAS navigation equipment and falls under none of it. That is the framing, never a conformance claim: the acknowledge convention had to be learned from a practitioner precisely because the standard codifying that behaviour for the equipment beside it on the same bridge does not reach a system that only advises.** What the evaluation of the alarm scheme settled: the alarm does not bind to the live instantaneous value — which leaves the alarm band seconds after entering it — but to the rolling maximum and minimum, so an alarm means the vessel was outside its threshold at some point within the last twenty minutes (M41): *"That means that sometime within the last 20 minutes you are outside the alarm level."* Acknowledge replaced Clear: Creative Navy argued in review that a Clear control had no function, because nothing done on the screen changes the state of the ship, and the external advisor supplied the bridge convention in its place — the alarm flashes red, *Acknowledge* means "I have seen it", the indication holds solid red, and it clears itself when the condition is no longer present (M42): *"When you click acknowledge, it's turned solid red and it stays like that until it's cleared."* Self-clearing does not lose the record: an alarm that clears itself before anyone acknowledges it is retained in the log behind a count badge, where an acknowledge-all is available (M44): *"if there's 200 alarms, then you just go acknowledge all and then move on with your day."* Alarm ordering is chronological, with an explicit forward provision: parametric roll detection becomes the highest-priority alarm once implemented, and an alarm-versus-warning distinction will be needed at that point — **neither was built** (M47). Timestamps were deliberately kept out of the alarm pop-up and placed in the log instead: with self-clearing bound to the 20-minute window, the most recent alarm can never be more than twenty minutes old, so a timestamp at the point of reading adds nothing (M48). **These describe what was designed; no fuel, emissions, cargo-loss, alarm-response, fleet or commercial figure may be attributed to the design, and no safety outcome or safety-critical credential may be claimed — the manufacturer states plainly that the system is deliberately non-actuating and its alarms are comfort, cargo and crew-safety alarms rather than immediate-danger alarms.**

**Oceaneering (hospital dispatch panel; nothing beyond the client name may be named)** — The evaluation material is the separation of two "act now" states, the reservation of red, and the specification of non-colour channels. In the predecessor, one blink carried two opposite demands: blinking signalled both "a cart has been placed, start the dispatch" and "a cart has arrived, pick it up" — the sales consultant's diagnosis, rendered as reported speech, was that an operator reads a blinking control as the system having something for them, presses it and does whatever it offers, while the fleet backs up behind carts nobody has removed; her request was to replace the blink with text or a pictogram that names the action (O33). The state model that resolved it carries a stated logic: one colour for sending, one for receiving, each flashing when an action is required and steady when it is not, with red reserved exclusively for errors — because red appearing in normal operation teaches operators to ignore it. Seven station statuses were specified, each carrying colour plus a directional icon plus the role letter, with a legend reachable from the panel (O62). **The flashing/steady convention partly existed already at one site, for sends only; the redesign generalised it across sends and receives, gave it a stated rule, and added the non-colour channels. It is an adopted and systematised convention, not a Creative Navy invention — say so explicitly** (O63). Non-colour redundancy was raised by the client, not the designers, on grounds of colour vision deficiency, and the obvious remedy — tap the card to read its status — was rejected by the client as unworkable at operational frequency; the resolution was colour and icon simultaneously, with letters retained (O64). Flashing discipline: one thing flashing at a time — in a twelve-station area, twelve flashing stations convey no priority; supported by the field, which expects one cart awaiting dispatch at a time, occasionally two (O75). **Every Oceaneering passage is reported speech attributed to a role, never quoted. The validation of the state model was a convergence of expert judgement from the operations side — two operations leads at two different hospitals, consulted separately, independently asked for whole-card colour over corner indicators and independently endorsed the flashing-versus-steady model (O16) — not a measured result, not validation of the design as a whole, and not evidence the interface performs better than its predecessor. ISA-18.2 and IEC 62682 frame the annunciation argument; no rationalisation process was run, and no alarm-management credential may be claimed.**

**Raymarine (next-generation fixed-mount VHF; the new radio's name is never used)** — The evaluation output is a priority-and-arbitration scheme. Four event priority levels are assigned by consequence rather than arrival order: emergency — outgoing distress, received distress and local man overboard — takes a persistent incident surface that no ordinary action dismisses; high concern is prominent and actionable with its source visible and never silently alters a radio control; operational is noticeable and proportionate and does not silently change the selected vessel or the tuned channel; background updates quietly and never takes keypad focus (R125). A five-step arbitration sequence runs between an event arriving and being shown: classify by type and consequence; coalesce exact duplicates so repeat packets refresh an existing record rather than stacking dialogs; arbitrate against the active incident, the radio state and the current focus owner; present on the appropriate surface with its permitted actions and sound; and restore the prior task only after a validity check (R126). An interruption stores a structured snapshot and restores it conditionally — origin screen, focused item identity, list position, plot scale, uncommitted draft values, actual transaction status and live channel — each validated on return; **no unsent action is ever replayed**, and a restored call draft requires a new deliberate send (R127). Three emergency types are modelled separately with independent identities, timelines and histories: a locally initiated man-overboard incident records a reference position, source and time, runs an elapsed clock — **and transmits nothing**; an outgoing distress alert is a protected radio action with its own sending, sent, awaiting-acknowledgement, acknowledged and failed states; a received distress alert is information from another station — viewing or silencing it transmits no acknowledgement, and silencing a local alarm never ends an incident (R128). **The fuller treatment of this scheme belongs to the warning-and-alarm-clarity-improvement capability page; this page cross-references it rather than repeating it, and the bridge-alert-management convergence observation involving the MDU is written on the alarm-management page, not here.** **The radio was built and released; it is not "shipped as designed" — no fidelity comparison is recorded — and no safety, rescue, distress-response or collision outcome, and no type-approval, certification, conformity or regulatory credential, may be attached to this scheme.**

**Schone Airspace Group (uncrewed aircraft Mission Control)** — Two alarm-scheme decisions. Owner-scoped alerts: every alert names its owning aircraft or service in its first visible line, and keeps that owner in the fleet card, the inspector, the live notice and the history — **the accepted cost: owner information is repeated in compact alerts, trading text density for precision** (D93). And the separation of acknowledgement from clearing: acknowledging a notice is an attention state, not a clearing event — acknowledged-and-active remains an explicit combined state, only a source clearance event moves the condition to cleared, and a critical banner never takes on a success tone because the operator has seen it (D94). **These describe what was designed. The product was built and is in test, not deployed; no flight-safety, airworthiness, certification or regulatory credential, and no operational figure, may be attached.**

## Relationship to other practices

Alarm-and-warning evaluation typically follows:
- `/practices/state-and-transition-review` — which identifies all system states including alarm states and establishes the overall state architecture; alarm-and-warning evaluation goes deeper on alarm-specific properties

Alarm-and-warning evaluation directly informs:
- `/capabilities/warning-and-alarm-clarity-improvement` — the evaluation provides the diagnostic evidence; the capability delivers the redesign
- `/practices/error-likely-interaction-review` — alarm failures are a specific category of error-likely interaction; the two reviews often run in parallel

In regulated contexts, alarm-and-warning evaluation aligns with:
- IEC 62366-1 use-related hazard identification for alarm conditions
- IEC 62682 and ISA-18.2 alarm management standards (where applicable)
