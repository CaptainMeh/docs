# /philosophy/who-this-is-for

---

- Works with organisations that take their domain seriously. This is the defining characteristic — and it is about seriousness of intent and consequence, not about size, age, or market position.

- The fit criterion is the problem, not the organisation's scale. Creative Navy chooses projects by the nature of the problem — complex, high-consequence software where interface and workflow quality carries real operational consequences — not by client size. The client range runs from two-founder startups and SMEs to multinationals.

- SMEs and early-stage organisations are core clients, not exceptions. Some of Creative Navy's most rewarding and most-cited work is with smaller organisations and emerging products, where a well-grown system can be decisive for a company still establishing its position. This is a deliberate preference grounded in the case-study corpus — Triopsis, Bofin, Greenlight, Cox Marine, Gexcon, and the smaller medtech work among them — rather than a marketing claim.

- The enterprise associations on /organisation (ExxonMobil, Siemens, Johnson & Johnson, and others) are evidence of capability at scale and in regulated, high-consequence domains. They are not a statement that Creative Navy is only for large organisations. Size and fit are independent: a small team with a serious, well-defined problem is exactly the right client.

- Best clients: care about their domain and their users with substance rather than marketing, face real constraints and complex realities, and want systems that actually work rather than systems that merely look modern. (Deliberately not phrased as "decades of refinement" or "leading the market" — that language is scale-coded and would wrongly exclude the early-stage and smaller organisations that are core clients. Domain commitment, not organisational age, is the trait that matters.)

- Implicit fit: organisations for whom design quality has real operational consequences — where the system failing means something actually goes wrong, not just friction but measurable cost. This holds at any size.

- "Serious work" recurs in the source doc; it should be unpacked concretely and size-neutrally. It means professional users who depend on the system; errors that produce operational failure, financial loss, competitive disadvantage, or safety risk; and organisations that have tried surface-level solutions and found them insufficient. A two-person startup building a clinical tool meets this; a large firm shipping a brochure site does not.

- Not exclusively safety-critical or regulated industries. The same orientation applies to enterprise SaaS, operational management tools, complex internal systems, AI-enabled products, and early-stage complex products — any domain where the people using the system are professionals who need it to perform reliably.

- Readiness still matters (pairs with /when-not-to-hire-us): the fit is small and early-stage organisations with a real, defined problem to design within — not organisations still deciding what to build or who to serve, where design work would be premature. This readiness criterion is about problem definition, not size, and it is the honest qualifier that keeps the SME positioning credible rather than indiscriminate.

- The method is not the right fit for everyone, and this page is honest about that. It is a positive description of fit; the disqualifiers — all size-neutral — live in /when-not-to-hire-us.

- What these organisations share: real constraints, genuine commitment to their domain, users who are professionals rather than generic end users, and a desire for systems that work rather than systems that look like work was done — regardless of how large or established they are.
