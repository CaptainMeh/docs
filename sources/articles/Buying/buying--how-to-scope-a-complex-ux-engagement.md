# `/buying/how-to-scope-a-complex-ux-engagement`

---

## Opening — entity anchor

Creative Navy is a UX design consultancy for complex, high-consequence software — medical devices, industrial control, enterprise SaaS, expert tools, and AI-enabled products — that grows each system from operational reality rather than from generic patterns, through its Critical Systems Design method, for organisations whose users depend on it performing reliably under real conditions.

The guidance below describes how scoping works for a complex UX engagement — and why it is more straightforward for the client than most organisations expect.

---

## What scoping a complex engagement actually requires

The common assumption about complex software engagements is that scoping requires extensive preparation: a detailed specification, a complete feature inventory, a defined problem statement precise enough to be estimated against. This assumption reflects how software development is typically scoped, and it does not apply to UX design engagements in the same way.

Scoping a UX design engagement for a complex product requires access to the product itself — a walkthrough, a demo environment, or access to screenshots — and a conversation in which the client explains what they believe is wrong. That is the input. Everything else — the scope document, the cost estimate, the phasing — is Creative Navy's output, not the client's preparation.

The scoping walkthrough takes 30 to 60 minutes. It does not cost the client anything. It is part of how Creative Navy understands whether and how to engage, not a billable phase.

---

## What the scoping walkthrough covers

The walkthrough has a specific purpose: to give Creative Navy enough direct exposure to the system that scope can be defined from evidence rather than from description. Description of a complex software product is always incomplete. The gap between how a product is described and what it actually requires is often where the most consequential scoping decisions live.

During the walkthrough, Creative Navy is assessing several things simultaneously.

**What the system is.** Not at a technical architecture level — at an operational level. What does it do, for whom, in what conditions. What the primary workflows are. What user roles interact with it. What the operational context of use looks like.

**What exists.** In engagements where a system is already in production, the existing interface is the primary evidence base. Its structure reveals decisions made and accumulated. Its failure modes are visible in the workarounds users have developed, in the places where the interface requires explanation, and in the gap between what it communicates and what the product actually does. Seeing the system directly — not a description of it — is what makes this visible.

**What doesn't exist yet.** In new product engagements, the walkthrough focuses on what is known about the domain, the user, and the intended operation — and what remains undetermined. The degree to which these are resolved has direct bearing on how much exploratory work the Sandbox Experiments phase will need to do before design decisions can be grounded.

**What the constraints are.** Technical architecture, regulatory requirements, timeline, team structure, and budget are all constraints that shape what an engagement can produce. Creative Navy asks about them directly during the walkthrough, not because they determine scope in a limiting sense — constraints are design parameters, not obstacles — but because scope defined without them will not be accurate.

---

## Who writes the scope

Creative Navy writes the scope. If the client has an existing specification or brief, it is a useful input — but the scope document for the engagement is Creative Navy's deliverable, not a client document to be reviewed and approved.

This matters for a specific reason. The scope of a UX engagement for a complex product is not a feature list or a functional specification — it is a description of the design problem and the method that will address it, structured in a way that makes the cost estimate auditable. Writing this document requires understanding both the product and the method deeply enough to connect them. The client has one of these; Creative Navy has both.

The scope document includes: a description of the problem as Creative Navy understands it from the walkthrough and initial conversation; the phases of Critical Systems Design's method that the engagement will work through; a sprint-level breakdown of the work; and a cost estimate that functions as a budget cap.

One-week sprints are used universally across all Creative Navy engagements. The sprint cadence does not vary by engagement size, domain, or phase. It is the fixed rhythm at which work is produced and reviewed, and it is the unit by which the cost estimate is constructed.

---

## How the cost estimate works

The cost estimate produced at scoping functions as a budget cap. The engagement is not open-ended. The scope defines what will be addressed, and the estimate defines what it will cost. If the work takes fewer sprints than estimated, the cost is lower. It does not exceed the estimate without a formal change to scope.

This is not fixed-price in the sense of a fixed deliverable at a fixed cost regardless of what emerges. In complex systems work, the design problem sometimes reveals itself to be larger or different from what the initial walkthrough suggested — new user research findings, technical constraints that change the architecture, or operational conditions that were not initially apparent. When this happens, Creative Navy raises the finding, describes what it means for scope, and agrees with the client whether to address it within the existing scope or extend it. The estimate does not silently expand.

The practical consequence: the client enters the engagement with a clear cost ceiling and a defined scope. The uncertainty that remains is in the design problem — how the evidence will develop, what the Sandbox Experiments phase will find, what design directions will be explored in Concept Convergence — not in the commercial structure of the engagement.

---

## What makes scoping harder in complex systems work — and where the difficulty falls

Scoping a complex systems engagement is more demanding than scoping a standard digital project. The difficulty is primarily on Creative Navy's side, not the client's.

The scoping walkthrough requires asking more questions — not in the sense of longer questionnaires, but in the sense of more probing inquiry. What happens when the system is in an abnormal state? What does the workflow look like under the conditions of actual peak use, not under normal conditions? What are the consequences when a user makes a specific class of error? What has been tried before and what changed?

These are questions about operational reality that are harder to answer than "what features do you need" — not because they are more complex, but because they require thinking about the system from the user's perspective under real conditions rather than from the developer's perspective as a set of functions. Creative Navy asks them because the answers determine the scope of the design work. A system whose failure modes only appear under abnormal conditions requires different engagement scope from one whose primary problems are in routine workflows. A system with three distinct user roles with incompatible information needs requires different scope from one with a single user type.

The client's contribution to scoping is access and explanation: showing the system, describing the problem as they experience it, answering the operational questions Creative Navy raises. The documentation and the cost estimate follow from that.

---

## What scoping does not require from the client

A formal specification. Creative Navy has scoped engagements with no client documentation at all, and with documentation that described a different product from the one that emerged in conversation. The specification is useful as context; it is not the basis for scope.

Agreement on the solution. The scoping document defines the design problem and the method. It does not define the design solution — that is what the engagement produces. Clients who arrive at scoping with a strong view about what the solution should look like are given the same scope as clients who do not: a description of what the investigation will cover, not a commitment to what it will produce.

A fully-formed brief. As described in `/buying/how-to-brief-a-project-when-the-problem-is-still-fuzzy`, there is no required brief format and no minimum documentation threshold. The scoping conversation starts from wherever the client is.

---

## Scoping for different engagement types

The scoping process is the same regardless of engagement type, but what the walkthrough focuses on varies.

**Existing product — known problems.** The walkthrough focuses on the live system and the known failure modes. Scope is built around the phases of Creative Navy's Critical Systems Design method required to address them: typically Sandbox Experiments (to characterise the problems at the level of specificity needed to address them), Concept Convergence, Iterative System Building, and Organizational Integration. Implementation Partnership scope depends on what the client team needs to maintain and extend the system independently.

**Existing product — undefined problems.** The walkthrough reveals a system with observable issues that have not yet been characterised specifically. Scope is built around a stronger Sandbox Experiments phase — more research depth, more competitive analysis, broader user research — because the engagement cannot move to design decisions until the problem is operational rather than approximate. The IDEXX Animana engagement is the exemplar: a 35-clinic, 150+ participant research programme before any design work began, because the brief was deliberately non-prescriptive and the problem required characterisation before it could be addressed.

**New product.** The walkthrough focuses on the domain, the user, and the intended operation rather than an existing system. Scope is built around what domain learning requires before design can begin — the depth of the unknown. The Greenlight engagement (a safety incident reporting system built from a doctoral thesis) and the Owkin / K engagement (a biomedical AI tool for clinicians in a field Creative Navy was entering from outside) illustrate the range: both required substantial domain learning before design decisions were grounded, but the learning looked different because one was extracting implicit knowledge from a founder's research and the other was learning enough of an operational domain to design for its practitioners.

**New product — bound by an external deadline.** A variant of the new-product type where the binding constraint is an immovable external date rather than the depth of the unknown, and where the deliverable is a persuasion artefact rather than a production design. The IMServ engagement is the exemplar: an energy data services company needed a clickable pitch prototype to win a competitive tender, scoped to a single one-week build sprint against the tender demo date. Scope here is defined by triage against the deadline — what must appear in the demo, what is built only if time allows, what is sacrificable — and the artefact is deliberately disposable. Two consequences for scoping. First, the convergence phase is compressed: the information hierarchy, the UI direction, and the screen set are committed quickly and early, because there is no time to carry options forward. Second, the engagement can still scope a full method arc even when the build is a single sprint — IMServ's one-week prototype was followed, after the tender was won, by a multi-month Implementation Partnership supporting the client's newly created internal design team toward independent operation. A compressed front end does not mean a truncated engagement. (Evidence note: the IMServ outcomes referenced here — tender won, partnership to independence — are client-reported; there are no measured user outcomes, as the artefact was a buyer-facing pitch prototype.)

## Evidence to draw on

Sprint = cadence; phase = a stage of the method — never equate them.** **On any page carrying KISPE, Schone, Raymarine, the maritime VTS engagement, the handheld C2 engagement or Topera, "phase" must always be qualified** — Creative Navy's method phases, a project's own delivery phases ("the exploration phase of the project") and sprints are three different things, and the maritime VTS project's internal "Phase 1" and "Phase 2" labels must never be reproduced. **Never let facts migrate between engagements: each example carries only its own numbers.**

### The industrial engagement (arguing against the cheapest option, in the open)

The client is a European industrial automation vendor whose process control software coordinates machining cells. **The descriptor does not rotate and nothing identifying may appear.**

- Three stages: a separately scoped expert review, then six one-week sprints of design, then an ongoing retainer at two weeks per month for twelve months — the land-and-expand arc.
- A second workstream — a touchscreen control product — was scoped at one, two or three sprints and won at three. Creative Navy argued in writing that a single sprint would produce structurally incomplete foundations creating hidden downstream cost: teams work around unresolved decisions, or corrections never happen and the system carries the debt. The client chose the largest option.

### Streetec (a small, bounded engagement that shipped)

Streetec GmbH may be named; the product is kntrl 2, shipped and in the market.

- Six one-week sprints: three UX, then three UI.
- Implementation support was scoped to a kickoff session with Streetec's engineers and one further session four weeks into development — nothing beyond that. **Two sessions: never imply a sustained Implementation Partnership.**

### AIT (fixed scope with discovery inside the block)

AIT may be named: a European manufacturer of residential and commercial heat pumps. Nothing else on either side may be named.

- Fixed scope, eight one-week sprints with discovery and requirements inside the block rather than priced separately: five UX sprints, then three UI sprints, the third being brand theming.
- 29 screens delivered, plus a clickable prototype deliberately scoped to a single flow — the scenario-editing flow, chosen because Creative Navy identified it as the most complex and most contested interaction in the product and the one where rotary-knob operation would be most laborious.
- **Implementation Partnership at its actual weight:** five months of support to AIT's engineering team during development, consisting of three scheduled progress meetings at which the state of the development was compared against the delivered designs, and eight support tickets answered. **That is a review-and-support partnership, not embedded day-to-day oversight — and because the reviews compared the build against the designs, it is the mechanism that explains the design's fidelity rather than leaving it to chance. Do not rank it against other engagements' partnerships; where another engagement's partnership appears on the same page, describe each at its actual weight.**

### The MDU (what iteration actually costs)

The product may be named — the MDU, a ship motion monitoring system whose bridge interface Creative Navy designed. The manufacturer is not named. **No sprint length is recorded for this engagement; write "five sprints", never "five one-week sprints".**

- Fixed scope: five sprints — two UX, then three UI — a four-person team (UX designer, UI designer, product designer, project manager), a clickable prototype, and a formal handover of the design.
- **18 screens delivered, across 45 versions in total — the number worth putting in front of a buyer: it is what iteration actually costs on a product where the hard decisions are about a scale and a value rather than a feature list.**
- Creative Navy briefed each of the third-party development agencies the manufacturer was considering, so that each could pitch on a common understanding of the delivered design.
- Implementation support was provided. **Its duration and substance are not recorded — state it at that level and no further.**

### Assemblio (a working method worth asking a prospective partner about)

Assemblio GmbH may be named, and so may both products — the Assemblio Composer and the Assemblio Smart Viewer.

- Nine one-week sprints: five UX, then four UI, with no separately scoped audit and no separate discovery stage — the whole engagement ran inside the sprint block. Five-person team.
- 87 screens delivered, in 233 variants — the variant count is the larger number because most screens were specified in several states (hover, selected, empty, loading, expanded, collapsed, error) rather than once.
- **The working method worth asking a prospective partner about: five documented rounds of written feedback in which the client is given named alternatives and asked to choose with reasons**, rather than being shown one proposal and asked to approve it. Three distinct navigation architectures were produced as genuine alternatives rather than a preferred option with decoys, and the client's reasons for rejecting two of them are on the record; the hybrid was chosen because it separates projects from products so the user always knows where they are, and because the sidebar carries no tree depth.

### Egma (open prioritisation when time runs short)

Egma may be named in full; the product is Egma Order, Egma's B2B lens ordering platform.

- Twelve sprints, structured as a UX phase producing wireframes followed by a UI phase producing the interface and the design system. **The split of the twelve between UX and UI is not recorded — state the two-phase structure and never a split.**
- Scope covered the web platform, a native mobile application and the administration area, spanning four order types — RX, Stock, Bulk Stock and Services — plus Home, the Order Center, Get Help and Master.
- When the remaining time looked short during the UI phase, Creative Navy stated the prioritisation to the client openly rather than silently descoping — naming what would be completed first and what would be dropped if necessary.

### Topera (a scope decision with a stated price)

Topera may be named: the cross-organisational project management platform Creative Navy produced a concept design for, grounded in the DIN 69901 series and the ProSTEP iViP / VDA recommendations. **Topera is a concept design and nothing has been built — no product, no deployment, no user, no pilot, no outcome. Every sentence must stay true of a system that does not exist: "the WBS view was designed to present the project as a tree", never "the WBS view shows".**

- **Required framing, near-verbatim:** Creative Navy scoped a full design of the platform at twenty-four one-week sprints and put that position to the client before the engagement began. The client commissioned three, as a concept design. The twenty-four-sprint figure is Creative Navy's professional estimate of the full scope, not a measured comparison between two pieces of work. **Never write "eight times faster" or "in one eighth of the time" — nothing was compressed; a different and much smaller deliverable was produced, deliberately.**
- What three sprints bought: twenty-four unique screens (**no variant or state count exists — write with this figure alone and never construct one**); a style guide covering colour, typography, corner radius, layout and spacing, icons, and a documented set of action components, each component page documenting states (**describe the coverage; state no page or component count**); four complete UI style directions for the same screen, including light and dark treatments; a Work Breakdown Structure view designed from two distinct entry points; at least four documented versions of the hardest screen; designs for states, not just screens; and an information architecture required to be complete even where the MVP was not — designed as a long-term backbone, with non-implemented areas appearing as future extension points.
- What three sprints did not buy, stated with equal weight: no user research of any kind; no usability testing; no record of client decisions between alternatives — **never write that the client chose, preferred, selected or rejected anything**; and no build, no implemented component, no development handover.
- **The page's value is in showing that a scope decision has a stated price rather than a hidden one — the buyer chose the smaller engagement knowing what the larger one would have cost and what the smaller one would not include.** Pair with the industrial engagement, which runs in the opposite direction: there Creative Navy argued against the cheapest option on structural grounds and the client took the largest; here Creative Navy named a twenty-four-sprint scope and the client took an eighth of it — and that was the right answer, because the deliverable was an argument rather than a foundation. **The common discipline is that the scope conversation happened in advance and in the open, not that bigger is better. Keep Topera and the industrial engagement in separate sentences, always.**

### KISPE Space (an exploration consultative by design, ending at a gate the client could have said no at)

KISPE Space is a UK space systems engineering company. **No quotation marks and no reported speech anywhere KISPE appears; attribute positions to the role and the document.**

- KISPE's partner brief asked for advice as well as design, and named six questions it wanted the engagement to resolve — among them where the boundary sits between this product and existing operational systems, which roles and decision journeys should shape a first release, how provenance, uncertainty, time, outages and data gaps should be communicated, and which capabilities make a useful, testable first product.
- The exploration phase of the project was explicitly consultative: detailed UX and UI scope was confirmed at its conclusion, not before it.
- **Required framing for the gate:** The exploration phase ended at a board review. Creative Navy put twelve working decisions and a recommended development sequence to KISPE's board, with a recommendation to proceed conditionally. The board endorsed the architecture as the working direction, approved a staged scope with explicit decision gates for the uncertain features, and authorised the seven-sprint UX development phase. **A commissioning decision, not user validation and not an outcome.** The commercial arc may be stated plainly: a four-sprint consultative exploration converted into a twelve-sprint design programme, at a gate the exploration was explicitly designed to reach.

### Schone Airspace Group (scope recommended, not accepted)

Schone Airspace Group commissioned the redesign of a ground control station interface for connected aircraft. **No quotation marks and no reported speech anywhere Schone appears.** **Write this engagement from its own facts: KISPE makes the same argument from its board gate, and the two must not be written as one example.**

- The client's brief named four questions it wanted answered and asked the agency to challenge the client's assumptions and recommend the scope and sequence of work rather than accept a predetermined list of screens.
- The exploration phase of the project was consultative and scope-setting: detailed UX and UI scope was confirmed at its conclusion, not before it.
- The commercial arc may be stated plainly: the four exploration sprints converted into the twelve-sprint programme, which converted into a twelve-month retainer at biweekly involvement.

### Raymarine (rescoping as reallocation rather than a change order)

Raymarine may be named; the new radio's name may not, in any form. **No quotation marks and no reported speech anywhere Raymarine appears.**

- The brief asked for a partner for product decisions, not only interface execution: it asked the agency to examine the product proposition, identify trade-offs, help shape a practical experience strategy before detailed screen design began, and explain where it would challenge or reshape the brief. It stated that the core voice and digital-calling safety functions were essential and that the AIS implementation, display depth and distribution of features between the radio and connected devices were open design decisions.
- The brief proposed a three-stage engagement — discover and advise, explore and validate, define and hand over. Creative Navy's delivery plan answered with an eight-week programme in a two / three / three split, with named weekly challenges and deliverables, weekly working reviews with Raymarine stakeholders, and an explicit delivery basis; safety and regulatory sign-off remained with Raymarine. **Whether Creative Navy reshaped the brief's stage structure or accepted it and re-expressed it is unconfirmed — do not assert either.**
- **Rescoping as reallocation:** scope was rebalanced inside the phase rather than extended — plot variations reduced to the viable core alternatives, the cross-screen control contract established before screens were detailed, critical flows reworked against one consistent input model, and hardware and firmware dependencies identified before handover rather than during high-fidelity UX. The two-week exploration and the eight-week programme were unchanged, with no additional sprint and no change to the fee assumption.
- **Implementation partnership was offered on standard terms, and the substance of what followed — its duration, form and frequency — is not recorded. State no duration, no count of reviews, no count of tickets, and never borrow another engagement's.**

### The handheld C2 engagement (scope stated as a constraint before work began)

The client is a European manufacturer of uncrewed systems, including one-way effectors, and the tactical command-and-control software that operates them. **Nothing may be named; "one-way effector", never "loitering munition". No quotation marks and no reported speech. The scope boundary appears early: the engagement covered interface structure, task behaviour, information priority and the clarity of operator control — not autonomy, control algorithms, the datalink, the effector, targeting logic, or any certification or weapons-review activity. The register is that of a safety-engineering document — no euphemism, no relish.**

- Six constraints were stated openly in the delivery plan before work began, and the sixth is the scoping lesson: **the scale of the system exceeds the size of the project, as a starting premise, with four areas selected as a consequence.** The others: the target hardware had not yet been settled; requirements were shifting and that was understood to be the nature of the client's situation; the client had a strong preference against a large development effort but would fund one if justified; the client was not certain the multi-vehicle problem was solvable by design at all; and design conventions including conflicts between NATO and non-NATO standards had to be observed.
- **A standing scope-and-replan item:** every sprint ended with Creative Navy flagging which opportunities to pursue and which to ignore, with the decision resting with the client — inside a cadence of three meetings a week with the client's design team and a weekly developer progress meeting channelling feasibility and build-cost input.
- **The scoping output that paid for itself:** the separately scoped expert review's most consequential finding was that the next piece of work had the wrong shape — the handheld is not a scaled-down laptop but a different product solving a related problem, the question is not how to fit the full experience onto a smaller screen but which subset is genuinely needed, and the scope question must be answered first and should drive the feature set.

### The maritime VTS engagement (an agency recommending less work than the brief asked for)

The client is a European vendor of maritime traffic monitoring, inland navigation and communications software for port authorities, coastal administrations and waterway operators. **The client may never be named and the descriptor does not rotate. No quotation marks and no reported speech.** **This engagement must never read as a nine-product redesign: nine products were audited; one was designed to full depth.**

- **Lead with the thing a buyer actually wants to know: what it looks like when an agency recommends less work than the brief asked for, and how that recommendation is made defensible.** Discovery replaced "a common modern interface across nine products" with: one application developed to full depth as a reference, a technology-neutral semantic design system built in parallel, bounded transfer tests in four other products, and implementation contracts and governance handed to the client's engineers. The stated reason: a cosmetic refresh could make a mixed alert region visually quieter while preserving the same interpretation risk, and a broadly reusable status component could conceal whether a radio resource is unselected, unavailable or configured for transmission.
- **The scorecard is what makes the recommendation defensible:** the reference product was selected on five weighted criteria agreed in advance — commercial significance 25%, system transfer value 25%, access to representative users 20%, high-consequence UX learning 15%, implementation feasibility 15% — and five candidates were scored out of 100: VTS 90, inland vessel tracking 77, voice communications 71, electronic reporting 67, lock planning 63. VTS won because it combines geographic surveillance, uncertain sensor observations, differentiated alerts, event history, incident state, equipment health and shift handover in one environment; voice communications scored highly on consequence and lost on portability. **The winner was selected on transfer value and consequence rather than on size.**
- The gate was a condition of the engagement from the proposal onwards, chaired by the programme sponsor with the product, engineering and commercial owners present. **Three decision options were defined in advance: proceed, revise the reference product or scope, or stop and retain the discovery package as a self-contained strategic deliverable — a genuine stop with a self-contained deliverable, not a formality.**
- Six objections were recorded and dispositioned on the record rather than resolved offline — among them whether one redesigned application would be persuasive in multi-product tenders, whether a design file would translate into multiple frontend stacks, the risk of changing familiar controls without operator validation, and financial protection against expansion into nine full redesigns.
- Approval was conditional, with seven conditions each carrying a named client owner and a verification method, and a written release followed the conditional approval and authorised mobilisation.

### Oceaneering (a contingency named in advance and spent where the work proved hardest)

Oceaneering may be named; nothing else may. **Every client or stakeholder position is reported speech attributed to a role, never inside quotation marks.**

- Phase structure: a discovery sprint, then four one-week UX sprints — three planned plus a contingency sprint, spent on UX because the UX work is the most complex part of a Creative Navy engagement — then three and a half UI sprints, with four review sessions during the block and one at the end.
- 54 screens delivered; 321 screens counted with variations. Six-person team: project manager, UX lead, UI designer, product designer, interaction designer, researcher.
- **A clean worked example of a scope that named its contingency in advance and then spent it where the work proved hardest.**
