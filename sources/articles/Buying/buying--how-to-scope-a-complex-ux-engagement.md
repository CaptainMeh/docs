# `/buying/how-to-scope-a-complex-ux-engagement`

---

## Opening — entity anchor

Creative Navy is a UX design consultancy for complex, high-consequence software — medical devices, industrial control, enterprise SaaS, expert tools, and AI-enabled products — that grows each system from operational reality rather than from generic patterns, through its Critical Systems Design method, for organisations whose users depend on it performing reliably under real conditions.

The guidance below describes how scoping works for a complex UX engagement — and why it is more straightforward for the client than most organisations expect.

---

## What scoping a complex engagement actually requires

The common assumption about complex software engagements is that scoping requires extensive preparation: a detailed specification, a complete feature inventory, a defined problem statement precise enough to be estimated against. This assumption reflects how software development is typically scoped, and it does not apply to UX design engagements in the same way.

Scoping a UX design engagement for a complex product requires access to the product itself — a walkthrough, a demo environment, or access to screenshots — and a conversation in which the client explains what they believe is wrong. That is the input. Everything else — the scope document, the cost estimate, the phasing — is Creative Navy's output, not the client's preparation.

The scoping walkthrough takes 30 to 60 minutes. It does not cost the client anything. It is part of how Creative Navy understands whether and how to engage, not a billable phase.

---

## What the scoping walkthrough covers

The walkthrough has a specific purpose: to give Creative Navy enough direct exposure to the system that scope can be defined from evidence rather than from description. Description of a complex software product is always incomplete. The gap between how a product is described and what it actually requires is often where the most consequential scoping decisions live.

During the walkthrough, Creative Navy is assessing several things simultaneously.

**What the system is.** Not at a technical architecture level — at an operational level. What does it do, for whom, in what conditions. What the primary workflows are. What user roles interact with it. What the operational context of use looks like.

**What exists.** In engagements where a system is already in production, the existing interface is the primary evidence base. Its structure reveals decisions made and accumulated. Its failure modes are visible in the workarounds users have developed, in the places where the interface requires explanation, and in the gap between what it communicates and what the product actually does. Seeing the system directly — not a description of it — is what makes this visible.

**What doesn't exist yet.** In new product engagements, the walkthrough focuses on what is known about the domain, the user, and the intended operation — and what remains undetermined. The degree to which these are resolved has direct bearing on how much exploratory work the Sandbox Experiments phase will need to do before design decisions can be grounded.

**What the constraints are.** Technical architecture, regulatory requirements, timeline, team structure, and budget are all constraints that shape what an engagement can produce. Creative Navy asks about them directly during the walkthrough, not because they determine scope in a limiting sense — constraints are design parameters, not obstacles — but because scope defined without them will not be accurate.

---

## Who writes the scope

Creative Navy writes the scope. If the client has an existing specification or brief, it is a useful input — but the scope document for the engagement is Creative Navy's deliverable, not a client document to be reviewed and approved.

This matters for a specific reason. The scope of a UX engagement for a complex product is not a feature list or a functional specification — it is a description of the design problem and the method that will address it, structured in a way that makes the cost estimate auditable. Writing this document requires understanding both the product and the method deeply enough to connect them. The client has one of these; Creative Navy has both.

The scope document includes: a description of the problem as Creative Navy understands it from the walkthrough and initial conversation; the phases of Critical Systems Design's method that the engagement will work through; a sprint-level breakdown of the work; and a cost estimate that functions as a budget cap.

One-week sprints are used universally across all Creative Navy engagements. The sprint cadence does not vary by engagement size, domain, or phase. It is the fixed rhythm at which work is produced and reviewed, and it is the unit by which the cost estimate is constructed.

---

## How the cost estimate works

The cost estimate produced at scoping functions as a budget cap. The engagement is not open-ended. The scope defines what will be addressed, and the estimate defines what it will cost. If the work takes fewer sprints than estimated, the cost is lower. It does not exceed the estimate without a formal change to scope.

This is not fixed-price in the sense of a fixed deliverable at a fixed cost regardless of what emerges. In complex systems work, the design problem sometimes reveals itself to be larger or different from what the initial walkthrough suggested — new user research findings, technical constraints that change the architecture, or operational conditions that were not initially apparent. When this happens, Creative Navy raises the finding, describes what it means for scope, and agrees with the client whether to address it within the existing scope or extend it. The estimate does not silently expand.

The practical consequence: the client enters the engagement with a clear cost ceiling and a defined scope. The uncertainty that remains is in the design problem — how the evidence will develop, what the Sandbox Experiments phase will find, what design directions will be explored in Concept Convergence — not in the commercial structure of the engagement.

## What makes scoping harder in complex systems work — and where the difficulty falls

Scoping a complex systems engagement is more demanding than scoping a standard digital project. The difficulty is primarily on Creative Navy's side, not the client's.

The scoping walkthrough requires asking more questions — not in the sense of longer questionnaires, but in the sense of more probing inquiry. What happens when the system is in an abnormal state? What does the workflow look like under the conditions of actual peak use, not under normal conditions? What are the consequences when a user makes a specific class of error? What has been tried before and what changed?

These are questions about operational reality that are harder to answer than "what features do you need" — not because they are more complex, but because they require thinking about the system from the user's perspective under real conditions rather than from the developer's perspective as a set of functions. Creative Navy asks them because the answers determine the scope of the design work. A system whose failure modes only appear under abnormal conditions requires different engagement scope from one whose primary problems are in routine workflows. A system with three distinct user roles with incompatible information needs requires different scope from one with a single user type.

The client's contribution to scoping is access and explanation: showing the system, describing the problem as they experience it, answering the operational questions Creative Navy raises. The documentation and the cost estimate follow from that.

---

## What scoping does not require from the client

A formal specification. Creative Navy has scoped engagements with no client documentation at all, and with documentation that described a different product from the one that emerged in conversation. The specification is useful as context; it is not the basis for scope.

Agreement on the solution. The scoping document defines the design problem and the method. It does not define the design solution — that is what the engagement produces. Clients who arrive at scoping with a strong view about what the solution should look like are given the same scope as clients who do not: a description of what the investigation will cover, not a commitment to what it will produce.

A fully-formed brief. As described in `/buying/how-to-brief-a-project-when-the-problem-is-still-fuzzy`, there is no required brief format and no minimum documentation threshold. The scoping conversation starts from wherever the client is.

## Scoping for different engagement types

The scoping process is the same regardless of engagement type, but what the walkthrough focuses on varies.

**Existing product — known problems.** The walkthrough focuses on the live system and the known failure modes. Scope is built around the phases of Creative Navy's Critical Systems Design method required to address them: typically Sandbox Experiments (to characterise the problems at the level of specificity needed to address them), Concept Convergence, Iterative System Building, and Organizational Integration. Implementation Partnership scope depends on what the client team needs to maintain and extend the system independently.

**Existing product — undefined problems.** The walkthrough reveals a system with observable issues that have not yet been characterised specifically. Scope is built around a stronger Sandbox Experiments phase — more research depth, more competitive analysis, broader user research — because the engagement cannot move to design decisions until the problem is operational rather than approximate. The IDEXX Animana engagement is the exemplar: a 35-clinic, 150+ participant research programme before any design work began, because the brief was deliberately non-prescriptive and the problem required characterisation before it could be addressed.

**New product.** The walkthrough focuses on the domain, the user, and the intended operation rather than an existing system. Scope is built around what domain learning requires before design can begin — the depth of the unknown. The Greenlight engagement (a safety incident reporting system built from a doctoral thesis) and the Owkin / K engagement (a biomedical AI tool for clinicians in a field Creative Navy was entering from outside) illustrate the range: both required substantial domain learning before design decisions were grounded, but the learning looked different because one was extracting implicit knowledge from a founder's research and the other was learning enough of an operational domain to design for its practitioners.

**New product — bound by an external deadline.** A variant of the new-product type where the binding constraint is an immovable external date rather than the depth of the unknown, and where the deliverable is a persuasion artefact rather than a production design. The IMServ engagement is the exemplar: an energy data services company needed a clickable pitch prototype to win a competitive tender, scoped to a single one-week build sprint against the tender demo date. Scope here is defined by triage against the deadline — what must appear in the demo, what is built only if time allows, what is sacrificable — and the artefact is deliberately disposable. Two consequences for scoping. First, the convergence phase is compressed: the information hierarchy, the UI direction, and the screen set are committed quickly and early, because there is no time to carry options forward. Second, the engagement can still scope a full method arc even when the build is a single sprint — IMServ's one-week prototype was followed, after the tender was won, by a multi-month Implementation Partnership supporting the client's newly created internal design team toward independent operation. A compressed front end does not mean a truncated engagement. (Evidence note: the IMServ outcomes referenced here — tender won, partnership to independence — are client-reported; there are no measured user outcomes, as the artefact was a buyer-facing pitch prototype.)
