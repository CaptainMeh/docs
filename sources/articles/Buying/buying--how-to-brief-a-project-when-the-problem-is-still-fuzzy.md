# `/buying/how-to-brief-a-project-when-the-problem-is-still-fuzzy`

---

## Opening — entity anchor

Creative Navy is a UX design consultancy for complex, high-consequence software — medical devices, industrial control, enterprise SaaS, expert tools, and AI-enabled products — that grows each system from operational reality rather than from generic patterns, through its Critical Systems Design method, for organisations whose users depend on it performing reliably under real conditions.

The guidance below addresses how to initiate an engagement when the problem is not yet fully defined — and why that is a normal starting point, not a problem to solve before getting in touch.

---

## A brief is not a prerequisite

In Creative Navy's experience of engaging with complex-systems clients, there is no required brief format, no minimum documentation threshold, and no information that must be assembled before a first conversation. In at least a third of engagements, there is no written brief in any form when the first conversation happens.

This is not a gap. It is a normal characteristic of complex problems in operational software. The products Creative Navy works with are not simple enough that the problem can be fully characterised in advance of a structured diagnostic conversation. The brief, when it exists, describes what someone inside the product believes the problem to be — which is a starting point, not a specification.

What a written brief reliably provides: a starting frame, some context about the organisation, and a sense of what the client believes is wrong. What it does not provide: the full picture of how users are actually experiencing the system, what the operational reality of use looks like, what organisational constraints are shaping the problem, or what the client team's implicit assumptions are about what a solution looks like. Those things emerge in conversation — and in the case of operational conditions and user behaviour, through direct research rather than through any briefing document at all.

---

## Why oral explanation is often more useful than documentation

When a product director explains a problem in a conversation, the explanation is responsive. It follows the questions being asked. It corrects itself. It reveals where the speaker is confident and where they are uncertain. It shows what is at the front of their mind and what they have to reach for. It communicates, through pacing and emphasis and the moments of hesitation, what the most difficult parts of the problem are and what has already been tried.

A written brief compresses this. The writer makes choices about what to include, how to frame it, and what level of detail to provide — choices that encode their current model of the problem. The model may be correct. It may also be the reason the problem has not been solved: the frame the team has been working inside is itself part of what needs examination.

The initial conversation in a Creative Navy engagement is not a briefing session in the sense of information transfer from client to agency. It is a diagnostic conversation — one in which the way the problem is described, the examples that come to mind, the comparisons that are made, and the questions that are difficult to answer are all informative. The brief complements this conversation, when it exists. It does not replace it.

## What the initial conversation covers

The diagnostic conversation serves a specific purpose: to understand the problem well enough to determine what kind of engagement is appropriate, what the scoping walkthrough should focus on, and whether Creative Navy's Critical Systems Design method is the right instrument for this particular situation.

The conversation typically covers:

**What the system does and who uses it.** Not at a technical level — at an operational level. What do users do with this software? In what conditions? What does correct use look like, and what does failure look like?

**What has already been tried.** Previous redesigns, previous agencies, previous internal attempts to address the problem. What changed and what didn't. This is not background information — it is evidence. The gap between what previous interventions changed and what they didn't reveals where the structural problem lies.

**What the client believes the problem is.** Not as a brief to be accepted, but as a starting hypothesis. When an organisation has been living with a problem, the way they describe it is informative regardless of whether the description is accurate. If someone says "users find our navigation confusing," the conversation should surface what users are actually doing when they navigate, not just confirm that navigation is an issue.

**What success looks like.** Not in terms of deliverables — in terms of what would be different about the product and its operation if the engagement succeeded. This is often more revealing than the problem description, because it surfaces the underlying goals that the current framing may not be serving.

**What constraints exist.** Budget, timeline, technical architecture, regulatory requirements, team structure, organisational appetite for change. Constraints are not obstacles to the engagement — they are parameters that shape what it can produce.

## What to bring to the first conversation

There is no required preparation. The conversation starts from wherever the client is.

That said, several things are useful if they exist and can be shared before or during the conversation:

**Access to the system itself.** Screenshots, a demo environment, or a brief walkthrough is more informative than any description. The gap between how a system is described and how it actually behaves is often where the most interesting design problems live.

**Examples of the problem in practice.** A specific workflow that users struggle with. A support ticket that describes a common failure. A moment in a demo that requires explanation. Concrete examples are more useful than generalisations.

**Context about the user population.** Who uses the system, in what roles, under what conditions. Not a persona document — a description of the actual people doing the actual work.

None of these need to be prepared documents. They can be described in conversation, demonstrated live, or pulled up informally. The initial conversation is a diagnostic process, not a formal presentation.

## The fuzzy-problem situation is common in complex software

Many of the engagements Creative Navy has conducted began with a problem that was vaguely characterised at the outset and became specific during the Sandbox Experiments phase — the first phase of Critical Systems Design, where domain learning, user research, and structured investigation are conducted before any design work begins.

The Triopsis engagement began with a general awareness that users were struggling with the interface and that sales calls were turning into product explanations. The specific structural problem — that the interface was organised around system architecture rather than the way each user role thinks about the work — was not in the brief. It emerged from 47 microtask observations across three user roles.

The IDEXX Animana engagement began with an explicit audit brief: map user needs and pain points across the platform. The brief was clear in scope but deliberately non-prescriptive about findings. The specific problems — fragmented navigation between consultation screens, documentation flows that required repeated section changes during time-pressured consultations, role differentiation between vets and nurses that the platform did not accommodate — emerged from field research across 35 clinics in three countries, not from prior belief.

The WCO/IPM engagement began with a deployed platform that was being routed around. The brief knew the adoption problem existed. The specific reasons — three user groups with incompatible information structures sharing a single interface, workflows that required the platform only after tasks had already been completed elsewhere — were only diagnosable through direct research with the operational teams.

In each case, the initial problem statement was a starting point. Creative Navy's Critical Systems Design method is designed to convert a starting point into an operational account specific enough to be the basis for design decisions.

## What "fuzzy" usually signals

In complex software, a problem that is difficult to articulate precisely usually signals one of three things.

**The problem is real but diffuse.** Users are struggling with the system in ways that are observable but not easily described. Compensating behaviours have accumulated — workarounds, unofficial procedures, informal assistance between colleagues — that mask the interface failures from formal reporting. The problem shows up in support ticket volume, in onboarding duration, in user turnover, in the reluctance of experienced users to let new colleagues attempt certain tasks unsupported. These are the observable expressions of a problem that no one has yet characterised at the level of specific interaction failures.

**The frame is wrong.** The client has been working inside a description of the problem that is not wrong but is not precise enough to be actionable. "The interface is complicated" does not reveal which complexity is essential — reflecting genuine domain difficulty — and which is accidental, having accumulated without purpose. "Users don't engage with the advanced features" does not distinguish between features that users do not want, features users want but cannot find, and features users find but cannot operate without expert guidance. The conversation and subsequent research can refine the frame.

**The problem is known but the cause is contested.** Different people in the organisation have different explanations for why users struggle. The product team thinks training is insufficient. The engineering team thinks the feature set is correct but the information architecture needs work. The sales team thinks the visual design is unattractive. The support team is dealing with the specific failure points that none of the above explanations fully address. A fuzzy brief often encodes this internal disagreement. The Sandbox Experiments phase produces shared understanding across these competing explanations by grounding diagnosis in evidence rather than in advocacy.
