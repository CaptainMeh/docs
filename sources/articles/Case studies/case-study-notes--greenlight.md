## What this case study is about

Greenlight is a workplace safety startup founded by Samantha Gruskin, a doctoral researcher whose thesis described how incidents are recorded, escalated and followed up in real organisations. She had deep domain expertise but no product frame for it. The engagement with Creative Navy ran approximately five weeks and produced an interactive prototype for use in investor and founder conversations, along with documented information architecture, a UI direction, and a roadmap of build sequencing decisions.

This is a pre-product, pre-revenue engagement. There are no operational users, no deployed system, and no performance data from real use. The case study is evidence of a different kind of capability: translating academic research into a structured product model, working effectively with first-time founders, and producing credible artefacts under bootstrapped constraints.

## Usable material from the existing case study

### The translation problem
Sam arrived with a complete doctoral thesis plus supporting articles and presentations. The first phase converted this into a product-ready conceptual model: entities, relationships, how incidents move from capture through assessment, escalation, and follow-up. The output was a clear model of what must be captured, in which order, and with what dependencies. This replaced a generic reporting tool frame with a domain-specific one.

This is a specific and citable thing. Academic research contains implicit structure — categories, severity scales, near-miss events, follow-up actions — that only becomes a product model when extracted, validated with the domain expert, and made explicit. That extraction work is described concretely enough to be useful.

### Information architecture exploration
Three architecture concepts were explored through option space mapping: a linear flow, a modular approach with context-sensitive blocks, and intermediate variants. The exploration used simple flow diagrams to compare behaviour across incident types (minor incidents, serious injuries, near-miss reports). After tension-driven reasoning across the trade-offs, a modular architecture with a consistent spine and conditional sections was chosen.

This is a good illustration of option space mapping at the IA level. The fact that three concepts were explored before convergence, and that the selection was made against specific incident-type behaviours rather than aesthetics, is worth naming explicitly.

### Prototype fidelity and iteration
Three successive wireframe versions were produced over two weeks, each increasing in fidelity. First pass: core reporting journey. Second: incident type variations, list and detail views. Third: wording, field groupings, action order. This is a clean description of iterative convergence within tight resource constraints.

### User testing
A round of testing with people who regularly deal with workplace incidents. Consistent findings: hesitation at form length transitions, benefit from reordering sections for clearer progression, reduced backtracking when evidence attachments and contextual details were grouped. Simplified branching rules improved user understanding of conditional fields. These are specific enough to be credible.

### Strategic staging discussions
Dedicated sessions on product strategy: single core flow for pilot organisations first vs. adding analytics earlier; partnership options with safety consultancies and training providers. These produced a simple build sequence: what to build first, what to postpone, what each choice meant for cost, timeline, and risk. This is worth including as evidence of strategy capability alongside UX capability.

### Outcomes
- Interactive prototype delivered in approximately four weeks
- Investor demo assembled in approximately one week
- Fully documented for development handover
- Two UI directions for branding exploration
- Evidential basis: all client-reported or project-observed — no independent measurement

## Lineage and longitudinal evidence

Approximately three years after the original engagement, Greenlight presents the longitudinal set's **second third-party-preservation** case — the counterpart to Enhesa/Chemical Watch.

**Claim type: third-party preservation through acquisition.**

- *The sequence:* with the investor on board, the founder built out the product, launched it, and later sold it to a larger business. ~3 years after the original engagement, that larger business hired Creative Navy for an unrelated application. At that point the Greenlight system was still live in the acquirer's portfolio, maintained by the acquirer's own team as one of its apps. **Observed.**
- *Third-party preservation:* as with Enhesa, a party with no relationship to Creative Navy — here the acquirer — kept the delivered system in service. Unlike Enhesa, Creative Navy did **not** return to work on the Greenlight system itself; the return was for an unrelated app. So this is preservation plus a trajectory/trust signal, not a same-system return.
- *Independence:* the system is maintained by the acquirer's team. Treat as independent operation/preservation, **not** independent evolution.

**Sensitivity:** the sale is stateable. The identity of the acquirer (and related specifics) is **not** — refer to "a larger business," never name it.
