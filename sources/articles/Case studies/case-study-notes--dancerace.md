## Client, product, and naming

**Client:** Dancerace. Can be named.

**Product:** Jacko — a B2B invoice management and accounts receivable portal for small businesses. Not a payroll platform. The product manages the distribution, confirmation, dispute, and settlement of sales invoices across a three-party structure.

**Three-party structure:**
- The **financier** operates Dancerace's existing C3 financing system and manages collections on behalf of suppliers. The financier activates supplier accounts on Jacko.
- The **supplier** is Dancerace's direct client — typically a small business that issues invoices and wants to be paid.
- The **debtor/customer** is the small business that receives invoices and must act on them.

The portal becomes the channel through which all three parties interact around the lifecycle of an invoice: distribution, acknowledgment, confirmation, dispute, remittance intent, and payment.

The three-party model was broadly understood at the start of the engagement. The design work did not change how the roles were defined, but it surfaced a large number of detail questions about how each role could do specific things, which states each interaction needed to have, and how the experience of one user connected to the experience of another.

**Product type:** Greenfield. Dancerace had an existing C3 backend system. Jacko was a new product built on top of it — not a redesign of an existing interface. Existing Dancerace customers were used as a source of information during discovery but the system was not designed around their prior expectations.

**Engagement duration:** Five months. All five phases of Creative Navy's Critical Systems Design method were included.

## The situation at the start of the engagement

Dancerace came to the engagement with strong domain knowledge, a clear commercial ambition, and a large list of premium features they wanted to build. The feature list — generated by Dancerace before the engagement — included DocuSign integration for purchase orders, a supplier marketplace, a payment calendar with RAG grading, mobile SMS notifications, live FX conversion, B2B debt insurance, Companies House credit checking, and more. This list represented a genuine vision for the platform's eventual scope. It also represented a risk: none of it was anchored to a model of what users would actually do on first encounter with the system.

The stakeholder group was divided along two fault lines.

One camp wanted the product to showcase its advanced features prominently — to signal sophistication, justify the subscription price, and make sure users appreciated the platform's full capability before they made a purchase decision. They worried that users would not discover or value the advanced features if those features were not made immediately visible.

A second camp wanted users to complete a full profile setup and configure rules, settings, and automation logic before doing anything else — so that the system could perform its core automated operations correctly. From this camp's perspective, the product could not deliver value until users had committed to the configuration work upfront.

Underneath both positions was a third pressure: converting free trial users to paying customers quickly enough to validate the business model. The trial was self-serve and required no credit card — meaning conversion was entirely dependent on users reaching value on their own before the trial period ended.

The fundamental problem with all three pressures was that none of them had been tested against what a real user would actually do. Dancerace's ideas were directionally correct — they understood their domain, their users, and their market. But they could not yet specify what states every interaction needed to have, how the debtor's experience connected to the supplier's, or what the precise consequence of each design decision would be for real user behaviour. They could not answer these questions when asked directly. The design work made it possible for them to have those conversations, because the wireframes gave them something to react to, disagree about, and make decisions against.

This gap between directional understanding and actionable specificity is what Creative Navy's Critical Systems Design method calls the **blanks phenomenon** — client intuition that is half-right but blank of the substance needed to make design decisions from it.

## Sandbox Experiments

### Challenge areas explored

Creative Navy identified three challenge areas as the critical design problems Jacko needed to answer before anything else could be resolved:

1. **Onboarding and initial setup** — how to get a new user to a point of value without requiring the full configuration work the second stakeholder camp wanted upfront.
2. **The returning user dashboard** — what to show users on each login, in what order, and at what level of detail.
3. **The payroll run / invoice action flow** — how users perform the core recurring task: reviewing invoices, taking action on them, and understanding their financial position.

Multiple concepts were explored for each challenge area through rapid prototyping and testing.

### Key discovery 1: The user hierarchy of needs

Through a combination of user testing, user interviews, and prototype observation, Creative Navy discovered that users approach the system with a strict internal priority order. Before anything else, users need to answer three questions:

- What do I owe?
- What is my cost?
- What tasks do I absolutely need to do right now?

Until those three questions are satisfied, users cannot be mobilised for anything else — not profile setup, not advanced features, not upsell prompts. This ordering was not stated explicitly by users. It was observed through how people interacted with prototypes, confirmed in interviews, and triangulated across the three research methods.

The implication was direct: a system that put configuration requirements or advanced feature showcasing ahead of these three questions would lose users before it delivered any value. The returning user dashboard had to answer these questions first, in order, before surfacing anything else.

### Key discovery 2: The cost of premature complexity

When advanced features competed visually with a user's immediate needs on the same screen, users disengaged — not just from the advanced features, but from the basic use of the system as well. The stake in showcasing advanced features produced the opposite of its intended effect: users who could not quickly find what they needed stopped using the system entirely.

This was demonstrated to stakeholders through analytical reasoning: showing, wireframe by wireframe, the specific consequence for user behaviour of each competing design decision. The argument was not abstract. It showed what a particular user type would see on a given screen, what they would likely do next, and what that meant for engagement, trust, and conversion.

### Key discovery 3: The reality of debtor behaviour

The domain learning phase produced an insight that shaped the interaction model throughout the system. Real debtors do not behave according to formal business norms. They delay, they acknowledge without committing, they use whatever informal signals are available to manage relationships and buy time. A debtor who knows they will pay late does not, in practice, open a formal dispute — they go quiet.

Rather than designing against this behaviour, Creative Navy modelled it and designed with it. The system was built to accommodate the things debtors actually do in practice, while simultaneously making that behaviour legible to the supplier.

One concrete example: the system allows a debtor to change the status of an invoice to "accepted." This is a symbolic gesture — it does not trigger payment. But it signals good faith. A debtor who knows they will run late can acknowledge the invoice without disputing it, communicating intent without creating a formal conflict. This keeps the relationship functional and gives the supplier meaningful information: they can see whether their debtor has seen the invoice, accepted it, ignored it, or disputed it. The informal behaviour of the debtor becomes operationally visible to the party that needs to make decisions based on it.

This insight came from modelling what people actually do in financial relationships under time pressure and limited attention — not from asking users what they thought they should do. The method behind this is domain learning: understanding the domain from inside its operational reality rather than from the outside.

This connects to the site page `/failures/workflow-failures/the-system-fights-the-user-task` — the failure mode the design was explicitly avoiding.

### Key discovery 4: The client's existing wireframe as a prior iteration

Before engaging Creative Navy, Dancerace had produced an internal wireframe: a dense, feature-complete dashboard showing every capability the system might eventually contain — sparklines for multiple invoice categories, a payments chart, a calendar, tasks, recent documents, and more, all on one screen.

This wireframe was not a design that Creative Navy built or tested. But it functioned as a useful prior iteration. It demonstrated what the system could contain, and it demonstrated clearly what the system should not be: a flat inventory of features with no information hierarchy, no priority ordering, and no model of what users needed first.

Creative Navy treated this wireframe as evidence rather than a starting point — using it to establish the direction to eliminate before Sandbox Experiments formally began. One iteration in the option space had already been run, at Dancerace's expense, before the engagement started. This is consistent with the principle in Creative Navy's Critical Systems Design method that learning what does not work is as valuable as learning what does, and that the option space should be mapped before convergence.

## Concept Convergence

### The tension

The central tension in Concept Convergence was the conflict between two positions that had appeared to be irreconcilable:

- The camp that wanted advanced features visible and prominent, so users would appreciate and ultimately buy the premium package.
- The camp that wanted users to configure the system fully before accessing its functionality, so automated operations would work correctly.

Both positions had legitimate reasoning behind them. The product did need to generate subscription revenue. The automated operations did need configuration data to function. But neither position had been tested against the reality of what a first-time user would actually do when confronted with their demands.

The design work during Sandbox Experiments had already established that premature complexity caused disengagement. Concept Convergence was where this evidence was used to resolve the tension rather than simply name it.

### The resolution: the chasing routines concept

The resolution came through a specific design concept: rather than asking users to configure complex, rule-based automation from scratch (which the second stakeholder camp wanted), the system offered a set of pre-built chasing routine templates — selectable patterns for how invoice follow-up would work. Users could pick a template and immediately have a functioning automation without understanding the underlying rule structure. The detail was available to users who wanted to go deeper, but it was not required to get started.

When this concept was presented to the stakeholder group, something shifted. The group had been treating simplicity and feature richness as opposites — as if making the system approachable for a first-time user necessarily meant hiding its capabilities. The chasing routines concept made visible that simplicity, done correctly, is an avenue into complexity rather than a barrier to it. A user who picks a template, sees it work, and builds trust in the system will explore further. A user who is asked to configure rules before they have experienced any value will not.

This was the moment the stakeholder group aligned. The insight was not that one camp had been wrong — it was that the opposition they had been arguing about was not the real choice. The real choice was about the path by which users reach depth, not about whether depth should exist.

This is what Creative Navy's Critical Systems Design method means by **tension-driven reasoning**: the tension between local optimisation (making advanced features prominent on each screen) and system coherence (building a product that earns user trust sequentially) was not resolved by compromise. It was resolved by examining what drove the tension and finding a configuration that made the opposition disappear.

The **competitive vector** that emerged: a platform that sequences value delivery so that each layer of capability is introduced at the moment the user is ready for it, rather than before. This is the strategic position that neither stakeholder camp had articulated before the design made it visible.

### Two-user-type problem

Dancerace's user base contained two meaningfully different types:

- **John Smith** (small business owner, approximately 25% of users): wants to complete a small number of tasks quickly. Will look at the dashboard highlights and act on the most urgent item. Will not explore unless the system has already established trust.
- **Janet** (accountant or finance director, approximately 75% of users): needs a more systematic experience with more detail. Will click into highlights to reach full data views. Manages a higher volume of invoices and allocates more time to the task.

Dancerace did not have the development capacity to build two separate interfaces. The design resolved this within a single interface through a specific mechanism: the dashboard surfaces highlights — the immediate answers to what do I owe, what is my cost, what must I do now — and every highlight is clickable, leading either to a detailed page or a modal with full data.

John Smith reads the highlights and stops. Janet clicks through to the detail. Neither user is forced to encounter what they don't need. The same interface serves both, because depth is available but never required.

This is an example of **constraint respecting** in Creative Navy's Critical Systems Design method: treating a genuine organisational constraint (single interface, limited dev capacity) as a design parameter rather than an obstacle, and finding a solution that works within it.

## Iterative System Building

Full interactive wireframe prototypes were built across all flows. The product specification spreadsheet shows the scope: account management, sales and purchase ledger, payments, messaging, notifications, customer and supplier management, templates, and a configurable dashboard. Each area was designed and resolved through divergent-convergent cycles.

The design system that emerged from this phase was documented at a level of completeness that was unusual in the engagement. According to Dancerace, developers rarely needed to reach out during implementation. What made this possible was the combination of component coverage, consistent page logic (making it always clear where a given element belonged), and accompanying written documentation explaining the reasoning behind design decisions. The design system functioned as a navigation map — teams could extend the system intelligently because they understood what they were protecting.

## Organizational Integration

The stakeholder alignment work was the primary integration challenge. The group had entered the engagement divided. The design process did not resolve this through persuasion. It resolved it by giving the group a medium — the wireframes — through which they could have conversations they had not been able to have before.

Seeing a wireframe and being asked "if you decide this, the consequence for the user experience is that" made the abstract specific. Disagreements in the team that had been about principle became disagreements about observable consequences. Decisions that had seemed impossible became possible because the design made the trade-offs visible.

This is distinct from stakeholder management in the conventional sense. The goal was not to get the group to agree with Creative Navy's preferred direction. The goal was to help the group understand their own product clearly enough to make real decisions — and then to make them.

The workshops produced shared understanding of: what the product is, who it is for, and how the mechanic generates value for users and then asks for the fee. Dancerace gained not just a designed product but a shared organisational understanding of why the product works the way it does.

## Implementation Partnership

The design system documentation was thorough enough that developers executed independently with minimal clarification requests. After delivery, Dancerace ran A/B tests using scripts provided by Creative Navy, executing them without Creative Navy's direct involvement. The tests addressed small but consequential interaction questions — default column configurations, whether detail boxes should be collapsed or expanded by default, and whether a search field needed to be visible or could be represented as an icon. Results were directional and informed final implementation decisions. Specific quantitative findings from those tests are client-held and not available for publication.

## Outcomes

### Primary measurable outcome

**36% demo-to-paying conversion rate**, measured six months after release across self-serve free trial users who signed up without providing a credit card.

**What makes this number meaningful:** Industry benchmarks for self-serve SaaS conversion at the time of release were 15–20%. Dancerace's own expectation going into the product launch was a conversion rate in that range. The 36% result is approximately double the expected rate.

**Evidential basis:** Client-reported. Measured by Dancerace against their own trial-to-paid conversion tracking over the six months following release. Not independently verified by Creative Navy.

**Causal connection:** The conversion rate is a downstream result of an operational outcome — users who encountered a clarity-first dashboard that answered their immediate questions sequentially built enough trust in the trial period to convert without a payment commitment. The design's specific mechanism (hierarchy of needs on the dashboard, no mandatory upfront configuration, progressive access to complexity via templates) is what enabled that trust to form within the trial window.

This connects to the site page `/evidence/outcomes/verifiable-performance-claims` — the 36% figure is a result that Dancerace can demonstrate in a product trial, not just assert in marketing materials.

### Operational outcomes

- Users can understand their financial position (what they owe, what tasks are urgent) immediately on login, without training or configuration.
- The system accommodates the real behaviour of debtors — acknowledgment without commitment, late communication, symbolic good-faith gestures — making informal financial behaviour operationally visible to suppliers.
- Both a time-pressured small business owner and a detail-oriented finance director can use the same interface effectively, without either being burdened by the other's needs.
- The design system enabled developer implementation with minimal clarification requests — evidence of the clarity of design logic and rationale documentation.

### Organisational outcomes

- A divided stakeholder group reached genuine alignment on what the product is, who it is for, and how value is delivered and monetised.
- Dancerace transferred capability to run their own user testing after the engagement, using Creative Navy-authored test scripts.
- The product launched with a clear distinction between baseline features and premium features — a structure that was absent and contested at the start of the engagement.

## Proprietary concepts illustrated in this case study

Use these terms accurately when writing the page. Do not introduce them all at once — weave them in where they are naturally illustrated.

**Blanks phenomenon** — Dancerace's stakeholders had directionally correct intuitions but could not specify interaction states, role connections, or consequence of design decisions. They could not produce this detail when asked. They could produce it when confronted with wireframes. This is the clearest instance of the blanks phenomenon in the case study set.

**Tension-driven reasoning** — The conflict between showcasing advanced features and requiring upfront configuration was not resolved by compromise. It was resolved by identifying the false assumption driving the conflict (that simplicity and feature depth are opposites) and designing through it. The chasing routines concept is the concrete artifact that dissolved the tension.

**Competitive vector** — The strategic position that emerged: sequential value delivery, where each layer of capability is introduced when the user is ready for it. This was not named by any stakeholder camp before the design made it visible.

**Performance in reality** — The Dancerace wireframe is the counter-example: a system designed for feature completeness rather than for how users behave under time pressure. The domain learning about debtor behaviour and the user hierarchy of needs are the positive examples.

**Constraint respecting** — Single interface for two meaningfully different users, within a genuine development capacity constraint. The progressive disclosure mechanism — highlights on the dashboard, depth available on click — worked within the constraint rather than against it.

**Domain learning** — The insight that debtors behave according to relationship management norms rather than formal business norms came from modelling real financial behaviour, not from asking users to describe their needs. The "accepted" invoice status is the specific design decision that came from this learning.
