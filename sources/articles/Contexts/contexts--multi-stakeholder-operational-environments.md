# `/contexts/multi-stakeholder-operational-environments`

---

## Domain vocabulary

- Multi-role workflows / role-differentiated interfaces
- Cognitive requirement by role — the specific information needs, time pressures, and mental models of each role; not preferences but structural requirements
- Physical environment by role — where the user is when using the system determines what the interface must do (front desk ambient multitasking; consultation room focused attention; field inspection; helm at speed)
- Error consequence by role — errors in the same system can mean different things for different roles; the design must acknowledge this hierarchy
- Handoff points — the moments where work or information passes between roles; where failures concentrate in multi-role systems
- Role-based access vs. shared context — the architectural question of what should be separated and what should be visible across roles
- Service design — the discipline of designing across organisational and role boundaries, not just within a single user journey
- Stakeholder governance — the internal problem of competing priorities among the people who commission and build the system, distinct from (but connected to) the user role problem. Has two failure poles: contested ownership (multiple owners pulling in different directions) and absent ownership (no one taking responsibility for decisions or understanding at all)
- Adoption density — in network-effect systems (WCO/IPM), low adoption by any stakeholder group degrades the value of the platform for all groups
- Downstream consumer — a stakeholder that consumes the platform's output rather than co-editing within it; not all parties in a multi-party system are screen-sharing co-users (Neugo: the state issuing authority consumes clean data through a pipeline rather than collaborating in the case interface)
- Value event — the moment a multi-party process produces the output the platform exists to create (a submittable case, a completed transaction); workflow design can drive the parties toward it rather than passively hosting their work
- The one-interface question — when to serve multiple roles through a single unified interface vs. when to recommend architectural separation

## Evidence to draw on

### WCO/IPM customs intelligence platform (cross-institutional, global scale)
- World Customs Organization customs intelligence platform — coordinates intellectual property enforcement between customs officers and rights holders across member administrations
- Three user groups: frontline inspection officers (ports, airports, land borders — speed, time pressure, field conditions), intelligence analysts (pattern analysis, historical cases), rights holder brand protection and legal teams (filing information, reviewing enforcement activity)
- Platform was already in production with low adoption when Creative Navy engaged. Workaround symptoms were present: parallel spreadsheets and email chains had emerged — the reliable diagnostic indicator of a system users have decided is more effort than it is worth.
- Network effect dynamic: low adoption by either officers or rights holders degrades the value for both sides. An intelligence platform only functions if both sides participate.
- Field conditions varied significantly across member states: unreliable connectivity, mixed device fleets, limited time per inspection
- Research included 47 usability participants across Italy, Romania, Uzbekistan, Algeria, and Spain — geographic spread confirming the multi-jurisdictional design held across genuinely different operational contexts
- **78% reduction in training costs for officers, based on reduced training hours. Client-reported.**
- **200% increase in rights holder user sign-ups. Client-reported.**
- **67% increase in platform use among rights holders; 20% increase among officers. Client-reported.**
- **107 governments signed up; 2000+ officers using the system in field operations. Client-reported milestone figures.**
- Jürgen Stock (Secretary General, INTERPOL) provided an external endorsement — available in case study file. The INTERPOL endorsement is notable because INTERPOL operates in the same enforcement ecosystem and has direct field visibility of platform performance.

### Triopsis workforce management (internal multi-role, measured outcomes)
- Three distinct roles with incompatible mental models: schedulers (speed, batch actions, team availability), operations managers (exception scanning, risk across broader time horizons), field technicians (task detail, safety measures, glove- and sunlight-tolerant confirmations)
- 47 microtasks mapped across 3 personas before any design decisions were made. For each: when performed, ease of discovery, ease of understanding, what the user needs, issues, opportunities, desired outcome, pain points, frequency, cognitive load, dependencies.
- The microtask analysis revealed that an improvement helping one role could create blind spots for another — a layout optimised for schedulers may hide signals managers depend on; a structure that reassures technicians may obscure timing data for planners. The conflicts were structural tensions in the organisation, not design errors.
- Multi-stakeholder governance on the internal side: founders, developers, sales, support, and key clients all had competing expectations; without a UX framework to arbitrate, decisions across modules were inconsistent
- All outcomes measured in product analytics (not usability testing):
  - **62% faster job discovery; 83% faster job sequence optimisation; 58% faster weekly planning.**
  - **Training: mandatory 1-hour session → optional 15-minute video; 90% self-onboarding. Client-reported.**
  - **"How can I" support tickets: fell to approximately 5% of previous volume. Client-reported.**
  - **Sales conversions multiplied by four; began winning clients 4–5x larger. Client-reported by CEO.**

### IDEXX Animana veterinary practice management (architectural role split recommendation)
- Eleven-year platform; 35 clinics, 150+ participants, 2 weeks, 3 countries
- Discovery finding: reception staff and clinical staff don't just have different preferences — they have fundamentally incompatible cognitive requirements
  - Receptionists: ambient awareness, sustained multitasking, multiple simultaneous demands, short task windows, errors visible immediately to clients. Cognitive requirement: breadth and speed.
  - Clinical staff: focused sequential case attention, one patient at a time, errors with clinical consequences. Cognitive requirement: depth and accuracy.
- These are not different preferences within the same task model. They are different mental models of what the software is for, activated in different physical environments, under different time structures, with different error consequence profiles.
- Conclusion from tension-driven reasoning: a unified interface optimised for one role degrades the other. No feature adjustment resolves this. The architecture itself must reflect the role split.
- **Recommendation: develop distinct UIs for reception and clinical roles.** This is the most architecturally significant conclusion in the multi-stakeholder cluster. Worth naming explicitly as a recommendation that emerged from evidence, not a preference.
- Client-reported 6 months post-engagement: recommendations are well-grounded; some implemented, remainder planned. Pace reflects internal velocity.

### Akrivia Health (cross-institutional governance complexity)
- Oxford University spin-off; clinical research platform serving NHS analysts (strict governance boundaries between research and operational use), academic researchers (ethics and data access approvals), pharmaceutical research staff (audit and reporting obligations)
- These three groups were not aggregated into a single "researcher" persona — the differences in institutional governance were the finding, not an inconvenience to smooth over
- Same information, different institutional obligations: what an NHS analyst can do with patient data differs from what an academic researcher can do, which differs from what a pharma team can do. The design must make those boundaries operational, not just stated in documentation.
- Governance reviewer tension: researchers need iterative hypothesis freedom; governance reviewers need to verify query logic independently without escalating to the researcher. This is the institutional version of the multi-role design problem.
- Client-reported outcome: governance reviewers could complete reviews without escalating to the research team. **State as client-reported.**

### Tetra/Prism (field and office, two-platform architecture)
- Property compliance — field-based property managers (mobile, average 12 properties each, monthly/weekly site inspections) and office-based directors/portfolio managers (web, reporting, oversight, compliance monitoring)
- The higher up in the organisational hierarchy, the more a user sees but the less they actively does — a fundamental role difference that shaped the entire architecture
- Field users had specific failure modes at engagement start: 10-minute load time on app launch (downloading entire portfolio offline), entity model inconsistency (tasks/actions/forms with unclear differentiation), dashboard surfacing the wrong priorities
- The mobile/web split is an architectural decision — the same data serves two genuinely different operational contexts that required different interaction models. Common entities (tasks, actions, properties) kept visually and structurally consistent across platforms; the interaction context adapted.
- **Mobile adoption: 12% → 64% one year after redesigned app launch. Client-measured.**
- **Web NPS: 72% → 85% approximately 4 months post-launch. Client-measured.**
- 2-year Implementation Partnership; 12 support tickets, average 1-hour response time

### Dancerace/Jacko (three-party commercial structure)
- Invoice management portal — financier activates supplier accounts; supplier issues invoices; debtor/customer receives and acts on them. All three parties interact through the same platform around the lifecycle of each invoice.
- The three-party structure was understood at the start; what was not specified were the interaction states — how each role does specific things, what states each interaction needs to have, how the supplier's experience connects to the debtor's.
- Key research insight: real debtors do not behave according to formal business norms. They delay, acknowledge without committing, and manage relationships informally. The design had to accommodate this rather than design against it — the "accepted" invoice status (symbolic gesture of good faith, not payment commitment) is the specific design decision that came from this domain learning.
- Stakeholder alignment challenge: two internal camps with conflicting positions. Resolution came not from persuasion but from a specific design concept (chasing routines as pre-built templates) that made visible that simplicity and feature depth are not opposites — they are a path through the same space. **This is the clearest example of stakeholder alignment through design in the portfolio.**
- **Demo-to-paying conversion: 36% vs. 15–20% industry benchmark. Client-reported, measured over 6 months post-launch.**

### Neugo (public-private multi-party case coordination; citizen–intermediary–state structure)
- UK visa application case-management platform. Coordinates four genuinely different parties around the lifecycle of a single case: visa seekers (members of the public, often travelling as a party rather than individually), advisers (legal consultants and family offices — commercial intermediaries with their own established tooling, frequently Excel), case workers (who move a case toward a submittable state), and the Home Office (the state issuing authority, which consumes Neugo's clean output as downstream data, where the visa decision is actually made).
- A distinct multi-party topology: not all stakeholders are screen-sharing co-users. The human collaboration happens among applicant, adviser, and case worker; the state participates as a downstream consumer of a data pipeline, not as a co-editor of the case. The design problem is producing clean, submittable output from a messy multi-party back-and-forth, not reconciling four roles on a single screen.
- The platform's value event is a submittable, complete case — not the open-ended process of preparing one. A specific workflow decision follows: the system actively prompts case workers on what to do next rather than waiting on initiative, driving the multi-party process toward the moment the platform earns its keep.
- Constraint respecting across the adviser firms: the platform absorbed the firms' existing tooling, integrating everything except a small number of fields unique to one single firm — serving the firms as a group without fragmenting into per-firm variants.
- **Stakeholder governance at the absent-ownership pole.** Where Dancerace shows multi-stakeholder governance as competing internal priorities resolved through design, Neugo shows the opposite pathology: no one taking ownership of decisions or of understanding the system at all — the nominal product manager functioned only as a conduit ("a sheet of glass," in the client's description). With no owner to align, design decisions were validated directly against the beneficiary firms at three checkpoints (see /method/iterative-system-building) rather than through an internal arbiter.
- **At a post-launch audit roughly a year after go-live, 15 legal firms were relying on the platform and had begun replacing some of their internal processes with its features. Creative Navy-observed during the audit.**
- **The engagement-1 prototype contributed to the system being commissioned: client-reported as roughly 30% of the decision factors, with dedicated demo sessions built around the prototype.**

### Industrial process control (structure the designer cannot fix — primary context)

- A European industrial automation vendor whose process control software coordinates machining cells. In development, not shipped; nothing here is an outcome. Observational items are phrased as observed, client-stated positions attributed to the client's framing.
- The core account, client-stated: there are no defined roles across customers — each company organises itself as it sees fit, and the product must adapt. Roles change: when a setup operator leaves, the preset operator must do both tasks, and will not accept two computers for it. Nobody knows which product components will exist in one, three, five or ten years. Customers configure the interface themselves: an administrator selects which components a group needs, and that selection generates the group's navigation. Five company archetypes were identified, from a single-operator shop to a large corporation with eleven operator groups across six components.
- Supporting: cell scale ranges from a single machine with no robot, where the operator does the robot's transport by hand, up to ten machines and twenty storage magazines. Operators hold strong ownership over their cell — they call it *my node* — and resist being instructed by software. A five-person team delivered 48 screens in the six-sprint block and 46 further during the retainer to date (engagement facts, not outcomes).
- **The argument:** where role definitions are unstable and the customer generates their own navigation, the designer cannot fix the information architecture — only design a chassis that stays coherent under every configuration a customer assembles. This is constraint respecting at the architectural level.

### Assemblio (the one-interface question answered in the second direction — primary context)

- Assemblio GmbH; the Assemblio Composer (planning tool used by engineers) and the Assemblio Smart Viewer (shop-floor application used by assembly workers). In production. **Assemblio's operating-context material is client-stated rather than observed, and every statement about the working environment is the client's description of it — never blend it with the industrial engagement's field observations, and keep the two engagements separate on this page: the industrial case is structure the designer cannot fix; the Assemblio case is structure the designer separates.**
- This lands on a slot the page already has open: the one-interface question — when to serve multiple roles through a single unified interface versus when to recommend architectural separation — has had no worked example of answering it in the second direction. Assemblio is that example.
- Two populations whose conditions are opposite in every dimension that matters, as the client described them. The Composer's user: the engineer, planner or head of assembly — *Arbeitsvorbereitung*, work preparation — at a desk with mouse, keyboard and desktop monitor. The Smart Viewer's user: the shop-floor assembly worker, in an environment that is bright (workers need light to see the parts), dirty and loud, standing, wearing gloves, at a work desk filled with tools, parts and paper documents; input devices are not guaranteed — no mouse and often nowhere to put one, sometimes a keyboard, some customers using foot pedals because the worker is standing with both hands occupied, usually a tablet on a holder or a monitor beside the workstation.
- **The cognitive-requirement-by-role material gains a structural case:** the attention budget is the binding constraint. Workers focus on what their hands are doing, not on the screen; the client's position is that the controls must be as simple as possible because the worker cannot spend cognitive resources on the viewer, and roughly 90% of the time the plan is consulted once and then left playing. **A role whose interface gets a tenth of its user's attention is a structural requirement, not a preference.**
- The separation: the Smart Viewer had been built from the Composer's components and looked almost identical to it, while the requirements for the two user groups differ. **It was separated from the Composer as its own interface — and the structural decision was the client's: they arrived at the engagement having already diagnosed that the two user groups needed different products (client-originated).**
- The role-specific content problem: the old viewer displayed the complete CAD hierarchy at all times — the engineer's structure — when what the worker needs is the parts used in the current step; the client's worked example was a step containing two visually similar types of screw, where the names in the list did not tell the worker which was which.
- The one-directional handoff and its answer: the worker had no way to send anything back to the planner — someone who discovers that a step does not work in practice had no route to say so. The answer: a comment attached to a specific assembly step, raisable directly from the 3D canvas as a floating input, with a Feedback tab in the side panel collecting them. The feature and its step-level binding were both the client's — their own customers had asked for it (client-originated); Creative Navy designed how it lives in the interface, raised from the canvas rather than from a menu, so the worker reports from the thing they are looking at.

### Oceaneering hospital dispatch panel (resolved by configuration rather than consensus — primary context)

- Oceaneering may be named; nothing else may. Client and customer speech is reported, never quoted.
- The full stakeholder account: an interface serving operators, supervisors, systems and applications engineers, sales staff and two hospital customers whose requirements conflict, with an external consultant layer between vendor and hospital — and **no agreed business requirements at the start**. The client project lead's account: there were no concrete business requirements; what existed was study of the deployed panels, the tool built in-house for one hospital, and conversations with sales, service and subject-matter experts.
- The conflict, concretely: whether supervisors may change station roles at all is site-dependent. At one site they may not, and the facility has run the system for fourteen years as a fine-tuned machine in which nothing is changed. At another, roles must change through the day, because wash-area stations send clean dishes around noon and receive dirty ones an hour later and there is not enough floor space for separate stations. Area sizes vary from four stations to a fully automated bay of fifty with no panel at all.
- The resolution: a configurable chassis. The client's stated want, reported: screens configurable per customer site, with nothing hard-coded, so an application engineer could arrive at a site, add an area, set up the card configuration, save it, and have it available for others to use. The card system carries it: station card standards specified numerically — minimum card 110 px, normal spacing 24 px, minimum spacing 3 px where two dispatch-ready cards adjoin, 20 px of growth when a cart is ready to dispatch, four card sizes so one layout system serves areas from four stations to fifty. **The same feature is essential at one site and forbidden at another. This is the page's strongest evidence that multi-stakeholder environments are resolved by configuration rather than consensus.**

### KISPE Space (shared semantics, different presentations)

- KISPE Space, a UK space systems engineering company. No quotation marks and no reported speech on any page carrying KISPE; positions are attributed to the role and the document.
- Three user populations, named in the client's own brief: field commanders and personnel who need concise, location-specific information under time pressure; operations and planning staff who need a wider view, historical context, forecasts and access to supporting evidence; and specialist support personnel who investigate sources, service limitations and information requests.
- **Resolved as shared semantics with different presentations — neither three products nor one compromise.** A dedicated field presentation was chosen over a responsive collapse of the desktop layout (which keeps complex controls too dense for field touch use, with essential freshness information liable to end up under a collapsed panel) and over user-configured panels (which let users hide significant information without noticing). The chosen direction prioritises map orientation, compact source status and a single expanded item, on the same data meanings as the desktop. The governing rule: different operating conditions justify different arrangements, not different meanings. The accepted cost was named — a dedicated view increases delivery and validation effort — and broad configuration was postponed until task patterns are understood.

### Schone Airspace Group (roles as responsibilities, one persistent shell)

- Schone Airspace Group; no quotation marks and no reported speech on any page carrying Schone.
- Five operator role contexts were modelled — remote pilot, payload operator, mission planner, fleet supervising operator, operations reviewer — described as responsibilities within an operation rather than as a fixed staffing configuration, because one person may hold several on a small flight and a larger operation may distribute them.
- **The resolution is unusual and is the contribution:** a single persistent command shell with fixed positions for command target identity, last reported flight mode, battery, control link state, telemetry freshness and consequential actions — carrying the same meanings for every role, with the task canvas changing beneath it. Workspace changes alter the centre content, never the location or meaning of the command region. Rejected: a per-screen toolbar, which lets a camera or fleet layout move the same command somewhere else, and a hidden safety drawer, which adds a search step at the moment the operator needs to act. The accepted cost was named: the shell reserves screen space that cannot be given to the map or the video.

### Handheld command-and-control (coordination that happens outside the system)

- A European manufacturer of uncrewed systems, including one-way effectors, and the tactical command-and-control software that operates them. No quotation marks and no reported speech on any page carrying this engagement.
- The three-population structure with three device classes, named in the client's brief: tactical operators working dismounted in the field under stress on a ruggedised handheld; mounted operators working from inside a vehicle on an integrated station with touch and keyboard and mouse; and commanders overseeing operations from a command post on a laptop or large screens.
- **The contribution the page does not have: a system whose most critical coordination happens outside itself.** The persona work identified a cross-cutting condition the brief had not: across all three populations the decisive exchanges are verbal or by radio, so the interface must coexist with a conversation it cannot see — glanceable and interruptible at every state, never heads-down.
- **The honest methodological note:** the persona work also challenged the client's own grouping of its users, observing that the mounted operator's workflow is closer to the commander's than to the dismounted operator's in parallel asset management and information density, and that the split deserved a second look. That is what persona work is for, and the page's existing entries do not contain an instance of it. The personas were labelled speculative by their own authors and must be described as such.

### Maritime traffic monitoring (handover as an accountable transfer — a named subsection)

- A European vendor of maritime traffic monitoring, inland navigation and communications software for port authorities, coastal administrations and waterway operators. No quotation marks and no reported speech.
- The role and authority structure: six stakeholder perspectives at discovery — product strategy, VTS, communications, RIS, frontend architecture and commercial — and four operator-facing roles in the reference product: operator, shift supervisor, technical staff, and product and engineering owners, **each with a different decision set, a different information requirement and a different boundary on what it may conclude.** Seven operator journeys were modelled — among them supervisor and shift handover — each decomposed into subtasks, decisions, visible states, permitted actions and recoverable interruptions, with normal, ambiguous and degraded conditions modelled separately.
- **The distinctive contribution — handover as an accountable transfer rather than a digest, worth its own subsection on the page.** A chronological activity list was rejected for not distinguishing open from resolved work. **Automatic ownership reassignment was rejected for assuming a successful transfer of responsibility without an explicit review point.** The selected model groups open alerts, source concerns and incident tasks with owner, latest attributable event and unresolved status, and requires the receiving operator to review and accept. The page's existing entries concern roles sharing a system; this concerns **responsibility moving between people at a defined moment**, which is a different problem.

### Topera (a designed structure for a multi-party environment — designed-for, not observed)

- Topera, a cross-organisational project management platform, exists only as a concept design; nothing was built, nothing was watched in use, and there was no user research of any kind. **The page's existing entries describe environments that exist; this one describes a structure designed for an environment, and the difference must be visible in the sentence — "was designed to", never "does".**
- The substance: external partners were specified to see a deliberately shallower system — three to four structural levels exposed, asynchronously, with no direct edit permissions, while internal users see full depth. The same objects carry two legitimate views at different resolutions, and the boundary between them is a designed object rather than a permissions setting.
- Work crosses that boundary with constraint caps attached: one party creates an initiative and assigns it to another with maximum duration, maximum effort and maximum cost attached; the receiving party sees only the assigned sub-initiative and its constraints, and may decompose further within them, with the system specified to warn, but not prevent, when decomposition passes four levels; the final structure is a stitched tree with aggregated values.
- The vocabulary the standards give this: the ProSTEP iViP CPM recommendations supply agreed cross-partner milestones, bilateral synchronisation points, interaction chains of chronological review points, and a role-to-role communication matrix — and explicitly exclude project management within the participating enterprises; they govern only the interface between companies.

### Egma (two layers: the client's five functions, and the chain → store → operator hierarchy)

- Egma may be named; the product is Egma Order, Egma's B2B lens ordering platform. Built and online; nothing about its performance was measured.
- **Inside the client:** five functions with five different accounts of the same failure — the CEO's revenue diagnosis, the product manager's navigation and support-load account, the marketing manager's premium-discovery and journey account, the development team's structure account, and the support team's order-on-their-behalf account — **and the disagreement is the finding.** Attribute each to its function; none is a measured result.
- **Outside:** a chain → store → operator hierarchy in which operators continue each other's drafts and switch without re-authenticating, while individual accountability is reattached at the two committing actions. Store operators work under rush-hour conditions and continue each other's orders; the login model Egma specified reflects this — a store manager logs the store in, operators switch between users within the store without re-entering a password, and the store's view is unchanged. A five-digit personal code, issued through the chain's IT manager, is required when an order is placed; Creative Navy extended the code to ticket submission as well and designed the masked and unmasked entry states. Accountability is reattached at exactly the two committing actions, rather than traded away for speed or bought at the cost of constant re-authentication. **Egma specified the store-login and operator-code model; mark the authorship.**

## What this page must not claim

- **Keep the industrial and Assemblio engagements separate on the page** — one is structure the designer cannot fix, the other is structure the designer separates — and never let Assemblio's client-stated operating context blend with the industrial engagement's field observations, which are the real thing.
- **Mark Assemblio's authorship:** the Smart Viewer separation and the feedback loop with its step-level binding were the client's decisions. No time-saving, error-rate, training, adoption, revenue or funding figure from Assemblio, including its own marketing claims; no shop-floor observation claim; no dates.
- **No efficiency, throughput, error-rate, time-saving or dispatch-volume outcome from Oceaneering** — nothing was measured, no operator was interviewed and no depot was visited; its validation sessions were design reviews, not usability tests, and no dispatch operator took part. Every Oceaneering passage is reported speech, never quoted.
- **Write Topera as designed-for, not observed**, with no outcome, no user, no stakeholder named, and no implication the system exists.
- **No post-launch measurement from Egma**, no revenue or support-ticket outcome, no usability-testing result, and no quoted speech placed in an outcome register — G20 is the CEO's diagnosis of his own business.
- **No KISPE validation claim** — nothing was tested with users, and the field presentation is a design proposal whose touch dimensions, legibility and glove behaviour were never evaluated on real devices.
- **Schone's roles material describes what was designed** — no claim that the shell produced a result, and its research entered late as validation and correction by nine participants, qualitative only.
- **The handheld C2 personas are speculative by their own authors' label**; Creative Navy has no primary field evidence on that engagement, and the operator behaviours its review reports are the client's observations relayed to Creative Navy.
- **The maritime VTS engagement's handover model is a design, not a measured operational improvement**; no production, training or commercial outcome exists, and the comparative study's figures do not belong in this page's material.
- **No engagement dates anywhere** — sequence and duration only.
