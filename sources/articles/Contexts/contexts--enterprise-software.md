## Evidence to draw on

### Triopsis workforce management SaaS (most measurement-rich enterprise case)
- Workforce management for utilities and road maintenance — schedulers, operations managers, field technicians coordinating thousands of interventions per week
- 47 microtasks mapped across 3 personas before any redesign decisions were made
- 43 user interviews, 21 participants; 3 in-situ observation sessions under real-time pressure (weather incidents, crew shortages, overlapping jobs); 5 stakeholder interviews
- **62% faster job discovery; 83% faster job sequence optimisation; 58% faster weekly planning. All three measured in product analytics from real users in the live system — not usability testing task times, not estimates.**
- **Training: mandatory 1-hour remote session → optional 15-minute video; approximately 90% of users self-onboarding. Client-reported operational data.**
- **Support ticket volume: "how can I" questions fell to approximately 5% of previous volume. Client-reported.**
- **Sales conversions multiplied by four; company began winning clients 4–5x larger than before. Client-reported by CEO.**
- **Tender scores improved by 10–20% attributable to design quality, depending on tender weighting criteria. Client-reported from formal tender evaluation documents.**
- 68-component design system, 200+ documented states, 15 workflow types — built as reasoning documentation
- 2-year Implementation Partnership after delivery — designers present for the full growth phase

### Gexcon CFD simulation software (expert enterprise, user base expansion)
- Computational fluid dynamics simulation for industrial safety engineering — gas dispersion, explosion risk, facility safety validation
- Brief at engagement start: extend product life by 25 years; retain scientific rigour; open access to newer engineers and non-technical roles (risk managers, safety analysts)
- 102 individual tasks documented; 24 user interviews; 23 workplace observations; 9 stakeholder interviews; 12 competitors benchmarked; 45 design variants across 10 key challenges; 37 evaluation sessions
- **Time to first successful simulation: 4 days → 6 hours. Measured by Gexcon in real deployments.**
- **Configuration errors per simulation: 5–8 → 1–2. Measured by Gexcon.**
- **Corrective load per error: 4–6 hours → approximately 20 minutes. Measured by Gexcon.**
- **Active users per team: 1 → 3–4. Client-reported.**
- **Training: 3-day instructor-led events → short webinars and video materials. Operational change.**
- 3-year total engagement (4 weeks research, 6 weeks option space mapping, 7 months execution, 2-year Implementation Partnership)
- Franz Zdravistch, Chief Training Engineer: attributed the team's self-directed domain learning speed as exceptional even by expert standards — direct quote available in case study file, confirms depth of domain immersion

### Polymatica analytics platform (capability democratisation, international expansion)
- GPU-backed OLAP analytics platform — technically superior but required personal training from the founder to use
- Before redesign: **2% of users completed key operations independently without help documentation.** After release 1: 40%. After release 2: **56%. Measured via product analytics.**
- Training model: Roman (founder) delivered personal training for every new customer → users could operate independently post-redesign
- **International expansion to UK, US, Germany became possible** — previously blocked because Roman's English was limited and he spoke no German. Client-reported.
- HSBC and Barclays came on as UK clients post-redesign — client-reported. At their data volumes, the GPU performance advantage (50–100x faster than competitors) became experientially perceptible for the first time.
- 8-month design engagement + 2-year Implementation Partnership; 67 support requests, 2-hour average response time

### Chemical Watch compliance intelligence platform (subscription price and exit)
- Compliance intelligence for chemical regulation professionals — REACH, SVHC, GHS/CLP, TSCA, multi-jurisdictional coverage
- Transformation from news publication to compliance intelligence platform
- **Subscription price tripled following the platform redesign. Client-reported as direct outcome.**
- **24x EBITDA exit multiple one year after launch. Client-reported; the design's role is in the causal chain: design → user adoption of features → justified price increase → revenue → valuation. Frame this chain explicitly, not as "the design caused the exit."**
- Google cited as one of Chemical Watch's enterprise subscribers; a representative described it as the best usability they had seen in a while — reported in a client meeting, not in writing, so cannot be quoted directly
- Engagement: 6 months design, 5 support requests over 6 months post-handover (minimal handover friction)

### Dancerace / Jacko invoice management (conversion rate)
- B2B invoice management and accounts receivable portal — three-party structure (financier, supplier, debtor)
- Greenfield product; 5-month engagement across all five phases
- **Demo-to-paying conversion: 36%. Industry benchmark at time of launch: 15–20%. Dancerace's own expectation was in the benchmark range. Client-reported, measured over 6 months post-launch.**
- The 36% figure is the result of designing for the user's hierarchy of needs (what do I owe, what is my cost, what must I do now) rather than the stakeholder's desire to showcase premium features — this mechanism should be explained, not just the number cited

### Pixelart Fugo digital signage CMS (NPS and revenue)
- Web CMS for managing digital signage content across locations — advertising agencies, hospitality, convention centres
- **NPS: 57% → 89%. Client-measured: survey of all existing users, single measurement before engagement and approximately 2 months after redesigned platform launched.**
- **Revenue doubled in the two years following launch. Client-reported directly. The redesign was part of a broader growth phase that also included rebranding — frame as a contributing factor, not sole cause.**
- 2-year Implementation Partnership structured as monthly developer show-and-tell sessions — confidence support for a development team unfamiliar with the new design system

### IDEXX Animana veterinary practice management (audit scope)
- 11-year-old platform; 35 clinics, 150+ participants, 2 weeks, 3 countries (Netherlands, UK, Germany)
- Central discovery: reception staff and clinical staff have fundamentally incompatible cognitive requirements — receptionists need ambient multitasking, clinicians need focused sequential attention. Standard unified interfaces degrade both.
- 100+ recommendations structured for development ticket translation
- 5-year product vision delivered
- Client-reported 6 months post-engagement: recommendations are well-grounded, some already implemented, remainder planned. Pace reflects internal organisational velocity, not issues with the recommendations.
- Demonstrates Creative Navy's audit capability — the engagement was research and vision, not full design execution

### Tetra / Prism property compliance (mobile adoption, NPS)
- Property compliance and management — field-based property managers (mobile) and office-based directors/portfolio managers (web)
- At engagement start: **12% of users who should have been using the mobile app were doing so.** One year after launch: **64%. Client-measured.**
- **Web NPS: 72% → 85%. Client-measured, approximately 4 months post-launch.**
- Key mobile problem: app downloaded entire portfolio on launch (up to 10-minute load time) — design solution was a property selection flow at launch limiting download to that day's properties. Architecture problem solved through design, not engineering.
- 2-year Implementation Partnership: 12 support tickets total, average 1-hour response time

### Enhesa legal compliance platform (NPS with clean attribution, training burden signal)
- Web-based legal compliance intelligence platform serving regulatory affairs managers, compliance officers, and legal teams. Users navigate dense legislative content across jurisdictions — baseline regulation pages aggregating legislative text, implementation timelines, requirements, and changes. Primary users are domain experts; approximately 20% are not expert users of the platform.
- Engagement covered three platform areas: baseline regulation pages (primary content architecture), dashboard (customisable landing page with multiple data visualisations), and account settings and onboarding.
- Research method: **95 session recordings reviewed; 31 workarounds documented across four categories** (Finding legal texts: 8; Using legal texts: 17; Account settings: 2; Other: 4); **12 error types documented with time-to-redress measurement.** 6 user interviews. The concentration of 17 workarounds in the primary content area was the diagnostic signal: the highest density of compensating behaviour was at the platform's core function.
- Design operated within an existing Enhesa design system — no changes to colours, typography, or existing components. **7 new components added** to address problems the existing system could not solve. Implementations timeline required 5 iteration rounds to find a solution satisfying both client requirements and design system constraints simultaneously.
- Design education delivered as a promised deliverable at every presentation — each review included explicit explanation of user behaviour evidence informing design decisions, enabling stakeholders to evaluate options on evidential rather than aesthetic grounds.
- **NPS: 68% → 84%, measured two months post-launch. Enhesa confirmed no other changes were made to the product between the 68% baseline and the 84% post-launch measurement — clean attribution to the design change specifically. Client-measured across full user base.** The 68% baseline, measured two months before the engagement began, was one of the factors that prompted Enhesa to seek design help.
- **NPS at two years: 87%. Client-measured. Subsequent product changes were made after the 84% measurement — the two-year figure is not attributable to the design work in isolation.**
- **Training burden signal: 45% of pre-redesign users sought training videos; 81% of those said the videos were not helpful. Only 21% of post-redesign users sought training videos.** Enhesa's stated mechanism: the redesigned platform was sufficiently self-explanatory that users no longer needed supplementary help. The 81% unhelpfulness rate confirms the training programme was not compensating for what the interface was failing to provide. Both figures from the same NPS survey. Pre/post comparison is across cohorts, not a longitudinal panel.
- 80% of documented workarounds addressed in the redesign. Error outcomes: 3 error types made structurally impossible, 5 made easier to recover from, 6 expected to reduce in frequency (inferred from design decisions; not independently measured post-deployment).
- One year of Implementation Partnership following design delivery.
- Evidence calibration: NPS figures and training video data client-measured by Enhesa. Clean attribution window confirmed by Enhesa. Post-redesign error outcomes are inferred, not independently measured.

### Egma Order B2B lens ordering platform (the page's largest single gain)

- Egma may be named in full, and so may the product: **Egma Order**, Egma's B2B lens ordering platform, through which optical stores order lenses. Egma is a Dubai-based ophthalmic lens manufacturer and optical supplier operating across the Gulf states and North Africa through four divisions — ophthalmic lenses, eyewear, instruments and a lens factory. The platform is **built and online — and nothing about its performance was measured.** No post-launch figure of any kind. Individuals are not named; role descriptors only ("Egma's CEO", "Egma's product manager", "Egma's support team").
- This page's existing vocabulary already names support-ticket volume as an evidence category, design-system constraint and component governance, multi-stakeholder governance and feature accumulation — **Egma is load-bearing on all four.**
- **The "before" material is the strongest the page will hold**, because it is a documented case of feature accumulation with a named mechanism. The development team's account: improvement requests arrived as requests to add buttons and content; there was very little structure, and new functionality tended to add chaos rather than clarity. The accumulated results, observed in the legacy configurator: mandatory inputs styled so they looked like error signalling, with some behind extra clicks; future order steps blurred rather than hidden — legible enough to read, so they added cognitive load while serving no purpose; a double-layer navigation added to compensate for users struggling to discover how to start each of the four order types, which made navigation more cumbersome rather than less; input fields and buttons sharing a visual style, so the whole order form read as blue and white; and the entire configuration — twelve or more parameter groups — presented on a single screen at once, with no indication of which choices governed which others.
- **The five-function stakeholder picture as a worked example of multi-stakeholder governance producing five incompatible accounts of one failure.** Internal stakeholder interviews across five functions each surfaced a different diagnosis, and the discrepancies are findings in their own right. The CEO: the configurator was restraining revenue because many customers did not appear to find all available configurations and looked elsewhere — **his diagnosis of his own business, not a measured finding.** The product manager: users found it hard to navigate previous orders and place repeat orders, the development team found it hard to add features without adding confusion, and the support team was overworked. The marketing manager: most customers never seemed to discover the premium options, there was no obvious branding, and no sense of a user journey. The development team: structure was the problem. The support team: customers frequently raised tickets asking that orders be placed on their behalf rather than use the forms. **Attribute each account to its function; none may appear in an outcome register.**
- **Four order types under one chassis:** RX (a prescription order for a named patient, which goes into production), Stock (finished lenses, no production time), Bulk Stock (store-level replenishment) and Services (fitting, tinting and coating applied to a lens Egma did not make) — plus Home, the Order Center, Get Help (ticketing) and Master (users, stores, roles, components, permissions, campaigns), across the web platform, a native mobile application and the administration area.
- **The chain → store → operator permission model, with roles composed from components:** an administrator creates a role, selects which interface components that role may reach, and sets the access level per component — no access, view, or edit. Permissions are the link between a role and a component; a bulk control sets every permission for a component at once, with individual toggles for exceptions. Different administrator types see different subsets: a chain manager's administration menu carries users and stores only, while a super administrator additionally has roles and campaigns.
- **The dependency-constrained order form and the four moves that answer it.** Availability as the primary mechanism: the form discloses progressively by dependency — the product, focal type, technology, index, coating and sun protection column is inert until a fitting type has been chosen, and thereafter only producible options are offered, with unavailable options hidden rather than shown disabled. An explicit account of absence: because hiding options destroys discoverability — and discoverability was the CEO's stated revenue problem — the design had to be able to say why something was not there, hiding options behind an info mechanism that tells the user why they are unavailable and what they would need to do to have them. The whole order stays visible while the user works: a multi-step wizard on separate screens was considered and rejected, because every choice limits the next and users need to check previous selections constantly; the trade-off was documented in Creative Navy's own words — it makes the order screen look more intricate, but it supports the user needs the research established. And cascade invalidation, warned before it happens: editing a completed earlier section states, before the change is committed, that all subsequent sections will be emptied, and offers apply-change or discard.
- **The three-layer order-tracking disclosure, proposed by the client and resolved by Creative Navy.** Egma's revision document set out the problem and proposed the mechanism — headline states, current sub-status on hover, full breakdown on click. The delivered design keeps the disclosure mechanism and changes the headline set: three groups, not four — Order Processed, In Production, Delivery — spanning eleven production steps, with the Orders area itself split into three tabs (drafts, in progress, complete). **Mark the authorship as the split it was.**
- **The table cell built to absorb every content type a table needs:** table content on this platform is unusually varied, so the table cell was built as a single component absorbing header cells, plain text, clickable tags, input fields, image thumbnails, icon buttons, checkboxes and toggles, each with default, hover and selected states — which is what makes the table variants across the platform cost almost nothing to produce. Supporting behaviours: a frozen first column with horizontal scroll, drag-to-resize columns, show/hide columns, card-view switching, pagination, and a bulk-delete mode with select-all and a sticky action bar.

### Assemblio Composer platform structure (deliberately secondary here)

- Assemblio GmbH, a Stuttgart software company and Fraunhofer IPA spin-off; the products are the Assemblio Composer and the Assemblio Smart Viewer. The product is in production. **Keep this entry short — Assemblio's load-bearing problem is not enterprise architecture.**
- Content hierarchy: folders > projects > products > versions — a project is a product family, a folder groups projects, typically by customer; one product carries several versions.
- Product states — Draft, In Review, Approved — with one owner at a time permitted to edit, the client's stated reason being conformance to company standards; while a product is in review, "Open assembly plan" becomes "View assembly plan", signalling read-only access (client-requested).
- Notifications as a panel with tabs for new and read, a third tab scoped to the current project when the user is inside one, and settings for what to be notified about and through which channel (tabs and project tab client-requested).
- A single reusable table component for list views across the application, parameterised through URL query parameters — the client's own architectural intent, stated in their feedback and adopted into the design.
- Upload handled as a placeholder card with a cancel action, not a modal and not a snack bar, because files can exceed 1 GB and several can process in parallel — progress belongs on the card for the product it concerns. Creative Navy's design.

### Topera (structural example only — guard the page)

- Topera, a cross-organisational project management platform, exists only as a concept design; nothing has been built and no outcome of any kind may be claimed. Its primary context is emerging products, and it belongs here only as a structural example.
- The structure worth citing: a two-axis information architecture — a time axis carrying the five DIN 69901-2 lifecycle phases and a knowledge axis carrying eleven management domains — in which **process-based and domain-based navigation were designed as co-equal routes rather than one primary and one fallback**, with the Work Breakdown Structure view designed to be entered from either.

### Maritime traffic software portfolio (fragmentation at vendor scale)

- A European vendor of maritime traffic monitoring, inland navigation and communications software for port authorities, coastal administrations and waterway operators. An also-relevant entry of one or two paragraphs — the primary context is expert tools, and if this entry starts to carry the design argument it has drifted. No quotation marks and no reported speech on any page carrying this engagement.
- The contribution is the portfolio condition: nine products across four families — Vessel Traffic Services (VTS), River Information Services (RIS), Voice Communication System (VCS) and Integrated Communication System (ICS) — built by separate engineering teams, whose interfaces had been designed by the software engineers who built them, extended per customer contract and tender requirement over many years, and sold increasingly as a suite, with no UX programme ever commissioned across the portfolio. **That is the enterprise-software fragmentation pattern at vendor scale rather than at product scale, and the page has no other instance of it.** The six RIS modules are described generically and never listed as an inventory.

## What this page must not claim

- **No post-launch measurement from Egma, of any kind.** The platform is online and nothing about it was measured — no support-ticket volume, no order-completion rate, no error rate, no average-order-value movement, no adoption figure, no time on task. The before-state is described in commercial terms by Egma's own executives and there is no "after" of any kind; a reader will expect one and must not be given one. No revenue, order-value, conversion or sales outcome — the CEO's and marketing manager's accounts are problem statements, and improving discovery of premium options was a design intention. No claim that Egma's support tickets fell.
- **No competitor benchmarking from Egma** — the client's kickoff answer was four words and nothing was tested against it. No usability-testing result — the wireframe sessions tested wireframes during design; the live platform was never tested. No regulatory or standards credential. **No capability-democratisation framing** — the platform was designed for an expert population at the client's explicit insistence; the burden that fell was the support team's, not the users'. Egma has no option-space counts at all, and no other engagement's counts or testing figures may drift onto it.
- **Egma's suppliers and partners are not Creative Navy's associations** — name a brand only where a design decision cannot be described without it. Personal names in Egma's design files are placeholder data.
- **No time-saving, error-rate, training, adoption, revenue or funding figure from Assemblio** — including Assemblio's own published marketing claims. Assemblio's customers are not named, and the Bosch, ABB, Emerson and Rosemount names in its design files are placeholder data, not clients. No AI framing for Assemblio. No dates — Assemblio's founding, funding and research provenance are publicly dated and none of it may appear.
- **No outcome, research finding, user insight or validation of any kind from Topera**, and no implication it exists as a working system.
- **From the maritime portfolio engagement:** no production, efficiency, engineering-effort or duplicated-development saving (the measurement framework was built and never reported back on); no tender, sales, demonstration or procurement result (the client's own commercial director recorded that no reliable measurement connects visual change to tender wins); no implication that nine products were redesigned — nine were audited, one was designed to full depth; and no "shipped as designed" — no design-fidelity comparison is recorded.
- **No engagement dates anywhere** — sequence and duration only.
