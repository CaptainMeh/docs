## Evidence to draw on

### Elsner Elektronik / Cala Touch KNX (consumer embedded, smart home)
- 4-inch round TFT LCD at 480×480px (designed conservatively at 320×240px first, scaled when panel confirmed — a deliberate risk-reduction decision)
- Wall-mounted at 140cm; designed for standing use, varying luminance including low-light bedroom conditions
- KNX building automation ecosystem — device is configured by systems engineers through ETS, operated daily by occupants who have no configuration access
- 54 countries, validated through dealer networks across 10 countries
- Hardware constraints disclosed at kickoff as absolute: laggy touch layer, microcontroller limits on programmable symbols, restricted colour depth and voltage — all treated as design parameters, not problems to overcome
- 6 navigation architectures explored before convergence on hamburger-plus-dashboard model replacing swipe-only carousel
- Research programme: 20-user survey (run by client), ergonomics research (Colle & Hiszem 2004 on touch targets — minimum 13mm, accuracy plateaus at ~22mm), 12-subject formal usability testing, 30-dealer prototype reviews across 10 countries
- **30 of 130 dealers rated the redesign as an improvement — 100% of those who responded. Client-reported, should be framed as such.**
- Client outcome: Elsner's product managers iterate the UI independently post-engagement. Evidence: observed/confirmed by client at close.
- Animation timing aligned with firmware update intervals so visual changes never drift out of sync with thermal values — specific engineering constraint resolved through direct collaboration

### Torqeedo maritime HMI (vessel control, hybrid propulsion)
- 10-inch embedded display, limited pixel density; 27 screens across 4 operational modes
- Domain: hybrid electric vessel control integrating propulsion motors, generators, battery banks (40–200 kWh), conversion units, auxiliary loads
- Scales from ~6m craft to 55m commercial ships
- Research: 12 sea trials over 6 months; 15 professional captains; temperature range −5°C to +35°C; night operations; vibration, sharp vessel movement, glare from cold water, rain, gloved interaction
- **50% faster energy state identification (new vs. legacy interface) — controlled environment experiment, 24 subjects. Directly measured.**
- **Glance reduction during manoeuvres — measured via eye tracking, 7 subjects, sea trials.**
- **All 15 captains reported the new interface as significantly better — participant-reported, structured feedback.**
- Yamaha Motor Co. acquired Torqeedo following the engagement; CEO reported the interface strengthened competitive position. Causal link between interface and acquisition is inferred, not documented — frame carefully.
- Key design problem: propulsion sensors, batteries, and generators update at different rates. Resolution was a grid structure that synchronises the different cadences into a unified display — captains perceive one system, not three competing signals.

### Cox Marine cluster displays (marine diesel, professional vessels)

### Stromer e-bike (consumer vehicle display, multi-surface system)
- Three-surface system: embedded Display L (with and without integrated camera variant), Display S, mobile companion app, web account — each surface with distinct hardware constraints and interaction models requiring coherent cross-surface design
- EU regulatory framework: EN 15194:2017 (European standard for electrically power-assisted cycles) governs warning device symbology, colour conventions, and marking requirements for the embedded display. This is a hard regulatory constraint, not a preference — colour conventions for warning states are specified by the standard, creating design tensions that must be resolved within the standard's permitted space
- Operating context: the embedded display is used during riding. The rider's eyes leave the road in brief glances — the same attentional profile as a driver glancing at an instrument cluster. Glance duration is therefore a safety-relevant design metric, not only a usability one
- **Average glance duration before redesign: 4.32 seconds — more than twice the 2-second threshold established by road safety research (Klauer et al., 2006, NHTSA Report No. DOT HS 810 594; NHTSA Driver Distraction Guidelines Phase 1, 2013; ISO 15007:2020). Creative Navy-measured via eye tracking during actual riding, 5 participants, real routes in Munich. Note: these standards are formally defined for four-wheeled vehicles; the threshold and principle apply directly to embedded displays used during riding.**
- **Average glance duration after redesign: 1.89 seconds — within the safe threshold. Glance frequency per kilometre reduced by 18%. Creative Navy-measured, same methodology and routes.**
- The core embedded design problem was warning architecture. A previous agency had established screen layout and interaction logic first, then added warnings as visual overlays. Warnings had no native structural relationship to the surface — they covered content, interfered with interactions, and appeared without contextual grounding. Creative Navy rebuilt the warning architecture from scratch: rules and principles governing how warnings, overlays, and interruptive elements relate to the screen structure across all states, established before component design began.
- Engagement scope: 42 distinct design topics across three surfaces over 14 months. Specific documented iteration counts: smartlock (5), firmware update (6), tire pressure sensor (4).
- Firmware update flow as an embedded design challenge: the flow spans both embedded display and mobile app, with technically complex preconditions (install only at 0 km/h, component-level updates distinct from system updates, background download requiring low visual prominence). The download/install distinction required resolving across two surfaces with different interaction models — 6 iterations before convergence.
- Animation deliverables: Lottie files for all screens covered. Animation work was approximately 10% of engagement effort. Key deliverables included: unlocked-to-ride-screen transition (each element animated independently), the signature "bike with cable" animation (appearing on both embedded and mobile), boot sequence, PIN feedback states, and an animation pattern framework (fullscreen modal, toast, partial vs. fullscreen overlay).
- **App store rating before engagement: 3.2. After relaunch: 4.1. Publicly verifiable.**
- Evidence label for glance data: Creative Navy-measured in real riding conditions. Evidence label for app store rating: publicly verifiable.

### CDR Foodlab chemical analysis instrument (laboratory analytical instrumentation)
- Portable chemical analysis instrument; 7-inch capacitive touchscreen, 5-point touch, 1024×600px. Bench-use in laboratory settings — physically constrained display for a device operated at close range on a laboratory workbench.
- Product family: FoodLab, BeerLab, WineLab, GalvanLab, and others. Each variant performs different analyses on different matrices (water, wine, beer, food products, galvanic solutions). The UX architecture is universal across the family; variants differ in which analysis steps are available (some steps are removed for variants with fewer parameters) and in colour scheme. This is a variant-coherent design system: one architecture serving multiple products without per-variant redesign.
- Users are non-specialist laboratory operators — professionals with basic chemistry training who understand how to follow an analysis protocol but do not have deep domain knowledge. Specifically: they do not understand what their procedural deviations mean chemically, or how errors affect measurement validity. This is a material design constraint: the interface cannot assume that users will correctly interpret ambiguous states or incomplete guidance. Instruction must be explicit; consequences of deviation cannot be left to domain knowledge the user does not have.
- Research: 13 remote user interviews. Five workarounds documented — each catalogued with the need it served, the outcome users were solving for, how users performed it, and estimated error or time-addition risk. One example: users printed paper lists of sample names and placed them beside the machine because naming samples through the interface was more cognitively demanding than maintaining a physical reference. **Research limitation: all interviews were remote. Physical interaction with the device during analysis (placing cuvettes, reading reagents, dispensing) could not be directly observed. This is a known limitation and should be stated when citing research findings that depend on physical interaction context.**
- **All five workarounds eliminated — user-reported confirmation. Not re-tested under controlled conditions. State as: users reported the workarounds were no longer necessary.**
- Previous agency context: a prior agency delivered a redesign that was never deployed. The client described the failure as a formal beautification — aesthetic treatment applied without product understanding. The client did not show the previous work to users. **Do not name or describe the previous agency beyond this framing.**
- Iteration record (option space mapping): dashboard 4 iterations, main navigation 3, working lists (design) 10, run multiple analyses 10, single analysis 4, history 3, tutorials 3, alarms 2. The working lists flow received the highest iteration count in the project — new feature with no existing precedent, 7-inch constrained screen, tensions between client requirements and UX feasibility that required the iteration process itself to resolve.
- Inline tutorial animations: the engagement replaced the existing static pictogram guidance system with inline step-by-step animations delivered as part of the design system. These are functional interface components — they appear within the analysis flow to guide users through physical sub-steps — not decorative elements. Delivered as part of the design system deliverable.
- Implementation Partnership: 7 months. Developer contacts: 3 total — two build reviews (development team presenting work for review against design intent) and one question session (approximately 5 questions). Three contacts over 7 months is the lowest contact frequency in the portfolio alongside Chemical Watch and Squaremind. The build review structure is notable: the contacts were not unsolicited clarification requests but proactive governance checks initiated by the development team.
- **Task completion time before redesign: 9 minutes average. Client-reported by CDR Foodlab from their own internal measurement.**
- **Task completion time after redesign: 3.4 minutes average. Creative Navy-measured, 14 users, final shipped product. Same task type, same user population, comparable conditions.**
- **User satisfaction before redesign: 72% (68 respondents). One year after redesign: 93% (76 respondents). Independent third-party survey, identical instrument, same population. Client-reported — Creative Navy did not conduct the measurement.**
- Evidence labelling discipline for CDR Foodlab: task completion before = client-reported; task completion after = Creative Navy-measured; satisfaction = client-reported (third-party-administered); workaround elimination = user-reported. State each basis separately whenever these figures are used together.

### Streetec kntrl 2 (aftermarket automotive HMI — the page's strongest single body of evidence)

- Streetec GmbH's kntrl 2, the control system for aftermarket automotive air suspension. Shipped and in the market. Verbatim client quotation is permitted for this engagement, attributed to role rather than person.
- A 4.3-inch 480×800 portrait capacitive panel, mounted in the centre of the dashboard.
- Thumb-reach positioning, in the client's words: "The majority of people is right handed, so you probably operate with your thumb. And so the control should be on the right side so that your thumb doesn't [cover] the units or the values."
- Finger occlusion designed around: pressure values sit above their adjustment arrows so the user's finger does not cover the number being changed.
- Discrete rather than continuous sliders for tank pressure and brightness, in fixed increments — because precision matters for pressure and "some people might have large fingers".
- Font sizes increased by 25% between review rounds, to 20px body text, after legibility was checked at physical size; designs were verified at true physical size, including measuring the rendered screen against a ruler during a review call when the on-screen size looked wrong, and committing to check the result on the real panel before sign-off.
- The keyboard constraint forced name entry onto the phone: a 4.3-inch screen cannot host a full alphabetic keyboard, so the HMI shows a two-character abbreviation and profile names are editable only in the mobile app — a hardware constraint resolved by allocating the feature across devices.
- The display supports brightness control, darkening triggered by a digital signal from the vehicle, a separate brightness value for the darkened state, and user-configurable haptic feedback intensity.

### AIT heat pump controller (rotary-first embedded interface — the page's first rotary-input evidence)

- AIT, a European manufacturer of residential and commercial heat pumps. AIT may be named; brands, OEM partners, programme and product names may not. **AIT is the only engagement in the corpus where a rotary encoder is the guaranteed input and touch is the optional one.**
- The hardware: a colour LCD of roughly five inches at 800×480, landscape — not finally fixed at kick-off, since the panel choice depended on price and availability and AIT said plainly it could change. A rotary push-button mounted centrally below the display, roughly 90 steps per revolution: turn to move, press to confirm, press for three seconds to return to the home screen, with a ring of LEDs around the knob indicating system status including faults. A physical Back button, and a second physical button whose function was undecided (candidate: Info). No Home button, by decision — the three-second knob press does that job.
- The governing requirement: every interaction must work identically through knob and touch, with the concept surviving the removal of either. Touch was optional throughout — if the touchscreen were dropped from the specification, the whole concept had to remain operable on the knob; in the other direction AIT anticipated touch-only units within a few years, and already exports the interface to a web server and a simulator where no hardware buttons exist at all.
- The decisions that follow: plus and minus buttons removed in favour of one scroll selector usable by both inputs — the decisive argument being consistency, one pattern applied everywhere, after AIT pointed out that 0.1° increments would mean pressing plus twenty times to move two degrees. The status bar held back from interactivity, because anything interactive in it must also be knob-reachable and its targets are too small to touch reliably at physical size; the single exception is the alert banner, whose area is large enough to select. The back control relocated out of the status bar to a touchable position below it, so it works on hardware with no physical buttons. The system tile on the home screen kept a single target, because nesting targets inside a tile is punishing to reach with a rotary knob. Numeric and alphanumeric entry (serial number, hydraulic code, IP address) uses per-digit scroll selection rather than a full keyboard — it works identically on knob and touch, and the values include hexadecimal digits, where a keyboard offering 0–9 and A–F confuses non-expert users; AIT's opening position was a keyboard and the position changed during the call, with both components specified so the decision remained reversible. Text entry (circuit names, mode names, Wi-Fi passwords) uses a full keyboard in a full-screen modal that hides the top bar, purely to maximise key size.
- Legibility at physical size: reviewing a wireframe at real screen size, AIT observed that the smaller text size was "really, really small" for an older installer with glasses; widget content was cut to three pieces of information each in direct response. Equal-size widgets resize to the number of installed modes — one to four modes fit with no horizontal scrolling at all, covering the overwhelming majority of installations, since most installations are heating and hot water only; between one and seven modes may be installed.
- **Close with the fidelity fact, at its required framing: the controller reached the market months after the engagement ended. Creative Navy compared the shipped interface against the designs it had delivered: the product ships as the design that was delivered. It ships in a touch-only hardware configuration.** The move to touch-only was AIT's hardware decision, not a Creative Navy outcome; nothing may be said about the shipped product's performance, reception or sales; and "it shipped as designed" is evidence that the design survived development, not evidence that the design is good.

### The MDU bridge interface (touch as the only input anywhere in the environment)

- The MDU, a ship motion monitoring system whose bridge interface Creative Navy designed. The manufacturer is not named — "the manufacturer", or "the MDU's manufacturer, a Danish sensor manufacturer" where a first-mention gloss is needed. Verbatim quotation is permitted, attributed to role. **The MDU is the opposite pole to AIT and worth naming as such: the only engagement where touch is the only input available anywhere in the operating environment, with no keyboard, no mouse and no hardware button to fall back on.**
- A 15-inch certified marine panel PC at 1024×768, active display area 305.13 × 229.1 mm, pixel pitch 0.2947 mm, 400 cd/m² brightness, 700:1 contrast, projected-capacitive touch, IP65-sealed front, rated −15 to 55 °C, with a backlight dimmable from near 0% to 100% and five capacitive bezel keys including day, dusk and night mode switching. The panel carries IEC 60945 4th edition, DNV 2.4 and IACS E10 certification and ECDIS colour calibration compliance — **these are properties of the panel the manufacturer selected. Creative Navy did not certify to, audit against, or work to any of them.**
- Touch only, in the manufacturer's own words: "The display is a 15'' inch touch display. There will be no mouse or keyboard on the ship."
- A documented touch-target and legibility review, grounded in published literature — Fitts's law, and MIT Touch Lab fingertip dimensions of 16–20 mm as reported in Yablonski, *Laws of UX* — converted into working minimums for that specific panel at approximately 3.35 pixels per millimetre: a 13 mm (44 px) minimum touch dimension, 6 mm (20 px) minimum spacing between adjacent targets, 20 mm (68 px) as the largest size that still improves accuracy, an 8 px baseline grid chosen as a common divisor of 1024 and 768, and a resulting 48 px minimum touch area, with an element 48 px tall specified to exceed 48 px in width. The review states its own dependency: the safe minimum also depends on the quality of the touchscreen.
- Decisions made on target size and touch precision: a four-digit numeric PIN and keypad instead of a password and full keyboard — the purpose is accident prevention, not security ("we don't want people to accidentally change it, that's the primary concern"), and a numeric keypad gives larger targets ("the individual numbers will be bigger so it's easier to press"). Sliders and scrub inputs rejected for numeric entry because precision is unreliable under a finger — "the roll where you have to like click with a finger or move it and get the precise value is going to be hard on a touch screen" — in favour of a number pad on every numeric field. Value editing as a bottom modal rather than a full-screen one, partly to maximise the dismiss area. Chart tooltips questioned on touch precision rather than display quality — the advisor's term was "sausage fingers" — with a tap-to-read-value interaction specified instead.
- The constraint with no equivalent elsewhere on this page: **the unit's position relative to its reader cannot be predicted** — "They will be wherever there's space physically on the bridge. So nobody knows." That is what rules out a navigation sidebar: a sidebar correct for someone standing to the left of the screen forces someone standing to the right to reach across the display and cover it. It also inverts the usual space constraint — horizontal space is scarce here, vertical is not — so bottom navigation was chosen over both sidebars and a hamburger menu, the hamburger rejected for costing an extra click when vertical space was not under pressure.

### Oceaneering hospital dispatch panel (wall-mounted, gloved, read from across a room)

### Raymarine next-generation fixed-mount VHF (a constraint on what a physical control can mean)

- Raymarine is the client and may be named, as may its existing fixed-mount VHF line — Ray53, Ray63 and Ray73, the documented precedent. **The new radio's name may not be used in any form.** No quotation marks and no reported speech on any page carrying Raymarine. The radio was built and released; **no design-fidelity comparison is recorded, so it must never be written as "shipped as designed".**
- The page's other entries describe constraints on **what a screen can show** — pixel density, contrast, touch targets, legibility at distance. **Raymarine adds a constraint on what a physical control can mean**: a 480 × 272 logical canvas with two rotary encoders, a four-way directional keypad with a confirm key, three softkeys whose actions are printed directly above them, a direct priority-channel key, a covered distress control, a dedicated man-overboard key, a power key and a wired microphone carrying push-to-talk, channel up and down, volume up and down, a priority-channel key and a transmit-power key — where the design question was not how much fits on the display but whether one input could carry three jobs. **Describe 480 × 272 as a design canvas, not a specification**: it was stated in the handover as a layout convention for the programme, with pixel density, display behaviour, hardware geometry and final test thresholds left as engineering inputs, and the physical panel size is not confirmed.
- **Lead the entry with the physical input contract**: ten controls, each specified in three conditions — radio view, AIS or menu view, and interrupted state — with five attached principles: any context-sensitive button must have a visible label on the active screen; transmit and receive state must remain visible during AIS browsing; a modal event must not silently reinterpret a continuous physical control; returning from an alert should restore a safe and comprehensible task context; and power, volume, transmit and protected emergency controls are never repurposed as general navigation inputs.
- How the contract was reached: exploration initially treated the hardware controls as an established input set, assigning functions per screen. A promising AIS concept used the lower encoder to move quickly between vessel targets — but the same encoder had already been assigned as the primary channel selector in the radio view, and settings would have given it a third role. When an alert interrupted an interaction, it became unclear whether the next rotation would select a vessel, change the tuned channel or navigate a message. The failure mode's specific character was recorded at the time: the action may appear to be accepted, because a visible object moves, so a mode error is not necessarily accompanied by an error message. The question was reframed from which AIS layout offered the shortest path to which control model could guarantee predictable communication behaviour across all screens, interruptions and levels of user experience.
- Four control models were compared against the same operational scenarios: contextual rotary (fast, with hidden mode-change risk); fixed role (predictable, at the cost of extra key presses on some AIS tasks); explicit mode (examined in two variants and both rejected — a persisting mode creates a memory burden after attention has shifted, a resetting mode changes the meaning of the next rotation unexpectedly, and a visible mode label does not remove the need to reacquire state under divided attention); and a dedicated new control (set aside rather than rejected on merit — a separate navigation encoder would preserve domain separation but carries industrial design, bill-of-materials and panel-space consequences, and remains recorded as an industrial design option if later product testing identifies unacceptable AIS browsing effort).
- Why fixed role prevailed, as recorded: consistency outweighs isolated task speed; interruptions remain intelligible; safety actions retain physical priority — priority channel access, distress initiation and push-to-talk do not depend on what the display currently shows; and hardware changes stay optional. The retained trade-off was recorded at the moment of decision, not discovered later: directional navigation through dense traffic needs usability checks, and the subsequent sprint was tasked with testing whether filtering, list sorting or accelerated key navigation reduces selection effort. The risk was characterised as structural rather than cosmetic — if the same encoder silently changed purpose, better labels could not fully remove the ambiguity. The pivot explicitly claimed no measured error reduction and no engineering cost saving; the value claimed was the removal of a known inconsistency from the preferred architecture before three weeks of detailed flow design and three weeks of UI production.
- The resulting invariant, carried through every subsequent sprint: a screen may change what the keypad and the labelled softkeys do; it must not change the domain of the dedicated channel encoder, the microphone channel controls, push-to-talk, priority channel access or the protected emergency actions. **An input may be unavailable within its own functional domain — it is never silently reassigned to a different one.** The operator sees a rejected channel request, not a surprise focus change.
- **Nothing was validated on production hardware.** Encoder detent force and press events, tactile performance, wet-surface operation, glove accessibility, input bounce, transmit timeout and alarm acoustics were all recorded as open engineering dependencies at the close of the engagement.

### Handheld command-and-control (a small screen whose user is gloved, standing, and holding the device braced)

- A European manufacturer of uncrewed systems, including one-way effectors, and the tactical command-and-control software that operates them. Nothing else may be named — no product, vehicle, hardware or individual; device trade names are replaced by their specifications, and every number is kept. No quotation marks and no reported speech on any page carrying this engagement. The handheld interface was built; no more may be said about its status, and the silence is not explained. The register is that of a safety-engineering document; the scope boundary holds — Creative Navy designed the surface through which a human supervises the system; it did not design the system.
- **The physical envelope first**: a 7-inch panel at 1920 × 1200 and 323 ppi (12.72 px/mm), 151 × 94 mm of glass, 2,500 nits, ten bezel keys around the screen, two front push buttons, the joysticks, one operator-presence button on the rear, MIL-STD-810H, IP65, −20 °C to +50 °C — **and roughly half the screen reachable by a braced thumb**. Against it, a 10.1-inch tablet at 1920 × 1200 and roughly 224 ppi (8.83 px/mm), 218 × 136 mm, 1,000 nits, of which roughly 5% of the screen is thumb-reachable in freehand set-down use — which the specification treats as one tier stricter in mission.
- **The page's existing entries are about small screens. This one is about a small screen whose user is wearing gloves, standing, and holding the device braced.** (The glove half of the evidence belongs on the glove-operable interfaces page, not here.)
- The three rules that make the constraint productive rather than merely limiting: **consequence tiers rather than a blanket floor** — four tiers specified in millimetres with pixel values derived per device, from T1 (critical and unprotected: recovery actions, holds, mode changes, limited to three or four per view, at ≥22 mm with 5 mm isolation) through T2 (primary in-mission actions, 15 mm), T3 (mis-tap-tolerant actions, ~12 mm) to T4 (planning and static density, 8–10 mm). **Protection patterns permitted below the critical tier and never at it** — slide-to-confirm, hold-to-commit at 300–600 ms, and select-then-arm are permitted at T2 and below, never at T1, where size is the protection and is never substituted. **And the capacity rule: if a view cannot fit its controls at their tier sizes, controls are cut or moved to hardware — tiers are not downgraded to fit.** The ceiling the rule produces: a maximum of roughly six to eight on-screen touch controls per in-mission view, and no more than three or four critical ones.
- Supporting rules from the nine design rules: one component library sized from the handheld column with two layout templates, so handheld layouts inherit safely onto the physically larger tablet; hit areas may exceed the drawn control, but the visual should approximate the true target and adjacent invisible hit zones are avoided; contrast of ≥7:1 for critical text and ≥4.5:1 for large text; state never communicated by colour alone; primary navigation on the handheld's bezel keys, with touch reserved for spatial tasks and dense interfaces moved into full-screen takeovers; night-vision compatibility present on one device and undetermined on the other.
- **The portability lesson: the specification was authored in millimetres with pixels derived per device because the display had not been chosen when the work began.** An earlier revision derives from an 8-inch panel at 11.16 px/mm; the settled specification derives from a 7-inch panel at 323 ppi and 12.72 px/mm. The unit choice is what made the specification survive the hardware decision, and what makes it portable to the second device.
- **Throughout: this is a specification, not a conformance claim, and not a result.** The specification measures itself against MIL-STD-1472 and states where it deliberately departs from it — the standard sets a 16 mm minimum touch target with 5 mm added for gloved use, two of the four tiers sit below that, and T2 at 15 mm sits below even the bare-hand minimum, applying the full standard only where a missed tap is unrecoverable. It was not validated, and nobody was measured operating anything faster or more accurately.

### The general argument the entries make together

- A design system serving products with different input models needs a shared visual layer and separate interaction layers. AIT is the rotary-guaranteed pole; the MDU is the touch-only pole; the industrial engagement states the non-transferability principle outright; Raymarine shows the same discipline applied to physical control semantics rather than display real estate.
- Three constraint classes now have dedicated pages and should be cross-referenced rather than re-argued here: the input-model page (`/capabilities/when-the-input-device-is-not-fixed`), the glove page (`/capabilities/glove-operable-interfaces`) and the ambient-range page (`/capabilities/designing-for-the-full-ambient-range`).

## What this page must not claim

- **Do not add Assemblio** — the Smart Viewer runs on tablets but is a browser application on general-purpose hardware, not an embedded device; its constraints are environmental and attentional. **Do not add Egma or visioncheckout** — web, native mobile and a 1920 × 1080 full touchscreen are general-purpose hardware, the same exclusion for the same reason. **Do not add KISPE** — a desktop application with a tablet presentation whose field hardware was never identified. **Do not add Schone** — a desktop console at a 1440 × 900 reference with no pilot command layout released below 1024 px; no constrained-hardware story exists and none may be invented. **Do not add maritime VTS** — two 27-inch displays at 2560 × 1440 with keyboard and mouse is the opposite of a constrained interface; do not import the other marine studies' primary context reflexively.
- **No Raymarine hardware, firmware, field, lighting, glove, vibration, wet-surface or acoustic claim** — nothing was validated on production hardware. No type-approval, certification, conformity or regulatory credential; no "shipped as designed"; no commercial or reception figure; the proprietary terms *performance in reality* and *competitive vector* are forbidden on any page carrying Raymarine.
- **The handheld C2 sizing material is a specification, not a conformance claim and not a result.** Never the standard's name alone — always the graduation and the stated departures. Never "protection patterns substitute for size" without the T1 exclusion in the same sentence. No safety, certification, weapons-review or regulatory credential; no claim the design prevents accidental activation or operator error; no field claim of Creative Navy's own.
- **The MDU panel's certifications belong to the panel the manufacturer selected** — never to Creative Navy's work. No usability, comprehension, field-research or persona claim from the MDU; nothing was tested and nothing was observed at sea.
- **No efficiency, throughput, error-rate, time-saving or training outcome from Oceaneering** — nothing was measured; gloved use is a documented operating condition with specified touch targets, not something tested with gloves. Oceaneering speech is reported, never quoted.
- **Nothing in the industrial engagement is an outcome**; the product is in development and the client is identified only by its locked descriptor.
- **No AIT performance, efficiency, commissioning-time, error-rate or commercial figure**, and no usability result — nothing was tested with users.
- **No engagement dates anywhere** — sequence and duration only.
