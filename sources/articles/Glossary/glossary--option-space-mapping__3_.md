# /glossary/our-concepts/option-space-mapping

---

- Definition from source: systematic exploration of multiple solutions for each challenge before converging on an approach.

- Used in Sandbox Experiments. Typically 3–6 variants explored per key challenge.

- Each variant is an experiment — a question being asked of reality, not a proposal being validated. Some solutions reveal unexpected problems. Others expose assumptions that don't hold. The best ones teach something that couldn't have been learned by asking or analysing alone.

- The output is not a set of concepts to choose from. It's a map of the option space — understanding of what works, what fails, why, and what each direction reveals about the problem.

- The contrast: most design processes converge early on one direction based on assumptions, then validate and refine. Option space mapping deliberately delays convergence, treating early convergence as a risk rather than efficiency.

- Why this matters: by exploring the terrain before converging, weak product frames are identified and abandoned before investment accumulates behind them. The costly pivots competitors face when initial assumptions fail under real use are prevented.

- Relationship to lateral exploration: option space mapping is the structured practice; lateral exploration is the principle. Lateral exploration is about learning through making. Option space mapping is the specific method of producing and examining multiple simultaneous solutions to understand the problem space.

- Grounded example — Elsner smart home controller: six distinct navigation architectures were built and evaluated before convergence: lower bar tabs, hamburger menu, top ribbon menu, carousel menu, multiple buttons layout, and a physical home button concept. Each direction was evaluated against the constrained hardware and against observed usage patterns. The temperature control had its own option space: a vertical thermometer-inspired scale was developed alongside a circular gauge, each teaching the team something different about how users read temperature state on a small round display. Convergence happened only after the terrain of each challenge was understood through building and observing, not through preference or assumption.

- Grounded example — Torqeedo maritime HMI: three concept types were built and tested using real data rhythms during sea trials: propulsion-first concepts (emphasising drive state above energy flow), energy-flow-first concepts (foregrounding battery and generator behaviour), and merged perspective concepts (attempting to present propulsion and energy as a single integrated view). Testing with real data rhythms revealed where promising concepts collapsed under vibration or produced hesitation at critical moments. Concepts requiring too many transitions or slowing down night manoeuvres were discarded on the basis of observed behaviour, not preference. The surviving configuration — 27 screens across 4 operational modes — emerged from this elimination process. The key word is elimination: options were not chosen from a menu but removed from the map as the terrain revealed what wouldn't work.

- Grounded example — Triopsis workforce management SaaS: four rounds of usability testing with prototypes across the engagement indicate that multiple workflow and layout directions were built and observed before convergence. 47 microtasks mapped against three personas provided the evidence base that each explored direction was tested against.

- Grounded example — Gexcon CFD simulation software: the most quantified instance of option space mapping in the case study set. Ten key challenges were defined for the product. Three to six solutions were explored for each, producing 45 total variants. These were evaluated across 37 test sessions with users and engineers. Each option was assessed against four explicit criteria: learning effort, expert performance, future extensibility, and coding cost. This four-criterion evaluation framework makes explicit what option space mapping is doing — not selecting a preferred design but understanding what each direction costs and what it enables along dimensions that matter to different stakeholders. Four decision workshops with product and engineering leadership then synthesised the evaluation findings into shared alignment across stakeholder groups, producing a detailed requirement structure that served as the foundation for the interaction design phase. The scale here — 45 variants, 37 sessions, four criteria, four workshops — reflects a full-product application of option space mapping rather than a component-level application.

- Grounded example — Beissbarth automotive calibration: three structural variants for the OEM embedded display were built and evaluated, exploring different approaches to grouping measurement values and procedure states on a screen read from 2–3 metres during movement. High-fidelity prototypes were tested under conditions reproducing workshop lighting and viewing distances — the real performance constraints, not lab conditions. This is a component-level application of option space mapping within a constrained hardware context: the option space being mapped was not the full product architecture but the specific challenge of state communication on a small embedded display. The evaluation conditions (workshop lighting, viewing distance, movement) are what made the mapping meaningful — without those constraints, the variants could not be evaluated against what mattered.

- Grounded example — Akrivia Health clinical research platform (hypothesis-structured option space mapping): five distinct interaction models for cohort construction were developed as competing hypotheses about how clinical researchers think when building complex queries. Each model embodied a different theory of the user's cognitive approach: a wizard model guided users through sequential steps; a nested logic blocks model represented the query as a visible tree of grouped conditions; a timeline model organised conditions around the patient record's temporal structure; a fragment reuse model emphasised reusable cohort components across projects; a side-by-side comparison model presented simultaneous views of cohort variants. These were not design concepts to select from — each was a structured experiment. The five models were tested through six design cycles of increasing fidelity, from rough wireframes to interactive prototypes. Eight usability sessions with NHS analysts, academic researchers, and pharmaceutical research staff tested realistic tasks: building a treatment-resistant depression cohort, adjusting inclusion criteria on an existing cohort, and explaining query logic to a colleague. Convergence produced a final query builder combining elements from three of the five models — the readability of the nested logic model, temporal organisation cues from the timeline model, and the fragment reuse capability. What distinguishes this instance is the explicit hypothesis structure: each model was designed to test a specific theory of how the user thinks, not to propose a preferred direction. The elimination process revealed not just which model worked but why each failed — information that made the convergence substantive rather than preferential.

- Grounded example — deSoutter Medical / Zethon surgical device interface (option space mapping under IEC 62366-1 requirements): eight information architecture patterns were developed and evaluated for an embedded GUI on a safety-critical powered surgical instrument. The patterns explored distinct structural approaches: a single hub model, a step-based sequence, clustered tabs, a flat layout organised by device states, a tool-centric view with persistent status, a parameter-centric view, a state-machine-oriented screen set, and a hybrid model combining aspects of several approaches. Each pattern was evaluated against representative surgical workflows derived directly from surgeon interview sessions — not against generic usability criteria. The evaluation criteria were operationally grounded: number of interactions required to reach essential functions, frequency of screen switching during cutting procedures, and clarity of readiness and warning states at a glance. This is option space mapping applied under regulated conditions: the evaluation criteria were determined by IEC 62366-1 use scenarios and risk considerations, making the elimination process traceable to the usability engineering requirements catalogue rather than to design preference. The surviving structure organised screens by procedural relevance rather than software module, limited navigation depth, and kept the most critical status information permanently visible — a configuration that none of the eight structural patterns provided in isolation, but that emerged from understanding what each pattern revealed about the problem.

- Grounded example — Swiss petrol station operator / embedded POS and forecourt systems (option space mapping evaluated against an observed transaction corpus): sixteen alternative POS architectures were modelled during Concept Convergence, each reorganising how transaction types were grouped, how fuel and shop items were combined, and how discount and loyalty logic was presented in relation to cashier task sequences. The evaluation mechanism was distinctive: each architecture was assessed against the observed dataset of 532 transactions coded by type and complexity during Sandbox Experiments — not against design principles or user preferences, but against frequency and operational weight of real transaction paths. Six architectures were selected for wireframe prototyping at the actual till resolution of 1920×1080 pixels, and evaluated across 29 structured test sessions with cashiers and supervisors. This is option space mapping applied to a high-throughput retail operations context, where the evaluation criterion (how often a given structure would touch common transaction paths under peak load of 84 transactions per hour) was derived directly from field observation. The scale — sixteen architectures before convergence — reflects a full-product application rather than a component-level one. What distinguishes this instance from others in the set is the transaction corpus as evaluation substrate: the option space was not mapped against user preference or design judgment, but against quantified operational reality.

- Grounded example — Stromer e-bike embedded display, mobile app, and web account (option space mapping across a full-product redesign of an existing live consumer product): the Stromer engagement covered 42 distinct design topics across three surfaces (embedded display, mobile app, web account) over 14 months of active design work. Option space mapping was applied topic by topic across the full scope: the smartlock flow (5 iterations), the firmware update flow (6 iterations), and the tire pressure sensor design (4 iterations) are documented examples; the remaining 39 topics followed the same pattern of deliberate exploration before convergence.

  What distinguishes this instance from others in the set is its context: this was not a greenfield product or a legacy system being restructured from scratch. It was a live consumer product with an existing installed user base, an inherited design system, and a previous agency's year of design work already in place. Option space mapping in this context served a specific function: it was the method for discovering the constraints, tensions, and failure modes embedded in the existing product that domain learning alone could not surface. Each iteration of a topic was a question asked of the design space — what does this approach reveal about the problem that the previous approach had not? — rather than a proposal being refined.

  The firmware update flow (6 iterations) illustrates this clearly. The iterations were not successive improvements of a known solution; each explored a different structural response to a genuinely unresolved tension: how to communicate a background download process (which could take hours and which Stromer did not want foregrounded) alongside a user-triggered install process (which required full-screen treatment and clear preconditions) across two surfaces (embedded display and mobile app) whose interaction models needed to remain coherent. No single iteration resolved this — the resolution emerged from understanding what each iteration revealed about the specific technical and UX constraints, which only became fully visible as iterations made them concrete. The convergence at 6 iterations was not a sign that the problem was unusually difficult; it was the deliberate exploration of a multi-constraint tension until the option space was understood well enough to produce a grounded decision.

  The scale — 42 topics, 1-week sprint cadence, three surfaces — represents the broadest full-product application of option space mapping in the current case study set in terms of topic count. The Gexcon engagement (45 variants across 10 challenges) is the most quantified single-product application; the Stromer engagement is the most extensive in terms of distinct design topics addressed across a single engagement.

- Grounded example — Squaremind dermatology scanning device (option space mapping under contradictory constraints in a patient-operated embedded interface): six components of the patient scanning flow were mapped across multiple iterations before convergence: patient ID (2 iterations), body part selection (3 iterations), full scan process (5 iterations), progress bar (5 iterations), progressive disclosure elements (5 iterations), and the pause button (7 iterations). The pause button required the most exploration because user testing revealed that different users held fundamentally incompatible mental models of what it meant: some interpreted it as useless (the patient is not supposed to move, so why pause?), some as an emergency control (stop immediately if something is wrong), and some as a contextual tool (relevant only if the robot moves uncomfortably close). These are not variants of the same mental model — they represent different theories of what the patient's agency is during the scan, and each theory implies a different design. Convergence on an emergency-like control was reached not by preference but by working through what each interpretation implied for the full Inform–Prevent–Correct architecture and which option produced the most coherent system behaviour across all patient states.

  The progress bar (5 iterations) faced a structurally different but equally constrained option space: it had to simultaneously communicate process completion, current step identity, approximate time remaining, and sequence context (front scan / back scan / complete) — on a 1024×768 4:3 screen, at standing-patient reading distance, in a context where the patient cannot interact with it mid-scan. Iterations that worked for one of these requirements failed for others. The evaluation was not against user preference but against the full constraint set that the physical and operational context imposed.

  What distinguishes this instance is the source of constraint: unlike Gexcon (evaluated against four explicit criteria) or the Swiss petrol station (evaluated against a transaction corpus), Squaremind's option space was mapped against the physical and operational reality of an undressed patient alone in a room with a moving robot arm — a context that imposes constraints on attention, on interpretability under stress, and on the consequences of confusion that no laboratory evaluation would have surfaced. The 7-iteration pause button count is the highest single-component iteration count in the current case study set.

- Grounded example — CDR Foodlab chemical analysis instrument (option space mapping on a new feature with no existing precedent, under hardware and client-requirement constraints): CDR Foodlab's working lists flow — the engagement's highest-iteration workflow — received 10 iterations for the analysis list design and 10 iterations for the run-multiple-analyses flow. These are the highest iteration counts for a complete workflow in the current case study set, distinct from the Squaremind pause button (7 iterations on a single component) in that they cover an entire user flow rather than an isolated interaction.

  The constraint structure was specific to this instance. Working lists was a new feature with no precedent in the existing product — users had no prior mental model for it, and the design team had no existing pattern to build from. The 7-inch constrained display (1024×600px, 5-point capacitive) imposed strict limits on the information architecture. And the client's functional requirements for the working lists feature introduced genuine UX feasibility tensions: whether the specified interaction model could be implemented on this hardware in a way that would be less burdensome than the manual process it was intended to replace. These three constraints — novel feature, hardware limits, client-requirement feasibility — could not be resolved independently. The iteration process was the method for understanding how they interacted and what combinations of decisions produced a coherent result across all three.

  The remaining flows show the full iteration distribution: dashboard (4 iterations), main navigation (3), single analysis (4), run single analysis (3), history (3), tutorials (3), alarms (2), create sample list (2), working lists run (2). Each set of iterations was presented to client stakeholders with pros and cons articulated, incorporating stakeholder feedback, user input, and Creative Navy's expert recommendations. Technical constraints introduced by the client during review shaped which directions remained viable — constraint respecting operating through the iteration process rather than as a separate phase.

  What distinguishes this instance from others in the set is the source of convergence pressure: unlike Gexcon (evaluated against explicit criteria) or Akrivia Health (competing hypotheses about user cognition), the CDR Foodlab working lists convergence was driven by a three-way constraint interaction where no individual constraint was sufficient to determine the direction — only the combination was. The resolved direction is not available for documentation; the iteration structure and constraint character are.

- Grounded example — Neugo UK visa application case-management platform (option space mapping as two-axis triage on desirability × feasibility): Neugo is the cleanest two-axis instance of option space mapping in the set, and it applies the practice at a different level from the others. Where the examples above map multiple design *solutions to a given challenge*, Neugo mapped the space of candidate *possibilities* surfaced in a short Sandbox phase and triaged which would make the build, on two independent axes: how strongly users wanted each (desirability) and how technically feasible each was within roughly a two-year horizon. The map partitioned into three regions — a large majority where want and feasibility were both high, which converged easily and made up most of the product; a small set dropped because feasibility was near zero regardless of want; and a weighed middle resolved by triangulating product-manager judgement, Creative Navy's input on user salience, and the developers' input on the designs. The distinguishing substrate here is the desirability × feasibility plane itself: unlike Gexcon's four explicit criteria, deSoutter's IEC 62366-1 use scenarios, or the Socar transaction corpus, Neugo's option space was evaluated on a simple, legible two-axis partition. Two calibration points must travel with this example: the region proportions (a large easy-converge majority, a small dropped set, a weighed middle) are the practitioner's recollection of the shape, not measured figures, so do not cite precise percentages; and this was prioritisation, not the resolution of a local-versus-system tension — it produced a prioritised configuration, not a competitive vector (see /method/concept-convergence on convergence as prioritisation). It is the set's cleanest example of option space mapping used to decide *what to build* rather than *how to build a given thing*.

- Grounded example — eToro multi-asset social trading (option space mapping across three connected decision surfaces, with rejected options carrying explicit regulatory rationale): each of the three foundational surfaces was explored through multiple structural concepts before convergence — landing (3 concepts), explore (4 concepts), buy flow (3 concepts) — treating each surface as a decision system rather than a screen. What distinguishes this instance is that the rejected options were eliminated for combined cognitive and regulatory reasons, not preference, and the rejections are as instructive as the convergences.

  On the landing surface, three concepts were built: signal-first entry (market movement, social activity, and volatility separated into distinct channels), an opportunity-framing "recommended assets" direction (improved hierarchy over the existing pattern), and a user-state-adaptive entry (different compositions for new, returning, and high-activity users). Convergence was a hybrid of signal-first and adaptive entry, with the recommended-assets direction deliberately dropped to avoid implicit persuasion signals — a rejection driven by the financial-promotion / implied-advice constraint as much as by clarity. On the explore surface (the highest divergence, 4 concepts): a ranked opportunity feed, a multi-signal comparison view, a narrative "market stories" model, and a social-proximity model leaning into copy-trading dynamics. Convergence combined the multi-signal and social-proximity directions but imposed strict separation between social and market signals to avoid implied-endorsement effects — again, the rejected and modified options were shaped by the SEC Marketing Rule / MiFID II surface, not only by usability. On the buy flow (3 concepts): a minimal-friction execution flow, a risk-expanded decision layer foregrounding exposure and downside before confirmation, and a context-enriched execution model. Convergence strongly favoured the risk-expanded layer with selective context integration for position sizing.

  What distinguishes this instance from others in the set: in Gexcon options were evaluated against four explicit criteria, in Squaremind against the physical reality of a patient alone with a robot arm, in Neugo on a desirability × feasibility plane. In eToro, a primary axis of elimination was regulatory — whether an option could read as a recommendation, an endorsement, or an implied outcome — operating jointly with cognitive clarity. It is the set's clearest case of constraint-respecting elimination where the binding constraint on the rejected options was a financial-regulation surface rather than hardware, criteria, or feasibility. Calibration: the concept counts (3 / 4 / 3) and the convergence directions are from the engagement record; eToro involved no AI, so the "signals" in question are market and social signals surfaced by the interface, not model outputs.

- What option space mapping is not: brainstorming followed by selection. The variants are not ideas to evaluate conceptually — they are built sufficiently to be tested against real constraints, real data, and real users. The learning happens through making and observing, not through comparison of descriptions. (The Neugo triage instance is the one place the practice operates on candidate possibilities rather than built design variants; even there, the possibilities came out of a Sandbox phase of building, and the axes — desirability and technical feasibility — were grounded in user salience and developer assessment rather than in preference.)

- Note: the term is clean — no prior associations in UX or agile literature that would interfere. Can be used without disambiguation.

- Note on iteration count records in the current case study set: highest single-component iteration count — Squaremind pause button (7 iterations). Highest complete-workflow iteration count — CDR Foodlab working lists (10 iterations per flow). Most quantified full-product application — Gexcon (45 variants, 37 sessions, 4 criteria). Most extensive by distinct topic count — Stromer (42 topics across 3 surfaces). Cleanest two-axis (desirability × feasibility) and the clearest triage-level application — Neugo (option space mapping used to decide *what to build* rather than *how to build a given thing*). Clearest case of regulation as the binding elimination constraint — eToro (landing/explore/buy concepts where rejected options were dropped for financial-promotion / implied-advice reasons jointly with clarity). These are different dimensions of scale and application and should not be conflated when citing evidence.
