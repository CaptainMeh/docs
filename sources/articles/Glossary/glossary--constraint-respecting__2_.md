# /glossary/our-concepts/constraint-respecting

---

- Definition from source: treating legacy systems as constraints to work within, not obstacles to overcome — preserving what works while restructuring what doesn't.

- Framing: legacy systems encode years of experience. That experience has value even when the system is technically outdated or visually dated. Users have internalized it. Workflows have formed around it. Breaking it blindly destroys value that cannot be easily reconstructed.

- What constraint respecting is not: accepting everything as fixed. Constraints are examined — some are real, some are assumed. "Which constraints are real and which are assumed? What actually must stay fixed vs. what just feels that way?" — this question is part of multi-perspective synthesis.

- The distinction the source doc makes: there is experience encoded in existing designs that represents real knowledge about how users work. Treating legacy as an obstacle to overcome throws away that knowledge. Treating it as a parameter to work within preserves the knowledge while restructuring what doesn't serve users.

- In practice: identify what works and preserve it. Identify what doesn't work and redesign it. Be explicit about which is which and why.

- Relationship to sense decay: constraint respecting is one of the mechanisms that prevents sense decay. When legacy design decisions are understood rather than overwritten, the meaning encoded in them is preserved. When they are overwritten without understanding, meaning erodes.

- Relationship to domain learning: you can only respect constraints you understand. Domain learning is what builds the understanding of which legacy patterns encode genuine operational knowledge vs. which are just accumulated technical debt.

- Also applies to: technical constraints (what developers say is possible or impossible), organisational constraints (what clients say they can or can't change), process constraints (established workflows that users depend on), regulatory constraints (external standards that define what the design can and cannot do — some are fixed, some permit more design space than they appear to on first reading), and design system constraints (a client's existing visual language, component library, and governance rules that define the parameters within which new design work must operate).

- Grounded example — Torqeedo maritime HMI: the previous embedded interface was treated as a source of evidence before any redesign decisions were made. Its structure revealed how professional captains had learned to compensate for scattered propulsion, battery, and generator information across separate screens — and where that compensation created stress and hesitation during manoeuvres. Analysing these compensation patterns through constraint respecting allowed the team to decide what deserved preservation and what required restructuring. The interaction patterns captains had internalised were carried forward; the structural failures (information fragmentation, contrast failures in daylight) were the targets for redesign. Every deviation from the legacy structure was conscious and documented. This is constraint respecting applied at the epistemic level: the legacy system was not a problem to replace but a body of encoded operational knowledge to be read.

- Grounded example — Elsner smart home controller: hardware constraints from the device supplier were incorporated as design parameters from the first engineering sessions, not discovered late. Voltage requirements, graphics interface behaviour, colour depth limits, and processing constraints shaped every visual and interaction decision. The firmware's discrete temperature increment commands (0.5° steps) determined the interaction model for the temperature control — the interface matched the firmware's behaviour exactly rather than imposing a different model that would have required reconciliation. When the final display panel had not yet been confirmed, the team designed at 320×240px and scaled up once the panel was selected — a risk-reduction decision that treated hardware uncertainty as a real constraint requiring an explicit response.

  Constraint respecting in this engagement also operated at the level of existing interface patterns. The Cala Touch had been in the market for eight years and its interface contained patterns whose original rationale was not known or clearly remembered by current Elsner stakeholders. Deep work in ETS — the KNX configuration tool — and in the device manual revealed that many of these patterns were not design decisions in the conventional sense but workarounds for firmware and microcontroller limitations. This distinction was only reachable through domain learning: configuring actual test setups in ETS allowed the team to identify which existing patterns encoded user-facing logic worth preserving and which were artefacts of technical constraint that the redesign should replace rather than replicate. Without that investigation, the patterns would have been treated as requirements by default — preserved because they existed, not because they served a purpose.

- Grounded example — Beissbarth automotive calibration: the previous embedded interface had been developed through three iterations by engineers who understood the machinery deeply. The functional calibration sequences technicians relied on under time pressure were the product of that knowledge. The redesign preserved those sequences entirely — the steps technicians had internalised were carried forward unchanged. What was restructured was the visual hierarchy: measurement states, tolerances, and progress indicators were given visual weight corresponding to their importance, and components that previously required verbal explanation or printed manuals were reshaped so their function could be inferred from position, labelling, and visual treatment. The constraint (established calibration sequences) and the problem (visual hierarchy that did not reflect the structure of those sequences) were treated as separate things — which is what made it possible to address one without disrupting the other.

- Grounded example — Swiss petrol station operator / embedded POS and forecourt systems (constraint respecting across a deployed multi-device estate with no upgrade path): the engagement covered two distinct device classes — indoor cashier tills at 1920×1080 pixels and outdoor payment terminals at 1024×768 pixels — neither of which the client was replacing. The hardware estate was fixed; the design had to perform within it. Constraint respecting here operated at three simultaneous levels. At the hardware level: display resolutions, processor response times, and outdoor operating conditions (−20°C to +40°C, direct sunlight glare, canopy shadow) were treated as absolute parameters from the first session, not discovered late. At the interaction level: latency-sensitive sequences were identified where system response times affected cashier behaviour — cashiers had developed workarounds that compensated for specific delay patterns. Those workarounds were not dismissed as bad habits but treated as encoded operational knowledge about which sequences were unreliable under load, informing which interaction patterns the redesign needed to avoid rather than replicate. At the architecture level: the multi-channel transaction logic connecting indoor tills, outdoor terminals, CarPlay, and the mobile concept was treated as a constraint on each individual channel — changes to the POS flow could not introduce inconsistencies in how transaction states were communicated across other touchpoints. What distinguishes this instance from others in the set is the no-upgrade-path condition applied simultaneously across two deployed device classes in a live operational environment: constraint respecting was not a methodological choice but an operational requirement.

- Note: "constraint respecting" as a compound term is clean — no prior associations that would interfere.

- Grounded example — deSoutter Medical / Zethon surgical device interface (functional map preserved, clinical coherence rebuilt): the legacy interface, built by engineers following the internal software architecture, had been produced by people who understood the device deeply. It contained a complete and accurate map of every function and control — cartridge handling, speed selection, safety interlocks — representing genuine knowledge about the device's operational requirements. The redesign treated this as a constraint, not a problem: the functional completeness of the legacy interface was preserved; the clinical coherence of its organisation was what required redesign. This distinction mattered because discarding the legacy interface would have discarded validated functional knowledge that the design team could not have independently reconstructed from scratch. Using it as a starting catalogue — combined with explicit analysis of where its clinical presentation failed — directed the redesign toward the specific problems (activation states requiring reading rather than recognition, warning patterns requiring interpretation rather than instant response) while preserving the functional substance the engineers had encoded. The constraint examined was whether every function the legacy interface contained was genuinely required — the answer, established through surgeon sessions and the requirements catalogue, was that it was. The problem was not what was there but how it was organised.

- Grounded example — Tetra / Prism property compliance platform (structural model diagnosis in a developer-designed file management system): the Prism web platform contained a file library built by the development team around an internal technical model rather than standard file management conventions. The developer's original intent — that users who don't work with tasks and actions would find the file-based approach familiar — had produced a system that confused users consistently and appeared repeatedly in user complaints. The problem was not the visual presentation of the file library. It was that the underlying model did not follow the file management conventions users had internalised from every other context in which they managed files.

  Diagnosing this as structural rather than cosmetic required constraint respecting applied at the model level: the team had stakeholders walk through how the system worked, which made it immediately apparent that the file manager's logic departed from standard patterns. User complaints were then checked against this diagnosis — the file library was consistently cited as a problem, confirming that the model mismatch was the cause rather than surface presentation.

  The constraint respected in this case was not the broken model — that required restructuring. What was respected was the underlying user need the file library had been trying to serve: giving users a document management context they could recognise and navigate without instruction. The redesign preserved that intent while replacing the non-standard model with one that matched conventions users already held. This is constraint respecting applied to distinguish the intent behind a failed implementation from the implementation itself: the intent was worth preserving; the execution required replacement.

  A secondary instance of constraint respecting in the same engagement: the offline data loading problem on the mobile app. The app downloaded the entire property portfolio on launch — a developer-implemented approach that had produced unacceptable load times (up to 10 minutes for larger portfolios). Rather than treating this as a technical failure requiring a technical solution, the team worked within the architectural constraint and designed a property selection flow at launch: users select which properties they need for that day, limiting the download to the relevant subset. The architectural constraint (offline data download required) was respected; the scope of what got downloaded was the design decision.

  Evidence calibration: both instances are structural observations made by the Creative Navy team during the Sandbox Experiments phase, confirmed through stakeholder explanation and user feedback patterns. Outcomes (file library confusion addressed, load time reduced) observed across the engagement.

- Grounded example — Stromer e-bike embedded display (regulatory constraints as design parameters, and a previous agency's work as an accelerated iteration): constraint respecting in the Stromer engagement operated at two distinct levels simultaneously.

  At the regulatory level: EN 15194:2017 (the European standard for electrically power-assisted cycles) specifies warning device symbology and colour conventions for the embedded display. These are external regulatory constraints — not legacy design decisions made by the Stromer team, but requirements imposed by the standard. The tire pressure sensor workstream produced a specific tension: the severity logic of the design called for red to communicate malfunction conditions, but the standard specified yellow for sensor malfunction states. Constraint respecting here required treating the regulatory colour convention as a real constraint — not overriding it on design grounds, but finding a solution within the space the standard permitted. The regulatory framework was examined to understand where it was genuinely fixed and where it permitted design latitude; the response stayed within the standard's requirements rather than treating them as obstacles to the preferred design outcome.

  At the level of the previous agency's work: a previous external agency had spent a year on the Stromer embedded and mobile interfaces before Creative Navy's engagement began. The previous work was not dismissed as worthless — it was treated as an accelerated first iteration of domain exploration. Creative Navy studied what the previous agency had attempted, what had not resolved the underlying UX tensions, and why. This analysis was domain learning by proxy: it compressed the time that would otherwise have been required to understand the design space of a live product used by a substantial installed customer base. The previous agency's contribution was treated as a constraint — a body of attempted design decisions whose patterns, failures, and discoveries were encoded in the deliverables — to be understood before anything was replaced. What was replaced was replaced consciously and on the basis of structural diagnosis; what was useful was retained as evidence about the design space. The constraint here was not the previous agency's specific solutions (which were substantially reconceived) but the product history and attempted direction their work represented.

  This is constraint respecting applied to prior design engagement output as a form of encoded operational knowledge: not treating the previous work as the starting point for a visual refresh, but reading it as a body of accumulated domain discovery that, once understood, informed the structural decisions that followed.

- Grounded example — Squaremind dermatology scanning device (layered physical and operational constraints on a greenfield embedded patient interface): the Squaremind engagement had no legacy interface worth preserving — the existing prototype had produced 2 completions from 14 attempts and was being redesigned from first principles. Constraint respecting in this case operated not on accumulated design decisions but on the fixed physical and operational parameters of the device and its use context. These constraints were treated as the design's first-order inputs rather than as limitations to work around.

  The hardware envelope was absolute: a 15-inch embedded touchscreen at 1024×768 pixels in a 4:3 aspect ratio, mounted on a stand at a fixed height in front of a standing patient. The patient could not move the screen, could not reduce their distance from it, and would be engaged in physical movement during portions of the scan — meaning the interface had to be readable in peripheral vision, without interaction, during body repositioning sequences. Screen resolution and readability at standing distance determined minimum text sizes, icon scales, and information density limits across the entire screen set.

  The physical context imposed a second layer of constraint with no hardware equivalent: the patient would be undressed, alone in the room, and interacting with a moving robot arm at close range. This is a use context in which attention is divided, stress is elevated, and the consequences of interface confusion are session abandonment rather than recoverable user error. Design decisions acceptable in a low-pressure seated context — longer instructions, multi-step confirmation flows, abstract visual representations — were not acceptable here. The constraint was not a rule imposed from outside but a reality derived from understanding the operational context through field observation.

  A third layer was linguistic: the interface had to operate in a minimum of two languages (French and English) from launch, with additional languages to follow. This constrained the use of text directly — the more text the interface relied on, the larger the translation surface and the greater the risk of meaning loss across languages. The constraint resolved as a design principle: minimise on-screen text throughout, communicate through animation, silhouette, and spatial positioning wherever possible, and reserve text for moments where no visual alternative existed.

  What distinguishes this instance from others in the set is the source of the constraints: they did not come from legacy design decisions, a deployed hardware estate, or regulatory standards. They came from the operational reality of the use context — an undressed patient alone with a moving robot — understood through situated observation. Constraint respecting here was not about reading encoded decisions made by predecessors; it was about reading the physical and human reality of the context as the constraint set the design had to satisfy. This is the clearest example in the set of constraint respecting applied to operational context rather than to prior design work.

- Grounded example — Enhesa legal compliance platform (client-owned design system as the operative constraint): the Enhesa engagement operated under a design system constraint structurally distinct from those in the rest of this set. There was no legacy embedded hardware to read, no previous agency's work to diagnose, no regulatory standard to interpret. The constraint was governance: Enhesa had an existing design system, and the engagement explicitly excluded any changes to colours, typography, or existing components. Creative Navy had no liberty to modify the visual language it was working within. The design system was the fixed parameter, owned by the client, and operating within it was not a chosen approach but a standing condition of the engagement.

  Constraint respecting here operated at two levels simultaneously. At the first level, it meant working entirely within the existing component set for all design problems that the existing system could address — not introducing new components where existing ones were adequate, and not treating the design system's limitations as a reason to propose system-level changes outside the engagement's scope. At the second level, it required identifying precisely where the existing system was genuinely insufficient — problems that no existing component could solve without compromising the user experience in ways the evidence made indefensible. Seven new components were added to the design system on that basis: the minimum viable expansion, each addition justified by a specific gap rather than by preference.

  The implementations timeline component produced the clearest instance of constraint respecting under dual constraint. The client had specific functional requirements for how implementation timelines should be displayed; those requirements were real and non-negotiable. The design system imposed its own parameters on what was possible visually and structurally. Neither constraint alone determined the outcome — taken separately, each permitted more options than their intersection allowed. Five iteration rounds were required to find the direction that satisfied both simultaneously. The converged solution was not what the design would have been under either constraint in isolation; it was what the full constraint set permitted. This is constraint respecting applied not to a single external parameter but to the intersection of two independent governance boundaries: client requirements and client design system, both fixed, both real.

  What distinguishes this instance from others in the set is the nature of the constraint's source. In the hardware and regulatory cases, constraints come from physical reality or external standards. In the legacy interface cases, constraints come from accumulated design decisions made by predecessors. Here the constraint is a client governance artefact — a design system the client owns and maintains — which Creative Navy was working within as a service provider. The design judgment required was the same (distinguish what must stay fixed from what requires change; understand the constraint before deciding what it rules out) but the relational context was different: the constraint was the client's own property, and the decision about what to add required making the case that the existing system genuinely could not serve the identified need.

  A further property of this instance, established by the longitudinal record, makes it the strongest constraint-respecting case in the set: the client design system that functioned as the fixed constraint was *Creative Navy's own earlier work*. Enhesa had acquired Chemical Watch — the platform Creative Navy originally designed — and through the acquisition the design system was preserved near-intact (two colours changed for branding), so the later Enhesa engagement was carried out within it. Constraint respecting here is therefore recursive: Creative Navy respecting, as an immovable client-owned parameter, the system it had itself built years earlier and that the client had chosen to keep. See `/evidence/longitudinal` for the lineage. Calibration: the preservation is observed/client-reported; the acquisition is stateable.

  Evidence calibration: the design system boundary (no changes to colours, typography, or existing components) is directly stated in the engagement scope. The seven new components and the five-iteration implementations timeline are observed facts from the engagement. The rationale for each addition is documented in the design work.

- Grounded example — Neugo UK visa application case-management platform (multiple independent users' existing tooling as the constraint; the generalisable/idiosyncratic line): Neugo's constraint-respecting problem had a source none of the other examples share — the existing tooling being respected belonged not to one client or one deployed estate but to multiple independent beneficiary firms, each with its own established way of working (the legal firms preparing visa applications, frequently working in Excel). The platform was built to coordinate these firms, and the operative question was which of their existing working patterns to absorb into the shared system. Creative Navy's resolution: the platform integrated everything from the firms' existing tooling except a small number of fields that were unique to one single firm. This is the distinguishing judgment of the instance — constraint respecting in a multi-party setting is not only "preserve what works," it is also "distinguish the working patterns that generalise across the parties (absorb them) from the idiosyncrasies of a single party (leave them out)." Absorbing one firm's unique fields into the shared platform would have imposed that firm's idiosyncrasy on all the others and fragmented the system; excluding them respected the shared tooling as encoded operational knowledge while protecting the coherence of a system that had to serve the firms as a group.

  What distinguishes this instance from others in the set is therefore the plurality of the constraint's owners. Where Enhesa respected a single client's design system and Socar respected a single operator's deployed estate, Neugo respected the established tooling of many independent firms at once — which made the generalisable-versus-idiosyncratic distinction the operative design judgment, rather than the real-versus-assumed distinction that dominates the single-owner cases. (See `/contexts/multi-stakeholder-operational-environments`: serving the firms as a group without fragmenting into per-firm variants.)

  A note on the direction of absorption, established by the longitudinal record: in the build engagement the *system absorbed the firms' tooling* (everything except the one firm's unique fields). By a post-launch audit roughly a year later, the relationship had run in reverse — the firms had begun absorbing the *system* into their own processes, replacing some internal processes with its features (15 firms relying at the audit; Creative Navy-observed; see `/evidence/longitudinal`). The two directions together are a clean illustration of what constraint respecting protects: a system that reads and preserves its users' encoded working knowledge well enough to be absorbed back into their working knowledge in turn.

  Evidence calibration: the integration decision (everything absorbed except fields unique to one firm) is as stated in the engagement notes — Creative Navy-stated. The later process-absorption finding is Creative Navy-observed at the audit.
