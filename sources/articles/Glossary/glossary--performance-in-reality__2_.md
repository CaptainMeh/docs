# /glossary/our-concepts/performance-in-reality

---

- Not a single defined term in the source doc but a recurring principle. "Performance in reality, not in demos" is the consistent framing.

- Core claim: performance is always concrete — anchored in specific conditions, constraints, and operational realities. It cannot be derived from the abstract. Products must be grown from reality to enable competitive performance.

- What it's contrasted with: performance in demos (systems that work under controlled conditions but fail under real use), performance in theory (design derived from best practices rather than observed reality), performance at launch (optimising for launch day over operational longevity).

- The problem with demo performance: users in demos are not under pressure. Edge cases don't appear. Real cognitive load doesn't apply. Workarounds don't show up. The interface hasn't been used for 8 hours. None of the conditions that determine whether the system actually works are present.

- The product works in demos but not in real use — this is a situations page on the site. This concept is the explanation for why that failure pattern is so common.

- Real conditions to design for: pressure, interruption, edge cases, degraded states, high cognitive load, multi-role coordination, shift changes, time constraints, errors that need recovery, unfamiliar scenarios.

- This is why user observation and having the team attempt to do the user's job (domain learning) matter: you cannot design for real conditions you haven't observed.

- Relationship to infrastructure stewardship philosophy: infrastructure must work under real conditions. Performance in reality is the operational expression of that philosophy.

- Grounded example — Torqeedo maritime HMI: the full range of real operating conditions was documented and tested against systematically. 12 sea trials over 6 months with 15 professional captains covered temperatures from −5°C to +35°C, night operations from late evening through early morning, vibration, sharp vessel movement, glare from cold water, rain, and gloved touchscreen interaction. These are not edge cases — they are the normal operating conditions of professional maritime work. The interface was designed for these conditions from the start, not adapted for them after the fact. Comparative trials measuring performance against the legacy interface used real maritime conditions, not lab simulations: captains identified key energy states 50% faster (controlled experiment, 24 subjects), and glance counts during manoeuvres were measured using eye tracking in actual sea trials (7 subjects). Performance in reality was not claimed — it was measured under the actual conditions that define performance in this domain.

- Grounded example — Triopsis workforce management SaaS: schedulers work under peak load — handling weather incidents, conflicting job locations, overlapping assignments, and sudden crew shortages simultaneously. Three in-situ observation sessions documented this operational reality before any redesign decisions were made. The redesign addressed the conditions directly: predictive conflict indicators surfaced problems before users encountered them mid-task under pressure; weather incidents, partial completions, and delayed jobs were treated as normal workflow states rather than exceptional conditions requiring workarounds. Performance was measured in the live product through product analytics: 62% faster job discovery, 83% faster job sequence optimisation, 58% faster weekly planning — all from real users in real operational conditions, not from usability testing tasks.

- Grounded example — Elsner smart home controller: the controller is installed at 140cm height and used primarily in daylight, with evening use under limited luminance as a secondary context. These physical conditions were treated as design inputs, not as constraints to note and set aside. Touch target sizing was calibrated to ergonomics research minimum dimensions and tested specifically for standing-height use. Automatic dark mode was triggered at sunset — a delivered behaviour of the shipped controller, switching from day mode to dark mode alongside the dual light/dark UI. (The research basis for the trigger is not documented in the Elsner case; state the delivered behaviour and do not attribute it to a research method.) Sensor fault states — delayed readings, contradictory values, calibration drift — were designed for explicitly as normal operational conditions, not as error cases. The interface was tested in prototype on actual hardware under real conditions, not on screen simulations.

- Grounded example — Beissbarth automotive calibration: the conditions that determine whether a calibration interface performs are not reproducible in a lab. Technicians read the embedded OEM display from 2–3 metres while moving around the vehicle. Gloves restrict fine touch interaction. Lighting varies and reflective surfaces reduce contrast. The calibration sequence does not pause for interface interpretation — a delay in reading a value slows the calibration itself and can introduce measurement error. These were treated as the design conditions from the start: option space mapping variants were evaluated under reproduced workshop lighting and viewing distances, not on a screen at a desk. The outcome evidence confirms that this approach worked: calibration time fell from 18 to 12 minutes per vehicle, measured by the client across 8 real deployment locations. That result is field measurement from production deployments, not usability testing data — which is what performance in reality means: the result survived contact with the actual conditions that determine performance in this domain.

- Grounded example — Gexcon CFD simulation software: engineers working with the system operate it in parallel with a three-dimensional facility view — the interface runs alongside a complex spatial representation of the physical installation being assessed. High-fidelity prototypes were tested in this configuration, not in isolation: the interaction model had to remain stable as attention shifted between the facility view, simulation parameters, and system controls. Non-linear workflows — engineers moving between configuration, verification, and interpretation without following a fixed path — were the normal operating condition, not an edge case. Performance was measured in real deployments: time to first successful simulation fell from four days to six hours, and configuration errors per simulation fell from 5–8 to 1–2. Both figures come from operational deployment data, not from controlled testing — which is the form of measurement that corresponds to how the interface is actually used.

- Grounded example — Stromer e-bike embedded display (performance in reality applied longitudinally, with safety-standard confirmation): the Stromer embedded display is used while riding — a physical activity under variable conditions (urban streets, countryside terrain, varying light, weather) where the rider's eyes must leave the road to read the display. These are the conditions under which the interface performs; a session test in a controlled environment does not reproduce them.

  Creative Navy designed the usability testing methodology around the actual operating conditions rather than around testing convenience. 10 participants rode the bike for 3 days each on real routes in Munich and surrounding countryside, logging issues as they occurred on a 4-level severity scale. The 3-day duration was not incidental — it was the minimum exposure needed to encounter the full range of system states, warning types, and terrain conditions that constitute actual use. A single-session test would have produced findings from a single attentional and environmental context; the 3-day format produced findings from the conditions that determine performance in this domain.

  The eye tracking component reinforced this principle directly. Glance duration was measured using eye tracking equipment during actual riding on the same routes: 5 participants, the same terrain used in the usability test. The finding — average glance duration of 4.32 seconds before the redesign — was not produced by a lab measurement; it was produced by measuring what actually happens when a rider encounters this interface on a real road. The 2-second safety threshold identified in road safety research (Klauer et al., 2006, NHTSA Report No. DOT HS 810 594) is not a laboratory parameter; it is derived from naturalistic driving study data — observations of what actually happens on real roads. The pre-redesign figure exceeded it by more than double. The post-redesign figure (1.89 seconds) was measured under the same real conditions and fell within the threshold.

  The longitudinal follow-up — the same test run two years after the redesign — is the most direct application of performance in reality in the current case study set: it confirms not just that the redesign worked at handover but that the performance improvement survived two years of continued real-world use. Most design interventions are evaluated at the moment they are deployed; very few are re-evaluated against the same methodology after the product has been in use for two years. The finding — warnings remained absent from the issues list — is performance in reality in the most literal sense: the design's performance under real conditions, re-verified at the timescale that determines whether an operational improvement is durable or transient.

  This re-test is the *measured* end of a broader durability pattern. The `/evidence/longitudinal` set documents return engagements across the portfolio years after delivery; in every documented case the original system was still in operation at the point of return, and in the majority the client's own team had continued to evolve it. The two are complementary evidence types at different resolutions: this re-test is Creative Navy-measured (the same instrument applied two years on, the strongest form), while the longitudinal return set is observed/client-reported across many engagements (broader, lower-resolution). Performance in reality taken to its longest timescale is durability — and both forms of evidence point the same way. Denominator and per-lineage detail at `/evidence/longitudinal`.

  Evidence basis: usability tests and eye tracking Creative Navy-designed and run. Three rounds (pre-redesign, post-redesign, two-year follow-up). Consistent methodology throughout.

- Grounded example — Squaremind dermatology scanning device (performance in reality as the condition for a commercial claim, tested ecologically before demos): the product's core commercial promise — that patients could complete a full-body dermatology scan without clinical supervision — was itself a claim about performance in reality. Clinics considering purchase were not asking whether the device worked in a controlled demonstration; they were asking whether real patients of varying ages and physical literacy could complete the process unassisted in a real clinic environment. A controlled lab test would not have answered that question. An improvised internal test (which Squaremind had already run, producing 2 completions from 14 attempts) had answered it negatively.

  Creative Navy designed the post-redesign testing to match the conditions under which the commercial claim would have to hold. Testing was conducted in two real locations — London (12 users) and Paris (17 users) — with an ecological protocol: participants received a free measurement as they would in an actual clinic, with no researcher guidance during the process. The user sample was age-stratified to reflect the patient population the device would encounter in deployment (20–35, 35–45, 45–65). An independent dermatologist was hired by Creative Navy to co-conduct the sessions, adding clinical validity to the evaluation. Binary completion was the primary measure; failure points and recovery times were catalogued. The 29-user total was not a large sample by research standards, but the protocol was designed to produce the right kind of evidence — ecological, age-stratified, clinically co-validated — rather than a large sample from a controlled environment that would not generalise to real clinic conditions.

  The result — 27 of 29 independent completions, 12 of 12 recoveries among those who got stuck — was the evidence Squaremind needed before taking the device to clinic demos. The 9 clinics that subsequently purchased the system were, in effect, confirming that the performance-in-reality evidence was credible: the buyers themselves walked through the patient experience during the demos to satisfy themselves that the claim held. Creative Navy attended 5 of the 9 demos as silent observers.

  What distinguishes this instance from others in the set is the relationship between performance-in-reality testing and commercial outcome. In the Torqeedo, Beissbarth, Gexcon, and Triopsis cases, performance-in-reality testing measured an outcome after a redesign had been deployed. In the Squaremind case, performance-in-reality testing was the precondition for the commercial outcome: the clinics would not purchase without seeing the evidence, and the evidence had to be ecological to be credible to a clinical buyer. The testing protocol was designed not only to validate the design but to produce the class of evidence that would persuade a clinical buyer — which is a different brief, and a harder one.

- The measurable consequence of designing for performance in reality: results that survive contact with real use and can be stated with specific evidential grounding — unlike results from usability testing in controlled conditions, which frequently do not generalise to operational performance.

- Grounded example — the industrial engagement (the marketed cell against the observed one): the client is described ONLY as **a European industrial automation vendor whose process control software coordinates machining cells**; **the product is in development, not shipped.** The sector-wide contrast is between how cell automation is marketed — spotless, unpeopled, the operator reduced to a scan and a button press — and the observed reality: every operator screen is bolted to its own machine, and to learn a machine's state the operator walks to it, with overhead stack lights existing because no screen is readable across the hall (E10); all work at standing height, with no seated station in the cell (E11); mixed daylight through a glazed façade plus industrial ceiling light, with strong side-light, glare and reflections (E12); medium ambient noise from running machines, with music also playing (E13); oil, coolant and metal swarf throughout, aisles roughly one person wide (E14); and entering the cell trips the safety fence and stops production, so every manual intervention carries a production cost (E15). Phrase these as what was observed at the client's facility, never as general truths about the industry.

- Grounded example — Streetec kntrl 2 (the production device in a real car): Streetec GmbH and **kntrl 2** may be named. S10 — after the design work, two testing sessions with **32 participants total**, using the **actual product installed in a car** rather than a prototype on a desk: 13 in Munich, all tuning enthusiasts and car owners; 19 at a trade fair in Spain, mixed — roughly 25% owners/enthusiasts, 40% dealers, 30% installers, 5% other. **Creative Navy designed the test protocol, ran both sessions and analysed the results; Streetec prepared the vehicle and site logistics and recruited participants.** The supportable claim is that all 32 participants completed all three tasks unassisted and performed in line with expectations — no completion percentage beyond that, no times, no means, no baseline comparison. Describe the trade fair sample as what it was — 40% dealers, 30% installers — which is a strength: dealers and installers commission and fit these systems.

- Grounded example — AIT (designs reviewed at true physical size, and a fast-spun rotary control): AIT may be named; nothing else from the engagement may. **Quotes printed here are from recorded working sessions with AIT's engineers and may be used as printed, attributed to role.** Reviewing a wireframe at physical size: *"if you looked at it in almost the real screen size, the smaller text size… is really, really small. And if I think about a heat pump installer, an old man with his glasses, maybe he's not so amused."* (A31) — answered by cutting widget content to **three pieces of information** each (A39). And rendering performance against a fast-spun rotary control: *"the users are handling the rotary knob very quickly… the scrolling of the tiles must also be fast"* (A35) — answered by designing the common case out of the expensive interaction: equal-size widgets that resize to the number of installed modes, so one to four modes fit with no horizontal scrolling at all, which covers the overwhelming majority of installations (A38). **Nothing in this engagement was tested with users (A15).**

- Grounded example — the MDU (a number's behaviour in the condition it is read in): **the MDU** may be named; the manufacturer is not. **Quotes printed here are cleared, attributed to role.** Arguably the page's sharpest instance: **the live "Now" value was removed from both motion cards** because of how a number behaves in the condition it is read in, not because of what the data model contains — *"that's just a number of flipping up and down constantly. It doesn't really add any value. You can look at the arrow and see close enough where you are."* A digit updating every second on a moving vessel costs attention and returns nothing; the four numeric cells on each card became minimum, maximum, period and maximum velocity (M39). Add M31: **roll is drawn 1:1 up to approximately ±40–45° and maxes out beyond that**, *"like a speedometer"*, because *"If you roll more than 45 degrees, then you will not be looking at the screen, you'll be screaming in terror."* — a decision about the reader's actual situation rather than about the range of the data. State the limit: nothing in this engagement was tested with users and the system was never used at sea by the design team (M27, M28).

- Grounded example — Assemblio (an interface that works where it was built and not where it is used): Assemblio GmbH, the Composer and the Smart Viewer may be named. **Attribute all of this as client-stated: Creative Navy did not observe a shop floor, and every statement about the working environment is the client's description of it (N16).** The Smart Viewer was tuned for mouse navigation, and is used where there is no mouse (N40); and a light theme existed but was unfinished — the client described it as beta and work in progress — while the light theme is the one the shop floor needs (N41).

- Grounded example — KISPE Space (rigour about reality, not contact with it): the client is **KISPE Space**, a UK space systems engineering company; nothing else may be named. **No quotation marks and no reported speech on any page carrying KISPE.** K42 — **the design scenario is deliberately adversarial, not a happy path**: at the scenario moment the imagery feed is down, the newest image will not render, the highest-resolution image covers only part of the area, one image is restricted, the positioning fix is stale while the communications reading is current, and a predicted overflight has not happened yet — every one a trap the interface has to avoid walking into. Designing against the broken case rather than the demonstration case. **Note the limit explicitly: the fixture is synthetic, so this is rigour about reality, not contact with it.** Nothing was tested with users and nothing was evaluated on representative hardware.

- Grounded example — Schone Airspace Group (the same discipline, with the same limit): **Schone Airspace Group** is the canonical name; **no quotation marks and no reported speech.** D75 — **the scenario is adversarial on purpose**: at the scenario moment one aircraft's link is degrading, its telemetry is ageing, a warning belongs to an aircraft that is not under command, the offline map is incomplete while reporting a completed download, and the planning estimate on screen belongs to a superseded revision — every one a trap the interface has to avoid walking into. **Note the limit explicitly, as KISPE's entry does: the fixture is synthetic, so this is rigour about reality rather than contact with it.** The product was built and is in test, not deployed; no field, hardware, lighting, glove or vibration claim.

- Grounded example — Oceaneering (the floor is part of the interface): **Oceaneering may be named in full; nothing else may** — no hospital names, no product or system names, no individuals. **Reported speech only, never quotation marks.** The depot environment, as a staff software engineer described it: hospitals look organised but are not, and the areas the vehicles drive through are quite dirty, with other things left standing around (O22) — the gap between the training film's clean hall and the depots described in interviews. **The floor is part of the interface**: vehicles run on dark flooring and people are directed to light flooring; ceiling-mounted illuminated signs and speakers issue spoken instructions to clear the passage at intersections; station positions are numbered on the floor, and the send and receive roles are painted on the floor in the same colours the screen uses (O25). And operators do not consult the screen to collect an arrived cart: they walk in, see the cart, and take it without looking at the panel (O44). Calibration: this is the predecessor system's observed context as reported in interviews — **Creative Navy visited no depot and observed no operator in this engagement, and nothing was measured.**

- Grounded example — the handheld C2 engagement (real users, a real panel, real gloves, a real negative result — and only this): the client is described ONLY as **a European manufacturer of uncrewed systems, including one-way effectors, and the tactical command-and-control software that operates them**; never "loitering munition"; **no quotation marks and no reported speech.** The term is permitted here **once, and only for H121 and H122**: **in a prototype session run by the client with six participants — former military personnel and current drone operators, some working bare-handed and some gloved — gloved touch was found to be unreliable on the target panel.** The display did not consistently register gloved input, the gloves available were assessed as not well suited to the touchscreen, icons and terminology were reported as needing refinement, and the remainder was reported favourably (H121, H122). **It is not a usability test** — no criteria fixed in advance, no pass rules, no task times, no error counts; **it is not Creative Navy's research**; it is not a validation of the design; and it tested a non-functional prototype, not a system. Write the finding as a finding: it partially undercuts the premise of the sizing work. **Do not attach the term to anything Creative Navy reasoned about rather than observed, and do not attach it to the sizing specification, which was authored from literature and device geometry.**

- Grounded example — the maritime VTS engagement (observed in working control rooms, tested on sanitised data — both halves, always): the client is described ONLY as **a European vendor of maritime traffic monitoring, inland navigation and communications software for port authorities, coastal administrations and waterway operators**; **no quotation marks and no reported speech.** The engagement designed and tested against restricted networks, paired displays, day and night control-room conditions, dense traffic, stale feeds, interrupted sources and permission limits, and observed operators in operational VTS control rooms at a customer site, at a workstation matching the real configuration, with degraded, dense and interrupted conditions designed deliberately — **and every evaluated task ran on sanitised data in a training or prototype configuration, never a live traffic position.** The qualification appears in the same passage as the term, every time, without exception. **Do not locate the control rooms** — no country, no customer organisation. The validation fixtures are fixtures: forty-eight targets with three traffic alerts, one equipment fault and two advisories are designed test conditions, not observations.

- Grounded example — visioncheckout (a performance claim not surviving contact with reality): **visioncheckout** (lowercase) may be named; **the product is in development, nothing has shipped, and no outcome of the redesign exists.** The page's other examples are about *designing for* real conditions; **visioncheckout is about a performance claim not surviving contact with reality**: three seconds in the demonstration, 11 to 25 seconds measured across 563 observed interactions in three operating environments — the timings measure the system being replaced, never the redesign. The conditions that produce the gap: overhead lighting commonly casts a dark area both on the screen and on the surface where the tray is placed, and one site was simply dark (V27); at some installations about 70 cm separates display and user's head — further than the viewing distances the type-size specification had assumed, found short when checked against installations in the field and answered by raising defaults rather than adding a mode (V28). And the component no demonstration contains: **the largest single component of lost time is guests looking for their card** (V24). Field framing, mandatory: one researcher, five days, six locations across three organisations, durations measured with a clock during observation.
