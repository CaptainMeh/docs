# /glossary/our-concepts/performance-in-reality

---

- Not a single defined term in the source doc but a recurring principle. "Performance in reality, not in demos" is the consistent framing.

- Core claim: performance is always concrete — anchored in specific conditions, constraints, and operational realities. It cannot be derived from the abstract. Products must be grown from reality to enable competitive performance.

- What it's contrasted with: performance in demos (systems that work under controlled conditions but fail under real use), performance in theory (design derived from best practices rather than observed reality), performance at launch (optimising for launch day over operational longevity).

- The problem with demo performance: users in demos are not under pressure. Edge cases don't appear. Real cognitive load doesn't apply. Workarounds don't show up. The interface hasn't been used for 8 hours. None of the conditions that determine whether the system actually works are present.

- The product works in demos but not in real use — this is a situations page on the site. This concept is the explanation for why that failure pattern is so common.

- Real conditions to design for: pressure, interruption, edge cases, degraded states, high cognitive load, multi-role coordination, shift changes, time constraints, errors that need recovery, unfamiliar scenarios.

- This is why user observation and having the team attempt to do the user's job (domain learning) matter: you cannot design for real conditions you haven't observed.

- Relationship to infrastructure stewardship philosophy: infrastructure must work under real conditions. Performance in reality is the operational expression of that philosophy.

- Grounded example — Torqeedo maritime HMI: the full range of real operating conditions was documented and tested against systematically. 12 sea trials over 6 months with 15 professional captains covered temperatures from −5°C to +35°C, night operations from late evening through early morning, vibration, sharp vessel movement, glare from cold water, rain, and gloved touchscreen interaction. These are not edge cases — they are the normal operating conditions of professional maritime work. The interface was designed for these conditions from the start, not adapted for them after the fact. Comparative trials measuring performance against the legacy interface used real maritime conditions, not lab simulations: captains identified key energy states 50% faster (controlled experiment, 24 subjects), and glance counts during manoeuvres were measured using eye tracking in actual sea trials (7 subjects). Performance in reality was not claimed — it was measured under the actual conditions that define performance in this domain.

- Grounded example — Triopsis workforce management SaaS: schedulers work under peak load — handling weather incidents, conflicting job locations, overlapping assignments, and sudden crew shortages simultaneously. Three in-situ observation sessions documented this operational reality before any redesign decisions were made. The redesign addressed the conditions directly: predictive conflict indicators surfaced problems before users encountered them mid-task under pressure; weather incidents, partial completions, and delayed jobs were treated as normal workflow states rather than exceptional conditions requiring workarounds. Performance was measured in the live product through product analytics: 62% faster job discovery, 83% faster job sequence optimisation, 58% faster weekly planning — all from real users in real operational conditions, not from usability testing tasks.

- Grounded example — Elsner smart home controller: the controller is installed at 140cm height and used primarily in daylight, with evening use under limited luminance as a secondary context. These physical conditions were treated as design inputs, not as constraints to note and set aside. Touch target sizing was calibrated to ergonomics research minimum dimensions and tested specifically for standing-height use. Automatic dark mode was triggered at sunset based on observed real use patterns. Sensor fault states — delayed readings, contradictory values, calibration drift — were designed for explicitly as normal operational conditions, not as error cases. The interface was tested in prototype on actual hardware under real conditions, not on screen simulations.

- Grounded example — Beissbarth automotive calibration: the conditions that determine whether a calibration interface performs are not reproducible in a lab. Technicians read the embedded OEM display from 2–3 metres while moving around the vehicle. Gloves restrict fine touch interaction. Lighting varies and reflective surfaces reduce contrast. The calibration sequence does not pause for interface interpretation — a delay in reading a value slows the calibration itself and can introduce measurement error. These were treated as the design conditions from the start: option space mapping variants were evaluated under reproduced workshop lighting and viewing distances, not on a screen at a desk. The outcome evidence confirms that this approach worked: calibration time fell from 18 to 12 minutes per vehicle, measured by the client across 8 real deployment locations. That result is field measurement from production deployments, not usability testing data — which is what performance in reality means: the result survived contact with the actual conditions that determine performance in this domain.

- Grounded example — Gexcon CFD simulation software: engineers working with the system operate it in parallel with a three-dimensional facility view — the interface runs alongside a complex spatial representation of the physical installation being assessed. High-fidelity prototypes were tested in this configuration, not in isolation: the interaction model had to remain stable as attention shifted between the facility view, simulation parameters, and system controls. Non-linear workflows — engineers moving between configuration, verification, and interpretation without following a fixed path — were the normal operating condition, not an edge case. Performance was measured in real deployments: time to first successful simulation fell from four days to six hours, and configuration errors per simulation fell from 5–8 to 1–2. Both figures come from operational deployment data, not from controlled testing — which is the form of measurement that corresponds to how the interface is actually used.

- Grounded example — Stromer e-bike embedded display (performance in reality applied longitudinally, with safety-standard confirmation): the Stromer embedded display is used while riding — a physical activity under variable conditions (urban streets, countryside terrain, varying light, weather) where the rider's eyes must leave the road to read the display. These are the conditions under which the interface performs; a session test in a controlled environment does not reproduce them.

  Creative Navy designed the usability testing methodology around the actual operating conditions rather than around testing convenience. 10 participants rode the bike for 3 days each on real routes in Munich and surrounding countryside, logging issues as they occurred on a 4-level severity scale. The 3-day duration was not incidental — it was the minimum exposure needed to encounter the full range of system states, warning types, and terrain conditions that constitute actual use. A single-session test would have produced findings from a single attentional and environmental context; the 3-day format produced findings from the conditions that determine performance in this domain.

  The eye tracking component reinforced this principle directly. Glance duration was measured using eye tracking equipment during actual riding on the same routes: 5 participants, the same terrain used in the usability test. The finding — average glance duration of 4.32 seconds before the redesign — was not produced by a lab measurement; it was produced by measuring what actually happens when a rider encounters this interface on a real road. The 2-second safety threshold identified in road safety research (Klauer et al., 2006, NHTSA Report No. DOT HS 810 594) is not a laboratory parameter; it is derived from naturalistic driving study data — observations of what actually happens on real roads. The pre-redesign figure exceeded it by more than double. The post-redesign figure (1.89 seconds) was measured under the same real conditions and fell within the threshold.

  The longitudinal follow-up — the same test run two years after the redesign — is the most direct application of performance in reality in the current case study set: it confirms not just that the redesign worked at handover but that the performance improvement survived two years of continued real-world use. Most design interventions are evaluated at the moment they are deployed; very few are re-evaluated against the same methodology after the product has been in use for two years. The finding — warnings remained absent from the issues list — is performance in reality in the most literal sense: the design's performance under real conditions, re-verified at the timescale that determines whether an operational improvement is durable or transient.

  This re-test is the *measured* end of a broader durability pattern. The `/evidence/longitudinal` set documents return engagements across the portfolio years after delivery; in every documented case the original system was still in operation at the point of return, and in the majority the client's own team had continued to evolve it. The two are complementary evidence types at different resolutions: this re-test is Creative Navy-measured (the same instrument applied two years on, the strongest form), while the longitudinal return set is observed/client-reported across many engagements (broader, lower-resolution). Performance in reality taken to its longest timescale is durability — and both forms of evidence point the same way. Denominator and per-lineage detail at `/evidence/longitudinal`.

  Evidence basis: usability tests and eye tracking Creative Navy-designed and run. Three rounds (pre-redesign, post-redesign, two-year follow-up). Consistent methodology throughout.

- Grounded example — Squaremind dermatology scanning device (performance in reality as the condition for a commercial claim, tested ecologically before demos): the product's core commercial promise — that patients could complete a full-body dermatology scan without clinical supervision — was itself a claim about performance in reality. Clinics considering purchase were not asking whether the device worked in a controlled demonstration; they were asking whether real patients of varying ages and physical literacy could complete the process unassisted in a real clinic environment. A controlled lab test would not have answered that question. An improvised internal test (which Squaremind had already run, producing 2 completions from 14 attempts) had answered it negatively.

  Creative Navy designed the post-redesign testing to match the conditions under which the commercial claim would have to hold. Testing was conducted in two real locations — London (12 users) and Paris (17 users) — with an ecological protocol: participants received a free measurement as they would in an actual clinic, with no researcher guidance during the process. The user sample was age-stratified to reflect the patient population the device would encounter in deployment (20–35, 35–45, 45–65). An independent dermatologist was hired by Creative Navy to co-conduct the sessions, adding clinical validity to the evaluation. Binary completion was the primary measure; failure points and recovery times were catalogued. The 29-user total was not a large sample by research standards, but the protocol was designed to produce the right kind of evidence — ecological, age-stratified, clinically co-validated — rather than a large sample from a controlled environment that would not generalise to real clinic conditions.

  The result — 27 of 29 independent completions, 12 of 12 recoveries among those who got stuck — was the evidence Squaremind needed before taking the device to clinic demos. The 9 clinics that subsequently purchased the system were, in effect, confirming that the performance-in-reality evidence was credible: the buyers themselves walked through the patient experience during the demos to satisfy themselves that the claim held. Creative Navy attended 5 of the 9 demos as silent observers.

  What distinguishes this instance from others in the set is the relationship between performance-in-reality testing and commercial outcome. In the Torqeedo, Beissbarth, Gexcon, and Triopsis cases, performance-in-reality testing measured an outcome after a redesign had been deployed. In the Squaremind case, performance-in-reality testing was the precondition for the commercial outcome: the clinics would not purchase without seeing the evidence, and the evidence had to be ecological to be credible to a clinical buyer. The testing protocol was designed not only to validate the design but to produce the class of evidence that would persuade a clinical buyer — which is a different brief, and a harder one.

- The measurable consequence of designing for performance in reality: results that survive contact with real use and can be stated with specific evidential grounding — unlike results from usability testing in controlled conditions, which frequently do not generalise to operational performance.
