# /glossary/our-concepts/blanks-phenomenon

---

- Definition from source: client intuition that is "half-right but blank of specifics." The structure of understanding is directionally correct but lacks the substance needed to make design decisions from it.

- What it looks like: a client knows their domain deeply but from one perspective. They know something is wrong with the workflow, or that users struggle with X, or that feature Y should be better — but they don't know specifically why, what exactly causes it, how it works in detail, or what would need to change to address it.

- What fills the blanks: multi-perspective synthesis. The Creative Navy team extracts the structure of the client's intuition and fills it with substance rather than with generic patterns.

- Why this matters: most design work addresses the surface expression of a problem — the stated need, the described pain point. The blanks phenomenon is the observation that the surface expression is often correct in direction but insufficient as a basis for design decisions. The underlying factors that generate the surface expression are what design must address.

- Example (from deliverables discussion): a persona that lists "experiences time pressure" as a pain point without explaining why there is time pressure, how it manifests, what generates it, how users deal with it. The surface expression ("time pressure") is noted but the blank underneath it — what actually creates it and what it means for design decisions — is not filled.

- This is why domain learning takes longer than clients expect: they don't realize their own understanding has blanks.

- The blanks phenomenon is also why workshopping is more effective than document circulation for knowledge transfer: the workshop surfaces blanks and fills them in real time through discussion. The document assumes the blanks don't exist.

- Grounded example — Triopsis multi-stakeholder governance: the client had strong domain knowledge and years of operational experience. Founders, developers, sales, support, and key clients each had directionally correct intuitions about what the product needed — planners needed throughput, operations managers needed stability, field teams needed safety, sales needed demo clarity. Each perspective was partially right. But without a UX framework to arbitrate between them, no one could make design decisions that held across all four perspectives simultaneously. The blanks were not in any individual's understanding but in the space between perspectives — the substance needed to translate competing correct intuitions into coherent interface decisions. Creative Navy's multi-perspective synthesis extracted the structure of each stakeholder's intuition and filled the gaps between them with observed user behaviour, in-situ research findings, and competitive analysis. This is a different expression of the blanks phenomenon from the individual persona example: the blanks can live in the gaps between multiple partial perspectives, not only in a single perspective's incomplete self-knowledge.

- Organisational blanks vs. individual blanks: the Triopsis case illustrates that the blanks phenomenon operates at the organisational level as well as the individual level. A client organisation can collectively possess all the domain knowledge needed to make good design decisions — and still lack the framework to synthesise it into actionable direction. The blank is structural, not informational.

- Grounded example — Gexcon CFD simulation software (internal blanks): product managers and developers understood parts of the user picture but not its full range. They knew the system well — they had built and maintained it. But their knowledge was organised around how the system worked, not around how users of very different experience levels and operational roles worked with it simultaneously. Interviews and observations revealed that senior CFD engineers, safety analysts, process engineers, newer engineers, and risk managers had different operational realities that internal perspectives had not fully mapped. The blanks here were not about missing knowledge of the domain but about missing knowledge of the space between user types — how the same interface failed differently for each group, and why the failures were not visible from inside the organisation. The research produced a picture that was more differentiated than what internal perspectives had described: not "users struggle with complexity" but specific, role-dependent failure patterns that required different design responses. This is the blanks phenomenon operating at the level of user research completeness: the direction of understanding was correct, but the specificity needed to make design decisions from it was not there.

- Grounded example — UNICEF internal planning, approval, and reporting tool (cross-tier organisational blank with an inverted client framing): this is the strongest organisational-blank instance in the set, and it adds a property the Triopsis and Gexcon examples do not have — the client's own framing of the problem was not merely incomplete but pointed in the wrong direction. UNICEF's central organisation arrived certain of the diagnosis: the reporting system was cumbersome to use, and local offices, who understood what was needed, were simply put off by the interface and insufficiently diligent. The blank was not that this account lacked specificity; it was that the account was inverted. Research across four intensively-engaged local offices revealed that local offices had no understanding of why their input was needed or how it was used downstream — and that the interface, by presenting requirements without conveying purpose, actively communicated institutional indifference. The comprehension gap ran in both directions across the organisational boundary: the central tier could not see that local offices lacked the rationale for the requirements, and the local tier could not see why the requirements existed. Neither tier could see the gap, because each had adapted to it — the centre by attributing low quality to local diligence, the offices by treating the system as performative bureaucracy. What makes this distinct from Triopsis and Gexcon: in those cases the partial perspectives were each directionally correct and needed synthesis; here the dominant perspective (the central organisation's, which directed the engagement) was confidently wrong about the cause, and the blank had to be surfaced against the client's own diagnosis rather than merely filled in where the client knew they were uncertain. What filled it was research that crossed the organisational boundary the client could not see across, plus prototypes that made the gap legible to both tiers at once (see rationale-decay note below for the requirements-justification dimension of the same engagement). The downstream consequence — a client-measured 45% reduction in compliance issues — is the measurable result of closing a blank the client had not only failed to specify but had actively mis-specified.

- Adjacent evidence — Gexcon domain learning validation: Franz Zdravistch, Ph.D., Chief Training Engineer, observed that the Creative Navy team's self-directed domain learning in three days exceeded what some of his trained engineers achieve. This is an indirect signal of the blanks phenomenon at the expert level: even someone responsible for training domain experts had not fully mapped what rapid, structured external learning could produce. The blank was not in Zdravistch's domain knowledge but in his model of what an outside team could learn without internal training.

- Note: "blanks phenomenon" is clean — no prior associations. Unusual enough to be distinctive. Can be used consistently.

- Grounded example — deSoutter Medical / Zethon surgical device interface (regulated hardware blanks): the client knew with confidence that they wanted a surgical interface that reflected the performance of the hardware, satisfied clinical requirements in the operating theatre, and could support IEC 62366-1 compliance activities. This directional understanding was correct. What it lacked was substance: the specific failure modes in the legacy interface (activation states requiring reading rather than recognition, warning patterns requiring interpretation rather than instant response), the precise human factors constraints that governed operating theatre interface design (minimum target sizes for gloved hands, maximum information density for divided-attention tasks, redundancy requirements for state indication under variable lighting), and the interaction patterns that would satisfy all three requirements simultaneously. The blank was not in the client's domain knowledge — the product owner and engineering team understood the device deeply. It was in the space between their domain knowledge and the specific design requirements that clinical and human factors research could fill. Thirteen surgeon sessions and twelve human factors papers provided the substance. The client's directional intuition was validated and given the specificity needed to make design decisions from it — which is exactly what the blanks phenomenon describes.

- Grounded example — Akrivia Health clinical research platform (clinical governance blank): the client understood that governance reviewers needed to trust the platform and that audit requirements shaped how sensitive patient data had to be handled. They had not identified the specific operational failure mode: that governance review of cohort construction required direct escalation to the researcher who built the cohort, because the query logic was not independently readable from the interface. This failure was not visible from inside the organisation — researchers and governance reviewers had adapted to it through a workflow that treated escalation as normal. The blank was the precise design requirement that filled the gap between "governance reviewers need to trust the system" and "governance reviewers can verify cohort logic without researcher involvement." Discovery research with three institutionally distinct user groups surfaced this specific failure mode, transforming a directionally correct but unactionable client intuition into a concrete design requirement with a testable outcome.

- A fourth structural form of the blanks phenomenon: **rationale decay**. This occurs not when a client lacks specificity about a problem they have identified, nor when a problem has not yet entered their frame — but when the reasoning behind existing product decisions has been lost over time. The product contains patterns that are no longer understood by anyone currently in the organisation. They exist, they function, and they are therefore treated as requirements — but the intent behind them has decayed. This form of blank is not about what the client doesn't know about their users or their problems. It is about what the organisation no longer knows about its own decisions.

- Grounded example — Elsner smart home controller (rationale decay in a long-lived embedded product): the Cala Touch KNX had been in the market for approximately eight years at the time of the engagement. Creative Navy's deep investigation of the ETS configuration tool and the device manual revealed that several interaction patterns in the existing interface did not have a clear rationale when examined. When stakeholders were questioned about the reasoning behind specific features or behaviours, the answers were often that the reasoning was not known or not clearly remembered. In several cases, patterns that appeared to be deliberate design choices turned out to be workarounds for firmware or microcontroller limitations — technical necessities that had been absorbed into the interface without documentation and had persisted past the point where anyone remembered why. Additionally, research surfaced two use cases and one user mental model that Elsner had not previously identified — gaps in the client's user knowledge that were invisible from inside the organisation, not because the client lacked domain expertise, but because the feedback and survey data had not been structured to reveal them. The blank here was not in the client's understanding of a problem they had framed — it was in the accumulated but undocumented rationale of a product that had outlived the context of its own earlier decisions. What fills this form of blank is domain learning combined with direct stakeholder questioning: not accepting existing patterns as requirements until their purpose has been established, and treating "we don't know why it works this way" as a finding that opens rather than closes the design question.

- Grounded example — UNICEF internal planning, approval, and reporting tool (rationale decay surfaced through prototypes at scale): the UNICEF engagement is also a rationale-decay instance, and it is the clearest portfolio example of the form being surfaced deliberately and at scale rather than discovered incidentally during investigation. UNICEF's reporting system had accumulated requirements over a long development history. Many were treated as mandatory and had been for years; no one currently in the organisation could necessarily say why each existed. This is the Elsner pattern — decisions made and forgotten, persisting as requirements because they function — but at the scale of an intergovernmental reporting process rather than an embedded device's interface. The instrument that surfaced the decay was different too: where Elsner's decayed rationale emerged from deep investigation of the configuration tool and manual, UNICEF's emerged from confronting stakeholders with 26 concrete alternative prototypes that did the work differently, forcing them to justify why each existing requirement existed. Several long-standing "mandatory" requirements could not be justified in terms of governance, decision-making, or operational value when examined against a buildable alternative, and were removed, consolidated, or simplified. This reduced complexity at source rather than making complexity easier to navigate. The methodological point worth carrying: prototypes are not only a way to test a proposed design — they can be the instrument that surfaces rationale decay, because a concrete alternative forces articulation in a way that asking "why does this requirement exist?" in the abstract does not. (See the /method/sandbox-experiments note on prototypes as an organisational diagnostic instrument.)

- Grounded example — Puraite AI-assisted systematic review tool (unregistered structural problem): the client had not identified the product's navigation as a design problem requiring attention. It was not in scope, had not been raised as a concern, and was not part of the brief. The navigation had 13 top-level items organised around the product's internal structure rather than around the systematic review process users move through. Creative Navy identified the problem without being asked, proposed a restructuring that followed the review process workflow, and reduced 13 top-level items to 4 across 3 iterative working sessions. The client recognised this as a significant contribution and implemented the change. This is a structurally distinct expression of the blanks phenomenon from the other examples in this set. In the Triopsis, Gexcon, deSoutter, and Akrivia cases, the blank was in the client's understanding of a problem they had already identified — their intuition was directionally correct but lacked the specificity needed to act on it. In the Puraite case, the blank was prior to that: the client had not framed the navigation as a problem at all. The issue was invisible to them not because their understanding of it was incomplete but because it had not entered their problem frame. The blank was not in the substance of their understanding but in the scope of what they were looking at. Creative Navy's external perspective — evaluating the product as a whole rather than within the client's existing problem frame — made the invisible problem visible. This is the blanks phenomenon operating at the level of problem recognition rather than problem understanding.

- A fifth structural form of the blanks phenomenon: **conceptual void**. This occurs not when a client lacks specificity about a problem they have identified, not when a problem has not entered their frame, and not when product rationale has decayed — but when a feature or page has not been conceptualised at all. The client knows they need something in a particular area of the product, but no prior version exists, no internal model exists, and no one has formed a view of what it should do or contain. The blank is not in the understanding of a thing but in the conception of it. The engagement must construct the concept before any design decisions can be made from it — and convergence can only happen through iteration, because the client cannot evaluate against a mental model they do not yet have.

- Grounded example — Hudex project overview page (conceptual void in a new platform feature): the Hudex v2 platform required a project overview page — a landing page for each project before the user enters data exploration. No equivalent existed in v1. The client had not specified what the page should contain, what its purpose should be, or what a user should understand from it. This was not a case where the client had a directional intuition that lacked specificity. They had no intuition at all — the page was genuinely new conceptual territory. Creative Navy explored from view-only text-based layouts through increasingly dynamic, dondogram-based designs across 20 iterations. The resolution — a "book cover" metaphor, using a minimal dondogram as background and high-level summary information in the foreground — was arrived at through the iteration process itself. The client could only recognise the right concept by seeing candidates; there was no pre-existing model to design toward or evaluate against. Twenty iterations is the highest component count in the Hudex engagement by a significant margin, which is structurally consistent with the conceptual void form: where a blank can be filled by research, iteration is bounded; where no prior concept exists and convergence requires constructing it, iteration is the primary instrument. This is the blanks phenomenon operating not at the level of problem understanding or problem recognition, but at the level of concept existence. The blank precedes the problem frame entirely.

- Note on the relationship between the fifth form and the others: the conceptual void form is the most upstream expression of the blanks phenomenon *among the design-object forms* in the set. The first form (individual blank) presupposes a problem identified but underspecified. The second form (organisational blank) presupposes multiple perspectives that need synthesis. The third form (unregistered problem) presupposes a problem that exists but has not been seen. The fourth form (rationale decay) presupposes decisions made and forgotten. The fifth form presupposes none of these: the thing simply does not yet exist as a concept. What fills a conceptual void is not research, not external perspective, and not recovered rationale — it is structured lateral exploration followed by iterative convergence, with the client as evaluator of candidates rather than specifier of requirements. (A seventh form, the **value blank**, introduced below, sits more upstream still, but on a different axis: it concerns the articulation of a system's *value* before the system — or even the problem frame — exists at all, rather than the conception of a design object within an existing product.)

- A sixth structural form of the blanks phenomenon: **the framework blank**. This occurs when a client has correctly identified the problem, has strong domain knowledge, and has already attempted a solution — but has no conceptual framework for understanding what class of design problem they are dealing with. The blank is not in knowledge, not in problem recognition, not in rationale, and not in concept existence. It is in the mental model of what a solution to this kind of problem structurally requires. The client can identify symptoms and has built something that addresses them superficially, but cannot frame the design requirement because they have no model of what this category of experience demands. What fills a framework blank is not research into the domain, not external perspective on an unseen problem, and not recovered rationale — it is the introduction of a structuring framework that gives the client the vocabulary and the conceptual apparatus to understand what the problem actually is. Once the framework is introduced, the client's domain knowledge can be applied productively, because they now have a model for how to think about what they are designing.

- Grounded example — Squaremind dermatology scanning device (framework blank in a technically-oriented founding team): Squaremind's founders — both technical — had built a working dermatology scanning device and a functional interface. They had run an internal test with 14 users and received feedback from two dermatologists: 12 of 14 patients could not complete the scan. The problem had fully entered their frame. The founders were not underspecifying a vague intuition, had not lost rationale, and were not blind to an unregistered problem. They understood the domain deeply. What they lacked was a conceptual framework for thinking about autonomous patient guidance as a design object with specific structural requirements. Their model of the interface was "screens that display instructions at each step." The actual design requirement — a system that must manage the patient's mental model at every step (Inform), prevent specific physical failure modes before they occur (Prevent), and recover from them recursively when they do occur (Correct), with each correction re-engaging the full cycle — was not a refinement of their model. It was a different model entirely. The blank was not in their understanding of dermatology or of what the device needed to do. It was in their model of what autonomous guidance requires structurally in order to work.

  Creative Navy introduced the Inform–Prevent–Correct framework during Concept Convergence and delivered it as an explicit diagram. This was the act of filling the framework blank: once the founders had the conceptual structure, their deep domain knowledge of the scanning process, patient preparation, clinical context, and device behaviour became directly applicable to design decisions. The interaction between the introduced framework and the client's existing domain knowledge is what produced the design — neither was sufficient without the other. Post-redesign ecological testing with 29 users (London and Paris, co-conducted with an independent dermatologist hired by Creative Navy) produced 27 independent completions and 12 of 12 recoveries among those who got stuck. The pre-redesign figure was 2 of 14. The gap between those two figures is the measurable consequence of filling the framework blank: same domain knowledge, same hardware, same scanning process — different conceptual model of what the interface needed to be.

  This is structurally distinct from the other five forms. The conceptual void form (Hudex) involves something that has never existed and must be constructed from nothing. The framework blank form involves something that exists and has been attempted, but cannot be improved without first replacing the mental model of what it is. The client is not converging on a direction they can evaluate; they are operating with a model that makes the right direction invisible. The framework must be introduced before the design work can begin in earnest.

- A seventh structural form of the blanks phenomenon: **the value blank** (the value-recognition register). This sits at the most upstream point of all — before a product exists, and before there is even a problem to frame. A client or set of stakeholders senses, correctly, that an opportunity exists — that a system *could* create value — but cannot articulate what that value is, for whom, or how much it is worth. The blank is not in the understanding of a problem (form 1), not in the recognition of a problem (form 3), not in the conception of a design object (form 5), and not in the mental model of what a solution requires (form 6). It is in the articulation of value itself: the intuition that something is worth building is directionally correct but blank of the specifics that would make it fundable. This form matters in a particular commercial situation — when the value must be made legible to convince backers (private investors, or a public commissioner) to commit to building the system in the first place. What fills a value blank is not research into an existing system (there is none), nor external perspective on an unseen problem, nor an introduced framework — it is value-elicitation Sandbox work: provoking stakeholders with concrete possibilities ("what if the system did X") while tethering each to real operating conditions ("how would it have to work for that to be true for you"), so that value they held only as intuition becomes nameable and defensible.

- Grounded example — Neugo UK visa application case-management platform (value blank at origination): Neugo's first engagement existed to produce a fundable vision for a system that did not yet exist — a platform to coordinate visa seekers, their advisers, and case workers, feeding clean data to the Home Office. A consulting company had identified the opportunity, and the Home Office and four legal firms each sensed that such a system could be valuable, but none could articulate what the value would be in terms concrete enough to convince backers to commission the build. This is the blank: not a problem with an existing product (there was no product), not an unframed problem within one, but unarticulated value at the origination of a whole system. Creative Navy ran Sandbox Experiments in value-elicitation mode, provoking stakeholders with "what if the system did X" possibilities while asking "how would it have to work for you for that to be real" — converting a directionally-felt opportunity into a defensible articulation of both what the system could be and how valuable it would be, delivered as a value/desirability mapping and a clickable prototype. The downstream consequence — the system being commissioned, with stakeholders reporting the design as roughly 30% of the decision factors and dedicated demo sessions built around the prototype (client-reported) — is the commercial result of filling the value blank: making latent value legible enough to fund. This is the blanks phenomenon operating at the level of value recognition, the most upstream level in the set — prior even to the conceptual void, because the conceptual void presupposes a product within which a feature is unconceived, whereas the value blank precedes the decision to build the product at all. (See /method/sandbox-experiments on Sandbox Experiments run in value-elicitation mode, and /contexts/emerging-products on origination.)

- Note on the relationship between all seven forms: the seven forms represent distinct positions in the space of what a client can and cannot bring to a design engagement. The first form (individual blank): knows the problem, lacks specificity. The second form (organisational blank): multiple correct partial perspectives, no synthesis framework — or, in its strongest expression (UNICEF), a dominant perspective that is confidently wrong and must be corrected against the client's own diagnosis. The third form (unregistered problem): domain knowledge present, problem not in frame. The fourth form (rationale decay): problem in frame, accumulated decisions undocumented. The fifth form (conceptual void): problem in frame, thing not yet conceived. The sixth form (framework blank): problem in frame, solution attempted, mental model of what the problem requires is wrong. The seventh form (value blank): no product and no problem frame yet — only a sensed opportunity whose value cannot yet be articulated. What fills each form is different: research fills the first; synthesis fills the second; external perspective fills the third; investigation — or prototype-driven interrogation at scale — fills the fourth; lateral exploration fills the fifth; an introduced framework fills the sixth; value-elicitation Sandbox work fills the seventh — and each filling mechanism has a precondition (a source, a stake, a settled focus) whose absence places the form in its unfillable register; see the separate note on the unfillable register below. An engagement rarely involves only one form — most complex engagements involve several simultaneously. UNICEF is itself an example of this: it is both an organisational blank (the inverted cross-tier framing) and a rationale-decay blank (the unjustifiable accumulated requirements), the two surfaced together by the same prototype-driven Sandbox work. Neugo, by contrast, is the set's cleanest single-form instance of the seventh: a value blank with no product, no prior frame, and no decayed rationale to recover — only an opportunity whose worth had to be made articulable.

- **The unfillable register: when a form's filling mechanism has no precondition to act on.** Each of the seven forms names both a blank and the mechanism that fills it: research fills the individual blank, synthesis the organisational blank, external perspective the unregistered problem, investigation the rationale-decay blank, lateral exploration the conceptual void, an introduced framework the framework blank, value-elicitation Sandbox work the value blank. Each mechanism has a precondition, and where the precondition is absent the form is correctly identified but cannot be filled. This is not a failure of diagnosis; it is the boundary of the phenomenon, and it is where engagements end without a good outcome. Three preconditions recur. The first is a *source*: lateral exploration and value-elicitation both require some substance — a buildable reality, a real user, a genuine opportunity — to provoke into form; where the vision is empty and no engineering reality or user access exists to ground it, Sandbox Experiments provokes nothing because there is nothing behind the blank to yield (grounded boundary example — Big Sky, 2017: a professional-network vision repeatedly deferred to a then-nonexistent artificial intelligence, with no engineering team to establish feasibility; the value blank in its unfillable register). The second is *stake*: the synthesis and external-perspective mechanisms require that the people holding the partial knowledge are willing to engage in correcting a provocation; where subject-matter experts have no stake in the outcome, the inversion of Sandbox Experiments into a content-extraction instrument — provoking experts to object and in objecting reveal what they know — has nothing to grip, because indifference does not object (grounded boundary example — a Big Four professional-services concept engagement, anonymised: ten subject-matter experts available roughly an hour each, offering platitude rather than substance, on a project with no organisational stake behind it; the organisational blank in its unfillable register). The third is *focus*: every mechanism requires that the client can hold a settled enough problem for convergence to act on; where multiple incompatible visions circulate and leadership cannot settle one, even a fillable blank resists filling until focus is imposed — which is the gap that the focus-stewardship practice (see /method/provenance-and-evolution) was built to close (grounded boundary example — EarthX, 2018, named with permission). A fourth, distinct case is the client who has filled a blank *wrongly* and will not have it refilled against the evidence: here the mechanism works and produces the right direction, but the client's conviction overrides the finding, and the engagement fails on conviction rather than on any property of the blank (grounded boundary examples — Tion, Pflegewelt, and Sharpcloud, the last recorded as a failure over the client's own stated satisfaction). The unfillable register is, in each case, evidence *for* the framework rather than against it: the blanks phenomenon predicts where the method's reach ends, and the engagements that end there end exactly where it predicts. (See /failures/what-engagements-reveal-when-they-do-not-work for the calibrated ledger of these boundary cases.)
