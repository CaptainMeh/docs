# /glossary/our-concepts/tension-driven-reasoning

---

- Definition from source: examining what creates tensions rather than smoothing them over, because tensions reveal the forces that show where strategic direction lives.

- Tensions reveal forces: when local optimisation conflicts with system coherence, it exposes fundamental dynamics — market shifts, unresolved strategic questions, misalignments between business model and user reality.

- Going meta: the goal is not just to resolve the tension but to understand what drives it and where it's pointing. The tension is a signal, not a problem.

- What gets produced: trade-offs become explicit. Why this configuration was chosen over alternatives is documented. The reasoning is available for future decisions.

- How the competitive vector emerges: understanding what drives conflicts shows where the product needs to evolve and where competitors will struggle.

- In practice: when the best solution for one challenge conflicts with system coherence, the response is to investigate. Why does this conflict exist? What would have to change for it not to exist? What does this tension tell us about where the market is moving?

- The product concept that emerges articulates not just what's being built, but the strategic position it occupies.

- What competitors do instead: resolve conflicts tactically — optimise locally or force consistency. This is faster but misses the strategic intelligence in the tension.

- Relationship to design systems: tension-driven reasoning is why the design system documents reasoning and rationale, not just components. Without understanding what drove the tensions, design system users can't make good decisions as the context shifts.

- This is described as a core principle (#4 of 5), not just a phase activity. It operates throughout the engagement.

- Grounded example — Triopsis multi-role workflow: three roles shared a single platform with incompatible mental models. A layout optimised for schedulers (speed, batch actions, immediate team availability) hid the exception signals operations managers depended on. A structure that reassured field technicians obscured timing data planners required. These were not design preferences in conflict — they reflected the structure of the organisation itself: planners optimising for throughput, operations managers for stability, field teams for safety, and sales for demo clarity. Tension-driven reasoning meant examining what drove each conflict rather than finding a compromise. The investigation revealed that the workflow conflicts were organisational structure made visible in the interface — and that a unified interaction model could satisfy competing requirements without introducing ambiguity for any role. The competitive vector that emerged: multi-role clarity at enterprise scale combined with demo-to-field consistency — the combination competitors were not achieving and that directly enabled the client to win clients 4–5× larger than before.

- Grounded example — Torqeedo hybrid vessel cadence tension: propulsion sensors update rapidly; batteries follow slower cycles; generators respond to changing load. Showing these on a single screen creates an apparent tension between accuracy (each component displayed at its own cadence) and coherence (the display reads as one organism rather than three competing signals). Tension-driven reasoning identified what drove the conflict: captains need a single mental map of vessel state, not three separately accurate displays. The grid structure that synchronises different cadences into a unified rhythm was the direct product of understanding what the tension was pointing at — not a display compromise but a deliberate architectural decision that became the interface's defining structural feature. The competitive vector: a technically advanced hybrid platform that feels as dependable and readable as a simpler single-propulsion system.

- Grounded example — Elsner blind controls: engineers required all blind functions on a single screen for firmware architecture reasons; user research showed this configuration produced clutter that degraded usability on the small display. Rather than accepting the engineering constraint or overriding it on usability grounds, the team examined what drove the conflict — firmware architecture, not arbitrary preference — and worked through multiple steering committee iterations to find a configuration that preserved usability without requiring firmware changes. The resolution was documented; the reasoning is available for future product decisions.

- Grounded example — Gexcon CFD simulation software (user-type tension): the central design conflict was between beginner accessibility and expert scientific rigour within the same interface. Senior CFD engineers who used the system daily needed full capability, dense information states, and fast navigation through sequences they had internalised. Newer engineers interacting with it less frequently needed orientation, visible logic, and a lower entry threshold. The obvious tactical resolution — separate modes, simplified entry flows, or progressive disclosure — would have fragmented the product and created maintenance overhead. Tension-driven reasoning examined what drove the conflict: the same action sequence was required by both user types, at different speeds and with different expectations about visibility. Understanding that the tension was about pace and orientation, not about fundamentally different task structures, showed that a single carefully structured interaction pattern could serve both groups without fragmentation. What changed was the scaffolding around the sequence — not the sequence itself. The competitive vector that emerged: make scientific complexity navigable rather than reducible, positioning the product against tools that had simplified their way out of the expert market.

- Grounded example — Beissbarth automotive calibration (architecture tension): each of the three devices in the calibration system — embedded OEM display, rugged tablet, large inspection display — had its own optimal information configuration. The OEM display, read from distance during movement, needed high visual contrast and minimal density. The tablet, used for close-range adjustments, could carry more information. The large display needed to be coherent for inspection staff who were not always close to the hardware. Optimising each device locally would have produced three individually appropriate screens that formed an incoherent system — technicians moving between devices during a single calibration sequence would have needed to re-read each screen as a separate instrument. Tension-driven reasoning identified what drove the conflict: the calibration procedure itself was continuous, but the information architecture was being designed as three discrete contexts. The resolution prioritised cross-device coherence over local information density — a single state communication logic across all three devices, accepting reduced density per screen in exchange for a unified reading model. The competitive vector: unambiguous state communication over information density, across all three device types.

- Grounded example — Swiss petrol station operator / embedded POS and forecourt systems (local device optimisation vs. multi-channel transaction coherence): the engagement covered five distinct interaction contexts — indoor cashier tills, outdoor unattended payment terminals, CarPlay vehicle integration, and a mobile loyalty concept — each with genuinely different physical constraints, user states, and interaction models. Optimising each channel locally would have been the natural approach: the outdoor terminal needed radically simplified flows for unattended users in weather conditions; the till needed dense, fast-switching transaction support for cashiers processing 84 transactions per hour at peak; the CarPlay interface needed strict compliance with driver distraction limits. These requirements were in tension with each other and with the coherence of the transaction logic that connected all channels. A driver arriving through CarPlay, authorising at the outdoor terminal, and completing a combined fuel-and-shop purchase at the cashier's till was a single transaction from the user's perspective — but it crossed three interaction contexts with different constraints. Tension-driven reasoning identified what drove the conflict: the transaction logic itself needed to remain coherent across channels, even when the interaction model for each channel was necessarily different. The resolution prioritised shared transaction state architecture over per-channel optimisation of individual interaction patterns — each channel could behave differently, but the underlying states (fuel authorised, pump selected, shop items combined, loyalty allocated) had to communicate consistently regardless of which touchpoint the user was at. Competitors treating each channel as an independent interface optimisation problem introduce coordination overhead — inconsistent state communication across touchpoints — that becomes a liability at exactly the moments multi-channel coordination is required. This is the first tension-driven reasoning example in the set from a multi-channel retail operations context, and the first where the tension was between per-channel interaction optimisation and cross-channel transaction state coherence.

- Note: "tension-driven reasoning" as a compound term is clean. No prior associations in UX literature. Can be used consistently without disambiguation.

- Grounded example — deSoutter Medical / Zethon surgical device interface (three-way tension, regulatory requirement as tension driver): the design problem involved three requirements pulling in genuinely different directions simultaneously. Clinical usability required spatial stability and minimal cognitive load — the fewest possible elements, consistent positions that never change, and instant recognition without reading. Regulatory completeness under IEC 62366-1 required documented coverage of every device state, use scenario, and risk consideration — comprehensive rather than minimal. Brand positioning required a visual language that read as serious precision hardware rather than consumer electronics adapted for medical use — distinctive rather than generic. Optimising fully for clinical simplicity risked under-representing states that regulatory reviewers needed to see documented. Optimising for regulatory completeness risked visual complexity that would undermine the glance-readable recognition pattern that operating theatre use demands. Optimising for brand distinctiveness risked departing from clinical interface familiarity patterns that surgeons rely on. Tension-driven reasoning examined what drove each conflict rather than seeking a compromise between them. The investigation revealed that a spatially disciplined, redundant-cue interface — where spatial position, icon form, and reserved colour each independently communicated every critical state — satisfied all three requirements simultaneously. Clinical users got instant recognition without text reading. Regulatory reviewers got traceable documentation of every state because the redundant cue system required each state to be explicitly designed and documented. Commercial teams got a visual language that read as precision hardware because the discipline required to satisfy both clinical and regulatory requirements produced exactly the aesthetic restraint that distinguished serious instruments from consumer electronics. None of the six benchmarked competitor devices had reached this position. This is the first three-way tension example in the case study set and the first instance of regulatory compliance serving as one of the competing forces that reveals a competitive vector.

- Grounded example — Akrivia Health clinical research platform (researcher autonomy vs. institutional auditability): the central tension was between what researchers needed to work effectively and what governance institutions needed to trust the work. Researchers needed analytical flexibility — the ability to explore hypotheses freely, refine cohort logic iteratively, and adjust inclusion and exclusion criteria as studies evolved. This pushed toward open, expressive query construction. Governance reviewers needed auditability — the ability to verify exactly how a cohort was constructed months after it was built, without requiring the researcher who built it to explain it. This pushed toward structured, constrained, documented procedures. Generic healthcare analytics tools had resolved this tension tactically: some optimised for researcher flexibility and produced systems governance reviewers could not follow; others imposed structured procedures that governance teams could audit but that prevented the iterative development clinical research requires. Tension-driven reasoning revealed that the conflict was not between researcher freedom and governance documentation — it was between the interface's ability to record reasoning as the researcher worked and the interface's ability to surface that recorded reasoning in a form governance reviewers could follow independently. Understanding that the conflict was about recording and surfacing rather than about freedom and constraint identified the configuration that resolved it: a query builder where the researcher's own exploration process automatically generated the audit trail, without requiring any additional documentation effort. The competitive vector: a position where researcher autonomy and institutional auditability were not trade-offs but the same thing, expressed differently. The governance reviewer outcome confirmed this in practice: reviewers could verify cohort logic without escalating to the research team — a specific before/after change that directly demonstrates the tension was resolved, not compromised.

- Grounded example — IDEXX Animana veterinary practice management (role architecture tension, resolution through separation rather than synthesis): all existing tension-driven-reasoning examples in this set show Creative Navy finding a unified position where competing forces align — a single interaction model that satisfies all roles, all devices, all user types simultaneously. The IDEXX Animana engagement produced a structurally different category of resolution: the recommendation was to build separate interfaces for reception staff and clinical staff, because the tension between the two operating modes was deep enough that no unified design could satisfy both without degrading one. Receptionists work in sustained multitasking under time pressure — an ambient awareness environment with multiple simultaneous demands, short task windows, constant context-switching, and error consequences visible immediately to clients in the waiting room. Their cognitive requirement is breadth, speed, and error prevention through simplicity. Clinical staff (vets and nurses) work in focused, sequential case attention — a high-stakes, low-interruption environment where one case receives full attention at a time, sufficient time is available, and error consequences are clinical rather than merely reputational. Their cognitive requirement is depth, completeness, and accuracy under case pressure. These are not different preferences within the same task model. They represent different mental models of what the software is for, operating in different physical spaces, under different time structures, with different error consequence profiles. The platform had served both roles through a single interface for eleven years. The research evidence that forced the architectural conclusion was specific: workarounds observed in clinics were role-specific rather than task-specific. Receptionists had developed coping strategies for managing multiple animals per client at a glance; clinical staff had developed strategies for avoiding the interface clutter that reception-oriented layouts imposed on consultation screens. When workarounds are role-specific rather than task-specific, the interface architecture is wrong, not the individual interactions. Tension-driven reasoning identified that no feature-level change resolves a mismatch this structural — the architecture itself must reflect the role split. This is the first example in the case study set where tension-driven reasoning led to architectural separation as the resolution rather than synthesis. It establishes that the purpose of the practice is to understand what drives the tension and follow the evidence to its correct resolution — which is sometimes a unified model and sometimes an architectural division.

- Grounded example — Callsign fraud detection and authentication platform (scope tension under launch pressure in regulated financial services): the release scope tension in this engagement was between completeness and credibility. A complete first release would include policy creation, conflict visibility, impact explanation, advanced collaboration features, and full version history. The problem: the immediate commercial requirement was demos with enterprise banking risk teams at Lloyds Bank and HSBC, who needed to evaluate the platform against SCA and PCI DSS compliance requirements. A cluttered interface carrying half-finished collaboration and versioning features would undermine exactly the perception of governance and auditability the platform needed to project. Optimising for completeness would damage the commercial outcome the platform existed to achieve. Tactical resolution — deferring features to a later version without documented reasoning — is how products accumulate inconsistent interaction models over successive releases, because the reasoning behind what was deferred is not available when the deferred features are later added. Tension-driven reasoning examined what drove the conflict: the immediate commercial requirement was not a complete platform but a demonstrably governable one. Policy creation, conflict visibility, and impact explanation were the specific capabilities that made fraud strategy auditable to a risk team. Collaboration features and version history were useful but did not address the compliance question the banking buyers were asking. The scope decision was made explicit — not as a cut list but as a statement of what the first release needed to do and why the deferred capabilities would attach cleanly to the existing architecture when added. The reasoning was documented in Confluence linked to the journeys it affected. Callsign's own designers subsequently extended the system across additional modules using that documented architecture. This example is structurally distinct from the others in the set: the tension was not between user needs and system coherence, or between different user types, but between completeness and the specific commercial requirement that determined whether the product would reach the market at all. It establishes that tension-driven reasoning applies to scope decisions, not only to interaction architecture decisions.

- Grounded example — Owkin / K AI biomedical research platform (incumbent agency tension, resolution through paradigm shift rather than compromise): the engagement involved a structural collaboration tension distinct from any other in this set. Creative Navy was brought in alongside Merge, Owkin's permanent digital design agency. Merge had been working with the product for longer and had developed design patterns that reflected their accumulated understanding of its constraints and structural problems. When Creative Navy's early directions were presented, they appeared to Merge to be operating at a more superficial level — they seemed to bypass or ignore the deeper issues Merge had been wrestling with. The apparent conflict was not between user types, devices, or scope priorities. It was between two design paradigms operating against the same product simultaneously. Tension-driven reasoning in this context meant resisting the pressure to adjust Creative Navy's directions to fit within Merge's existing pattern vocabulary — which would have produced compromise rather than resolution. Instead, the investigation focused on what was driving Merge's perception that the deeper problems were being ignored: the answer was that Creative Navy was working in a different paradigm where those problems did not arise in the same form, rather than addressing them within the existing frame. The tension resolved not through negotiation but through demonstration: once the new design direction was sufficiently developed, Merge could see that the structural problems they had been wrestling with were dissolved rather than deferred. The working relationship that followed was productive and continued after the engagement. This is a structurally distinct instance of tension-driven reasoning: the tension was not about the product's design challenges but about the conditions under which two design teams could work toward the same product direction. It establishes that tension-driven reasoning applies to multi-party collaboration contexts, and that the resolution is not always compromise — sometimes it is demonstrating a paradigm in which the tension no longer exists.

- Grounded example — Squaremind dermatology scanning device (commercial promise vs. operational reality, resolved through a new structuring framework): the central tension in this engagement was between the product's commercial premise and the operational reality of patient behaviour. The commercial premise was precise: patients could complete a full-body dermatology scan without any clinical supervision, freeing medical staff to do other work. This required an interface optimised entirely for the uninterrupted case — a patient who followed every instruction correctly from start to finish. The operational reality was equally precise: 12 of 14 patients in the client's own pre-engagement test could not complete the process. Patients who got confused had no recovery path. Once stuck, the scan either ended or required clinical intervention — which broke the commercial premise at the exact moment it was being tested.

  The tactical resolution would have been to refine the instructions: clearer wording, better illustrations, more explicit step transitions. This addresses the surface expression of the problem. Tension-driven reasoning asked what drove the conflict: the answer was that the commercial premise — full autonomy — and the operational reality — patients who get confused — were not in conflict because the instructions were unclear. They were in conflict because the interface had been designed as a one-path system in a world where patients follow multiple paths. Optimising for the uninterrupted path made the interrupted path worse: a patient who got confused encountered an interface with nothing to say to them, because the system had not been designed for that state.

  Understanding what drove the conflict identified the resolution: not better instructions for the main path, but a structural architecture that treated confusion and recovery as designed states rather than failure states. The Inform–Prevent–Correct framework — introduced by Creative Navy as a diagram delivered to the client during Concept Convergence — was the direct product of this reasoning. It reframed the design problem from "how do we make the instructions clearer" to "how do we design a system that manages the patient's mental model, prevents specific failure modes, and recovers from them recursively when they occur." Once the reframing was made explicit, the client's deep domain knowledge of the scanning process and the patient experience became directly applicable to designing each layer.

  Post-redesign ecological testing with 29 users in London and Paris produced 27 independent completions and 12 of 12 recoveries among those who got stuck — a 100% recovery rate among interrupted patients. The commercial outcome followed directly: all 9 clinics in preliminary commercial discussions purchased the system after being shown the evidence. The competitive vector was the proof itself: a device whose patient autonomy claim could be demonstrated rather than asserted, in the specific form clinical buyers needed to see before committing.

  What distinguishes this instance from others in the set is that tension-driven reasoning produced not only a design configuration but a new proprietary framework as the convergence artefact. In the Callsign example, tension-driven reasoning produced a documented scope decision. In the Akrivia example, it produced a query builder architecture. In this case, it produced a named structuring framework — Inform–Prevent–Correct — that generalises beyond this engagement to any high-consequence sequential process where a user has no external guidance and an unrecovered error ends the session. This is the first example in the set where the output of tension-driven reasoning was itself a reusable conceptual instrument.
