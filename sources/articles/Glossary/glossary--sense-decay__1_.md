# /glossary/our-concepts/sense-decay

---

- Definition from source: "a slow erosion of meaning between intent, behaviour, and interpretation"

- What it describes: systems that function, comply with specifications, and remain internally coherent — while drifting away from operational reality.

- Manifestations named in source:
  Decisions that follow procedure yet worsen outcomes.
  Post-incident reports that conclude "everything behaved as expected."
  Systems that do exactly what they were designed to do, long after the context they were designed for has changed.

- What causes it: automation accelerates structure faster than meaning can adapt. As systems become easier to build, coherence becomes cheaper to produce. Apparent consistency is achievable without genuine alignment to reality.

- Why it matters more now: as automation increases, the risk shifts from technical failure to sense decay. A system can fail its users without failing technically.

- What prevents it: the Critical Systems Design method — by preserving meaning under change, maintaining coherence anchored in operational reality, exercising disciplined judgment over time.

- Relationship to other concepts: sense decay is what organic system building and constraint respecting specifically protect against. It is also what the design system, when built with reasoning documentation rather than as a component library, guards against — because teams understand what they're protecting and why.

- This concept is the answer to the question "why does it matter how a system is built if it appears to work?" The system appears to work, and then it doesn't, and there's no evidence trail to explain why because meaning was never systematically maintained.

- Grounded example — Torqeedo legacy interface: the previous embedded GUI had drifted from operational reality over years of development. Propulsion status, battery state, and generator information had accumulated on separate screens — a structure that was internally consistent and technically functional, yet no longer matched how professional captains needed to understand vessel state during manoeuvres. Captains had developed compensation patterns: learned workarounds for a system that was doing exactly what it was designed to do, long after the operational context it was designed for had grown more complex. The system behaved as expected. The captains were stressed and hesitant. This is the defining sense decay pattern: the gap between specification compliance and operational reality, invisible until it is made visible through field research. Creative Navy's sea trial programme discovered these compensation patterns and made them the starting point for redesign — which is constraint respecting applied to a sense decay problem.

- Grounded example — Triopsis legacy SaaS: modules that had been built by developers and a graphic designer over several years behaved inconsistently. Users had to scan multiple screens to confirm information needed for a single decision. Under peak load this created stress and repeated checking. The system functioned. The interface had accumulated inconsistencies that made every decision harder than it needed to be — not through technical failure but through the slow erosion of coherence as features were added without a central design framework. Multi-stakeholder governance without a UX framework is a structural cause of sense decay: each decision is locally reasonable, and the system accumulates incoherence that nobody designed but everybody experiences.

- Note: "sense decay" appears in the source doc as a proprietary term — capitalise and treat consistently. It does not have prior associations in UX literature. It is clean terminology.
