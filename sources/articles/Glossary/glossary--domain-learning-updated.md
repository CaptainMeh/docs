# /glossary/our-concepts/domain-learning

---

- Definition from source: the team becomes productive users of the systems being redesigned — studying manuals, performing tasks, understanding constraints from inside the work.

- This phase takes longer than clients expect because they don't realize their understanding has blanks.

- Why it's necessary: you cannot design for real conditions you haven't experienced. Observed behaviour reveals reality; stated needs are often wrong assumptions.

- What domain learning involves in practice: studying manuals, performing tasks as a user, shadowing professionals, observing workflows, understanding the domain from the inside. In the deliverables discussion: "having users teach us how to use the system and do their job, us trying to do the job as a user."

- What it reveals that other methods don't: silent patterns users have developed, how users consciously think of the process, how they believe the system should work, how they learned to use it, how challenges changed as they became more experienced, emotional weighting of all of this. And: what the user would or would not be capable of if the system were different.

- Context of use is also discovered here: a tool is always embedded in context. The context is where real potential lies — many things can be made more valuable by integrating better with their context.

- The connection to multi-perspective synthesis: domain learning is part of how Creative Navy synthesizes perspectives that clients can't see from the inside. Clients see their domain deeply but from one perspective. Domain learning gives the Creative Navy team enough grounding to understand what they're seeing from other angles.

- Why clients undervalue this: they don't realize their own understanding has blanks — what the source doc calls the blanks phenomenon. Domain learning is partly about filling those blanks with substance.

- In physically demanding and operationally complex environments, domain learning reveals findings that no other method can produce. The Torqeedo maritime engagement required 12 sea trials over 6 months with 15 professional captains, across temperatures from −5°C to +35°C and through night operations from late evening to early morning. This programme revealed how vibration affects readability at the pixel level, how glare from cold water reduces contrast differently from other light sources, and how scanning patterns in night harbour manoeuvres differ from daytime open-water operation. None of these findings were accessible through interviews, existing documentation, or controlled lab testing.

- Domain learning in legacy expert software requires an additional capability: distinguishing between interface structure that encodes genuine operational knowledge and structure that has accumulated without purpose over years of development. In the Gexcon engagement, the team studied calibration manuals, YouTube tutorials, Gexcon's own internal training videos, and ran controlled tests inside the application before any redesign decisions were made. Two four-hour stakeholder sessions allowed the team to reverse-engineer the sequence of scientific operations embedded in the legacy interface. The depth reached through this process was confirmed externally: Franz Zdravistch, Ph.D., Chief Training Engineer at Gexcon, observed — "I can't believe how much you learned on your own in three days, even some of the experts I train need more time." This is the only instance across all documented case studies where a domain expert provided direct third-party validation of the depth of domain learning achieved. The consequence of this learning was the ability to separate essential scientific complexity — structure that supported correct outcomes — from accidental complexity accumulated over fifteen years. Without that distinction, the redesign would either have preserved everything (and changed nothing) or discarded everything (and broken what worked).

- Domain learning in precision embedded hardware contexts extends to the physical operating environment itself. In the Beissbarth automotive calibration engagement, the team studied calibration manuals, engineering diagrams, and the sensor logic of the measurement system before research began. The domain learning addressed not just what the software did but what calibration technicians were doing while using it: how tolerances are interpreted in real sequences, how borderline values are handled during measurement, and how technicians confirm alignment states while moving around the vehicle. The physical constraints — viewing from 2–3 metres while moving, gloves, variable lighting, reflective surfaces — could only be understood as design inputs after the team understood the calibration procedure from inside. This is domain learning applied to a physically constrained workflow where the operating environment is as important as the software behaviour.

- Domain learning in marine embedded display design extends to protocol-level data behaviour — not only the physical operating environment but how the data itself behaves under operational conditions. In the COX Marine cluster display engagement, domain learning covered NMEA 2000 protocol behaviour and engine telemetry update rates under varying load states: at low speed the frequency and relative importance of rpm, coolant temperature, oil pressure, fuel rate, and trim values differ from their behaviour during a high-speed transit or an emergency fault condition. Understanding these data rhythms was the precondition for designing appropriate information prioritisation — an interface that surfaces the right values at the right moments cannot be designed without knowing when and why those values change criticality. Domain learning also extended to the full range of physical operating conditions: sunlight-readable LCD constraints, military night vision mode requirements, the ergonomics of gloved touch interaction under hull vibration and slamming, and how operator scanning behaviour changes between open-water fast transit and close-quarters docking manoeuvres. This is domain learning operating at three simultaneous levels: the protocol layer (how data arrives and behaves), the hardware layer (what the display can render under what conditions), and the operating environment layer (what the operator can perceive and act on at any given moment). The COX engagement is the first in the case study set where protocol-level data behaviour — not just physical constraints or software architecture — was a domain learning target.

- Note: domain learning is particularly valuable in regulated and high-consequence contexts (medical, aviation, oil and gas, industrial, maritime) where the gap between how a domain looks from the outside and what actually happens in it is very large. The Torqeedo engagement is the most operationally immersive documented example: sea trials are not a research method choice but an operational necessity when the conditions determining interface performance cannot be reproduced any other way.

- A distinct sub-form of domain learning operates in regulated and high-consequence contexts where the relevant domain knowledge is codified in peer-reviewed academic and scientific literature rather than (or in addition to) practitioner knowledge accessible through observation and immersion. In these contexts, the research literature contains operational findings — minimum effective interaction parameters, cognitive load thresholds, perceptual constraints under specific conditions — that practitioner observation cannot efficiently reproduce because the underlying mechanisms are not directly visible to users themselves. Academic literature domain learning is not background reading. It functions the same way as observational domain learning: it generates constraints that design decisions must respect, and it is reviewed before any design work begins. The distinction from observational domain learning is the knowledge source, not the epistemic function.

- Grounded example — Akrivia Health clinical research platform (EHR literature as operational constraint): before any screen design began, thirty-two academic papers on electronic health record interface design and healthcare analytics were reviewed. Eight were identified as directly informing interface decisions. The papers documented how clinicians and researchers navigate between structured clinical data and narrative notes, how EHR users lose context during long sessions, and specifically where EHR interface design fails to make query provenance visible — a finding inaccessible through interviews with the Akrivia team because the failure pattern was not visible to them from inside their domain. The findings were translated into design constraints: the platform required visible provenance cues, stable query history, and a persistent view of which patient data was currently in scope. This is domain learning from the literature: the team entered an established body of interface research evidence and extracted operational requirements that the client's own domain knowledge had not fully specified.

- Grounded example — deSoutter Medical / Zethon surgical device interface (human factors literature as regulatory and operational constraint): twelve human factors studies and ergonomics papers were reviewed before any interaction design decisions were made. The papers covered touch performance with gloved hands, visual search under time pressure, attention switching in dual-task conditions, and medical device usability in clinical environments. Two are directly citable with their findings applied to specific design decisions: Colle, H. A., & Hiszem, K. J. (2004), "Standing at a kiosk: Effects of key size and spacing on touch screen numeric keypad performance and user preference," *Ergonomics, 47*(13), 1406–1423, which informed minimum effective target sizes and spacing; and Tao, D., Yuan, J., Liu, S., & Qu, X. (2018), "Effects of button design characteristics on performance and perceptions of touchscreen use," *International Journal of Industrial Ergonomics, 64*, 59–68, which informed button sizing and feedback timing specifications. These citations were not included for credibility signalling — the findings became named sources in the requirements catalogue, traceable in the IEC 62366-1 documentation to the specific use scenarios and risk considerations they informed. This is the most explicit instance in the case study set of peer-reviewed research functioning as operational constraint rather than as inspiration or reference.

- Note on the two sub-forms: observational/immersive domain learning and literature-based domain learning address different knowledge types. Observational learning accesses tacit knowledge — the patterns, compensations, emotional responses, and contextual adaptations that practitioners cannot fully articulate because they have internalised them. Literature-based learning accesses codified knowledge — findings from controlled experiments and structured studies on mechanisms that are not visible to practitioners in their own practice. In regulated and high-consequence contexts, both are often necessary: the literature provides the parametric constraints (minimum target sizes, maximum cognitive load thresholds, safe feedback timings); observation provides the operational reality that determines which parameters are binding under actual use conditions. The Torqeedo sea trials and the deSoutter literature review address the same knowledge gap from opposite directions: one discovers what conditions the system must perform in; the other establishes what design parameters govern performance in those conditions.

- A third form of domain learning operates in emerging product contexts, where no operational system exists to observe or immerse in. Here the client is the domain — a founder, researcher, or practitioner who holds deep expertise that has not yet been structured into a product model. Domain learning in this context means extracting implicit structure from the client's knowledge corpus: reading research documentation, identifying entities and relationships, mapping how processes move through stages, and validating the extracted model back with the client until it is stable enough to design from. This is not background reading and it is not interviewing. It is a systematic process of making explicit what the client knows implicitly — because implicit knowledge cannot be designed against until it has been given product form. In the Greenlight engagement, the corpus was a doctoral thesis on workplace safety incident reporting. The team read the main chapters, extracted incident categories, severity scales, near-miss classifications, escalation dependencies, and follow-up action structures, then validated the synthesis in working sessions with the founder. The resulting conceptual model — entities, relationships, and the sequence in which information must be captured — became the foundation for all subsequent architecture and interaction design decisions. Without this extraction phase, the design work would have operated on an unexamined assumption about what incident reporting requires; with it, the design was grounded in a researched and validated model of the domain. This is domain learning functioning as product foundation rather than as design input: the first deliverable of the engagement was the model itself.

- Note: domain learning is particularly valuable in regulated and high-consequence contexts (medical, aviation, oil and gas, industrial, maritime) where the gap between how a domain looks from the outside and what actually happens in it is very large. The Torqeedo engagement is the most operationally immersive documented example: sea trials are not a research method choice but an operational necessity when the conditions determining interface performance cannot be reproduced any other way. In emerging product contexts, domain learning is not an enhancement to the engagement — it is the precondition for any other work to begin.

- A fourth form of domain learning operates in AI-enabled product contexts, where the relevant knowledge is not in an operational environment to observe, a practitioner to shadow, a literature to review, or a founder's knowledge corpus to extract — but in the internal architecture of a working AI system that the design team must understand before they can design the interface that governs it. In these contexts, domain learning means making the mechanics of the system explicit: how the model operates, what it produces, what the policy or rules layer does with those outputs, and where the boundaries between automated decision-making and human-configurable control actually lie. This is not technical documentation review. It is a conceptual clarification process — working with product, engineering, and domain specialists until the design team understands the system well enough to distinguish what should be designed as model-internal (and therefore not surfaced in the interface) from what should be designed as policy-configurable (and therefore the primary design target). Without this clarification, the interface design risks either over-exposing model internals (producing complexity that users cannot act on) or under-exposing the configurable layer (producing a system that feels opaque and ungovernable). Grounded example — Callsign fraud detection and authentication platform: the engagement opened with workshops involving product, engineering, and security specialists to map the existing rule structures, the fraud scenarios they needed to cover, and the points where conflicts or gaps appeared. The output was not a list of features or user needs but a conceptual separation: the fraud detection model (which scores behavioural events) is distinct from the policy layer (which applies thresholds, overrides, and workflow decisions to those scores). That separation had not been made explicit in the existing interface — which exposed the combined system as database views and configuration tables, matching the system's internal structure rather than the analyst's reasoning structure. Making it explicit was the domain learning output. Every subsequent design decision — the policy-as-central-object information architecture, the separation of configuration and evaluation environments, the audit trail structure — depended on that conceptual model being in place. This is domain learning applied to an AI system's internal logic: the deliverable is not a user research synthesis or a literature review but a conceptual model of how the AI system works that is precise enough to design the governance interface from.

- A fifth form of domain learning operates when neither user access nor operational immersion is available, and the constraint is acknowledged and addressed transparently. In these contexts — typically short-duration engagements on beta or MVP-stage products where no user base exists to research — domain learning is conducted through team expertise: a member of the Creative Navy team with prior firsthand experience of the domain substitutes for external user research. This substitution is not treated as equivalent to observational or immersive domain learning. It is presented to the client explicitly, with its tradeoffs identified: the team member's perspective is real and grounded, but it is a single perspective from a specific point in the user's learning curve, and it does not capture the range of experience levels, workflows, and organisational contexts that a broader research programme would reach. The client accepts this framing as a deliberate methodological choice given the constraints, rather than receiving an undisclosed approximation. The epistemic transparency is itself a practice: the basis for design decisions is made explicit, including its limits. Grounded example — Puraite AI-assisted systematic review tool: the project manager on the Creative Navy team had prior firsthand experience using systematic review software. No dedicated user research was possible — the product was at beta stage with no established user base, and the timeline ruled out structured sessions. The PM's domain experience was presented to the client as a deliberate proxy with named limitations, accepted as the practical path forward. It grounded design rationale in real user behaviour — how systematic review work actually proceeds, what the cognitive rhythm of screening looks like, what information a reviewer needs at the moment of an inclusion/exclusion decision — without claiming to represent the full range of user types the product would eventually serve. This is domain learning functioning as an epistemically bounded substitute: real knowledge, explicitly scoped, transparently positioned.

- A sixth form of domain learning operates when entering a live product with a substantial prior design history — specifically when a previous agency or internal team has already produced a year or more of design work. In these contexts, the prior work is not dismissed or treated as the starting point for a cosmetic update. It is treated as an accelerated first iteration of domain exploration: a body of attempted design decisions, discovered constraints, and tested-and-failed hypotheses that encodes real knowledge about the product's design space — knowledge that would otherwise take months of direct exploration to accumulate. Domain learning from a prior engagement's output means systematically studying what was attempted, what tensions the prior work revealed but did not resolve, what the failure modes of the previous solutions were, and what the previous team's choices imply about the constraints they encountered. The deliverable of this domain learning phase is not a critique of the prior work but a design space map: an understanding of what the prior iterations established, what they left unresolved, and what a structurally grounded redesign must address. This is meaningfully different from reviewing a legacy interface in the conventional sense. A legacy interface that has evolved over years without external design input reveals accumulated operational knowledge through its workarounds and constraints. A prior agency engagement reveals a different kind of knowledge: what an external team attempted when confronted with the same problem, and what their attempts teach about the structural tensions the product contains. Grounded example — Stromer e-bike embedded display: a previous external agency had worked on the Stromer embedded and mobile interfaces for a year before Creative Navy's engagement began. That agency produced a design system and visual redesign work. Creative Navy reviewed this work at the start of the engagement not to salvage usable components but to understand what had been attempted and why it had not resolved the underlying structural tensions — particularly the warning architecture, where warnings had been added to a fixed screen architecture that was not designed to receive them, producing covering, interference, and contextual detachment failures. The analysis of what the previous agency had done and why it had not worked compressed what would otherwise have been months of direct exploration into weeks of diagnostic understanding. It also established what was worth preserving: not the visual decisions, which were substantially replaced, but the domain discovery they represented — the evidence of what the design space contained and where its irresolvable tensions lay. This is domain learning from a prior engagement's output: not reading it as a legacy to update, but reading it as an accelerated iteration whose failures are as informative as its successes.
