# /evidence/how-we-work-with-evidence/triangulation-not-confirmation

---

- The point: multiple research methods are not run in parallel to confirm common patterns. They are used against each other. Each source interrogates the others. Discrepancies between sources are the signal, not agreement.

- The concrete example from discussion (worth preserving in detail):
  Stakeholders say feature X is important and users want to save time.
  Users say feature X is important and they are under time pressure because each mistake takes long to resolve.
  These sound like the same finding. They are not.
  The hidden nuance: the problem is error resolution time, not time saving generally.
  That nuance then gets taken back to stakeholders — what does this change about how you think about the feature?
  Then to competitive analysis — how do competing products handle error prevention or make error correction faster?
  Then to technical constraints — what is technically possible to do differently?
  Each source shines a different light on the nuance. The nuance itself is only visible because the sources were used against each other, not to confirm each other.

- This is described as "detective work" — paying attention to little nuances and discrepancies, then shining different perspectives onto them. Not looking for consensus. Looking for what each source reveals that the others don't.

- Using many methods and many details this way ends up generating a very different picture of: the product, the market, the competition, how to bring value to users, how to sell the product better, and what needs to be built technically. The finding is not a confirmed fact but a richer understanding of the situation.

- Research methods involved: stakeholder interviews, user interviews, contextual inquiry, field study, competitive analysis, heuristic evaluation, analytics audit, technical constraints discussions, operator environment audit, domain expert knowledge mapping, legacy system analysis, and more. The value is not in any single method but in how they are used together.

- This triangulation is also why Sandbox Experiments matter: discovery is not just observing what exists. Prototypes create new reality and allow discovery of what could exist alongside what does. The two illuminate each other.

- The hunter/bureaucrat distinction applies here: the same activities produce different results depending on how they are pursued. Persisting to find the right participants. Returning for a second session. Asking a daring question. Gaining access to software not publicly available for benchmarking. Asking developers to reconsider technical constraints — not because the design team knows better, but because a changed perspective sometimes generates value even for the developers themselves.

- Users are often more open with a third party than with their own provider. This is part of why access to real users through Creative Navy produces different information than what the client organisation already has.

- User observation specifically: having users teach the team to use the system, team members attempting to do the job as a user. These methods reveal: silent patterns, how users consciously think of the process, how they believe it should work, how they learned to use it, how challenges changed as they became more experienced, emotional weighting of all of this. Also reveals what users would or would not be capable of if the system were different.

- Context of use: a tool is always embedded in context. The context is where real potential lives. Many things can be made more valuable by integrating better with their context.

- Grounded example — Triopsis workforce management SaaS (five-source triangulation): five distinct evidence sources were used against each other, not to confirm common patterns. Stakeholder interviews (5) surfaced competing organisational priorities — throughput, stability, safety, demo performance. These did not confirm a single direction; they revealed structural tensions. User interviews (43 sessions, 21 participants) revealed how the same interface failed three roles differently: schedulers described speed problems, operations managers described exception visibility problems, technicians described clarity and confirmation problems. These were not the same problem seen from different angles — they were genuinely different operational failures in the same system. In-situ observation (3 sessions) revealed behaviour under real-time pressure that interviews could not surface: hesitation patterns, repeated checking, and error patterns such as incorrect sequencing and duplicate assignments that users did not describe but observers could see. Behavioural science and ergonomics research was used to interpret observed patterns — not to generate solutions but to understand the mechanisms behind what was observed. Benchmarking provided competitive context and identified specific gaps the client had not recognised as problems. The discrepancies between sources were findings: stakeholder priorities conflicted with each other; user self-report conflicted with observed behaviour; benchmarking revealed gaps no internal perspective had named.

- Grounded example — Torqeedo maritime HMI (five-source triangulation with different structure): the evidence sources in this engagement had different roles from each other — not just different perspectives on the same questions. Legacy system analysis revealed compensation patterns: how captains had learned to work around information fragmentation. This was only visible through analysis of the existing system, not through interviews or observation alone. Sea trial observation (15 captains, 12 sessions) revealed scanning patterns and physical interaction failures that only appeared under real maritime conditions — vibration, glare from cold water, night operations. These could not be reproduced in a lab and could not have been predicted from interviews. A controlled experiment (24 subjects) then quantified what observation had suggested: captains identified key energy states 50% faster with the new interface. Eye tracking in sea trials (7 subjects) directly counted glance reductions during manoeuvres, producing a measured finding from real operational conditions. Captain satisfaction feedback (15 captains, unanimous) confirmed the direction of the qualitative findings with structured feedback. Each source did something the others could not: the controlled experiment quantified what observation had suggested; the eye tracking measured what the controlled experiment had established in principle; the legacy analysis explained why the patterns appeared that the sea trials revealed.

- Grounded example — Gexcon CFD simulation software (six-source triangulation, expert software domain): six distinct evidence sources were used against each other across the Sandbox Experiments phase. Domain learning — studying manuals, tutorials, internal training materials, running controlled tests inside the application, and two intensive four-hour stakeholder sessions to reverse-engineer the workflow sequence — produced an understanding of the system's scientific logic that could not have come from interviews or observation alone. It established a baseline that made the other sources interpretable. User interviews (24 sessions) then surfaced how different user types — senior CFD engineers, safety analysts, process engineers, newer engineers, risk managers — experienced the same interface differently. These were not variations on a common theme but genuinely distinct operational relationships with the same system. In-situ observations (23 sessions) revealed behaviour under real working conditions that self-report could not capture: how engineers navigated non-linearly between configuration, verification, and interpretation; the cognitive load of managing dense state within a single environment rather than across distributed screens. Stakeholder interviews (9 sessions) produced organisational and product priorities that did not align cleanly with what user research showed — a discrepancy that pointed at the blanks phenomenon operating within the client organisation. Competitor benchmarking (12 systems) identified structural patterns and specific gaps the client had not recognised as problems, including practices that increased cognitive noise and patterns that could be reinterpreted more rigorously. Feature and task analysis (102 tasks documented with goals, frequency, difficulty, and actions) provided the empirical backbone that synthesised the other sources into a specific requirement structure. The discrepancies between sources were the findings: stakeholder priorities conflicted with observed user behaviour; internal product knowledge did not account for the full range of user-type differences; competitor benchmarking revealed gaps no internal perspective had named. The result was not a confirmed set of problems but a richer, differentiated understanding of what was actually happening across user types, roles, and operational contexts — the basis for design decisions that could serve all of them without fragmentation.

- Grounded example — Swiss petrol station operator / embedded POS and forecourt systems (quantitative transaction coding used against live observation to surface compensating behaviour): the Sandbox Experiments phase combined two evidence sources that interrogated each other rather than confirming each other. Forty hours of structured observation and twenty-four interviews produced a qualitative picture of where cashiers experienced friction. These were then used against a coded dataset of 532 transactions — categorised by type (fuel-only, combined fuel-and-shop, voucher redemption, loyalty, exception handling) and complexity — which produced a quantitative picture of how often each friction point arose and under what load conditions. The sources disagreed in informative ways. Interview self-report and observation suggested that combined transactions were the primary source of cashier stress. Transaction coding revealed that the frequency and complexity distribution was different from how cashiers described it: certain transaction types that cashiers rarely mentioned were disproportionately represented in error recovery sequences. The discrepancy was the finding: cashiers had normalised certain compensating behaviours — personal shortcuts to work around field-ordering inconsistencies — to the point where they no longer registered them as problems in self-report or even in observed-behaviour description. The coding made those patterns visible precisely because it counted them without relying on cashier recognition. This is a structurally distinct triangulation from the method-based examples (Triopsis, Torqeedo) and the institutional-group examples (Akrivia Health, WCO): the two sources here are not different research methods applied to the same population, nor different populations applied to the same system, but a quantitative operational corpus used to interrogate qualitative observation of the same events. The compensating behaviour that the redesign ultimately addressed — the personal shortcuts experienced cashiers had developed that made training harder for new staff — was only surfaced through this discrepancy, not through either source alone.

- What distinguishes triangulation from multi-method research: in conventional multi-method research, different methods are used to increase confidence in a finding by confirming it from multiple angles. In triangulation as Creative Navy practises it, different methods are used to interrogate each other — with discrepancies treated as the most informative findings. A result that every source confirms is less interesting than a result where sources diverge, because the divergence points at something the individual sources cannot see.

- Grounded example — Akrivia Health clinical research platform (triangulation across institutional contexts): the discovery research involved three institutionally distinct user groups — NHS analysts, academic research teams, and pharmaceutical research staff — who all used the same platform but operated under fundamentally different institutional constraints. NHS analysts maintained strict governance boundaries between research and operational use. Academic teams worked through lengthy ethics and data access approvals before touching real patient records. Pharmaceutical teams had more exploratory freedom early in studies but faced strict audit and reporting obligations later. Rather than aggregating these groups into a single "researcher" persona — which would have produced a description of common patterns and lost the structural differences — the three groups were used against each other. The discrepancies between what each group needed were treated as the primary findings. The NHS group's strict governance boundaries revealed requirements for clear workflow state that the academic group's longer approval timelines made visible from a different angle. The pharmaceutical group's late-stage audit obligations revealed requirements for query history and decision traceability that the academic group's ethics constraints had implied but not fully specified. The critical finding — that confusion concentrated at handover points between governance stages and between team members — was only visible because three groups with different handover requirements each described the same failure from a different institutional position. No single group's account of the failure was complete; the triangulation across groups produced the full picture. This is a structurally distinct form of triangulation from the existing examples: rather than interrogating the same population through different research methods, it interrogates the same system through institutionally different populations whose different constraints illuminate different aspects of the same operational failures.

- Grounded example — World Customs Organization / IPM (triangulation across geographically and institutionally dispersed user groups in a live enforcement platform): the Sandbox Experiments phase involved three user groups with genuinely different operational relationships to the same platform — frontline inspection officers checking shipments against alerts under time pressure in field conditions; intelligence analysts requiring structured access to case histories and seizure patterns; and rights holder brand protection and legal teams needing clear paths to file information and monitor enforcement activity. These were not the same job seen from different angles. They were structurally different work contexts making incompatible demands on the same system. Research combined interviews and workflow mapping with WCO teams and remote observation across selected member administrations. The geographic span of validation — 47 usability testing participants from Italy, Romania, Uzbekistan, Algeria, and Spain — was not a sampling strategy for statistical coverage. It was a deliberate choice to interrogate whether design decisions that worked in one administrative and linguistic context would hold in genuinely different ones. The discrepancies this produced were findings: what constituted a clear status indicator in one context was ambiguous in another; terminology that was normalised in European customs practice required explicit labelling in Central Asian contexts; progressive disclosure assumptions that held for experienced officers failed for newer users in administrations with less institutional IPR enforcement history. Each geographic source interrogated the design decisions that the other sources had produced, revealing where solutions were locally appropriate but not globally robust. The result was not a design validated across five countries but a design that had been stress-tested against five genuinely different operational contexts and revised where the discrepancies pointed at genuine fragility. This is the first triangulation example in the set from a live intergovernmental system — where the user population is not a defined customer segment but sovereign administrations with their own institutional constraints — and the first where geographic distribution was the primary triangulation axis rather than role difference or method difference.

- Grounded example — Squaremind dermatology scanning device (triangulation across five sources with commercial outcome verification): the Squaremind engagement assembled five distinct evidence sources that interrogated each other across the full arc of the work — from pre-engagement context through post-redesign testing to commercial confirmation.

  The first source was the client's own pre-engagement test: 14 users, 2 completions. This was client-reported background data, not Creative Navy-generated evidence. It established the scale of the failure and the age-stratified failure pattern (users aged 45–65 failing faster than those aged 20–35) but could not explain its structure — what specifically caused patients to get stuck, at which steps, and why.

  The second source was Creative Navy's 4 unstructured field observation sessions in France. These were deliberately not structured as measurement — the existing system was too poor to measure usefully. What they produced was situated understanding: the physical relationship between patient and screen, the spatial context of the scan, the observable pattern of confusion as body movement before abandonment. Field observation could identify that patients got confused but not which design failures caused the confusion or how to address them. It interrogated the client's test data by showing where the age-stratified failure pattern was visible in real-time body language.

  The third source was the structured post-redesign testing in London (12 users) and Paris (17 users), co-conducted with an independent dermatologist hired by Creative Navy. This was the primary measurement source: binary completion, timed recovery, failure point catalogue. It interrogated the field observation findings by quantifying what observation had described qualitatively — and it interrogated the pre-engagement test directly: same type of users, same device, different interface. The comparison was not incidental; the testing was designed to be the evidential counterpart to the pre-engagement failure data.

  The fourth source was the independent dermatologist's clinical judgement, applied in real time during the testing sessions. This source interrogated the user testing findings specifically at the boundary between usability and clinical appropriateness: a patient who completed the scan but positioned incorrectly during sensitive body segments would register as a completion in the testing data but not in the dermatologist's assessment. The clinical perspective was not confirmatory — it added a dimension the completion data could not contain.

  The fifth source was Creative Navy's direct observation of 5 of the 9 clinic demos after the redesign. These were not usability tests — the buyers themselves walked through the patient experience to satisfy themselves that real patients could complete the process. The observation here interrogated the London/Paris testing findings against a different kind of real condition: not an ecological test with recruited participants, but a high-stakes demonstration where the commercial decision was being made on the basis of the performance being observed. All 5 observed demos resulted in purchase.

  What distinguishes this instance from others in the set is the sequential structure of the triangulation: each source was not gathered in parallel but built on the previous one, with each source interrogating the preceding findings from a position the earlier sources could not have occupied. The pre-engagement data established the failure; field observation explained its texture; testing quantified the redesign's effect; clinical judgement validated it along a non-usability axis; commercial observation confirmed it against the criterion that determined whether the product would survive in the market. No single source could have produced or confirmed the commercial outcome claim; the claim is credible because five sources, assembled sequentially and used against each other, produced consistent findings from genuinely different positions.
