# /evidence/how-we-work-with-evidence/deliverable-format-follows-purpose

---

- Core claim: the appropriate form of any deliverable depends on who needs to receive it and what they need to do with it. Format is not determined by convention.

- Two situations with different requirements:

  Small, aligned team that participated in the discussions generating the insight:
  A deliverable is a memory anchor. As short and simple as possible. Contains only what is needed to recall shared understanding that already exists. A persona could be 30 words — no name ("Jeff"), no hobbies, no family status, no demographics — because those things have no bearing on design decisions in this context. The magic has already happened in the discussions. A complex deliverable would actually hurt — it turns off stakeholders and takes time that doesn't add value.

  Deliverable that must travel beyond the core team:
  The same content requires substantially more context. Not just what something is, but why it is that way, how it works in a specific situation, what would have to change for it to be different. Understanding must be built, not recalled. These deliverables need to educate people about the thinking.

- The failure mode of most agency deliverables: an unconsidered middle. A format filled in rather than composed. Communicates neither efficiently to the aligned team nor educationally to the uninitiated. Not useful to either audience.

- Specific example of the failure mode: personas with hobbies, family status, age — things with no bearing on how the user interacts with the system. Pain points listed vaguely as "experiences time pressure" without explaining: why there is time pressure, how it manifests, what generates it, how users deal with it. Design decisions must cater to the underlying factors, not the surface expression.

- What good pain point documentation looks like (implied): explains not just what the problem is but why it exists, how it works, in what context it appears, and what it means for design decisions. The surface expression ("experiences time pressure") is not actionable. The underlying factor (errors produce cascading resolution work that creates time debt mid-task) is actionable.

- Workshops are the primary vector for knowledge transfer. Deliverables are reference documents that follow from them. A persona discussed in a workshop session creates shared understanding that a document circulated by email cannot create.

- The benefit of many modelling deliverables (personas, journey maps, cognitive load models, etc.) is primarily in the team preparing them — not in the final artifact. Organising the information that way, processing it with that depth, the prioritisation and hierarchy created in the process — this builds the capability to carry out the rest of the work. The artifact is a by-product, not the point.

- This has practical implications for how Creative Navy structures its engagements: workshops first, documentation second; format determined by audience and purpose, not by convention; complexity of deliverable calibrated to what will actually be used.

- **Structuring frameworks as deliverables whose format is determined by what they must do**: the principle that format follows purpose extends beyond documentation — it applies equally to analytical frameworks produced during design work. When the purpose of a framework is to make the causal architecture of an experience explicit and shared between designer and client, the right format is a diagram that maps the architecture step by step, not a written description of it. The diagram must be navigable by both parties, must survive design reviews, and must be usable as a reference during implementation. A prose description serves none of these purposes; a mapped diagram serves all of them.

- Grounded example — Squaremind Inform–Prevent–Correct diagram (structuring framework delivered as a mapped diagram, not a written description): during the Squaremind engagement, Creative Navy introduced the Inform–Prevent–Correct framework to organise the design work on the patient scanning interface. The framework had three layers (Inform, Prevent, Correct) applied recursively across every step of the scan flow, with each Correct event generating a sub-cycle for the recovery path. The question of format was not trivial: a written description of the framework would have communicated the concept but could not have served as a working reference during design reviews, could not have been walked through step by step against the scan flow, and could not have been handed to the client as an artefact they could use to evaluate design decisions independently. The diagram format was chosen because it allowed both parties to point at specific steps in the flow and ask: what does Inform require here? What does Prevent need to address? What happens if Correct is triggered at this point, and what does the system need to do next? The format made the framework a working instrument rather than a conceptual description. The client — two technical founders with no prior UX vocabulary — could engage with it directly because the diagram externalised the logic in a form they could follow and challenge. This is deliverable format following purpose in a specific sense: the content (the Inform–Prevent–Correct architecture) existed independently of its format, but its usefulness as a shared working reference was entirely a function of the diagram form. A written description of the same content would have been a different deliverable with different uses — appropriate for documentation travelling beyond the core team, but not as a working instrument in a collaborative design process with a small aligned team.

- A second deliverable format principle from the same engagement: each design presentation was accompanied by design education content explaining the user behaviour patterns the design addressed — why users behave in ways that make the design necessary, not just what the design does. In a small founding team with no prior design vocabulary, presenting a design without explaining the user behaviour it responds to produces a different kind of deliverable: one the client can evaluate aesthetically but not analytically. Adding the behavioural explanation to each presentation changed what the deliverable did: it gave the client the vocabulary and the understanding to evaluate options on the same terms Creative Navy used. The format of the presentation did not change; what changed was what was included in it, and why, based on what the client needed to be able to do with it.
