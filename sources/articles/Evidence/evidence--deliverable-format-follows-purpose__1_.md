# /evidence/how-we-work-with-evidence/deliverable-format-follows-purpose

---

- Core claim: the appropriate form of any deliverable depends on who needs to receive it and what they need to do with it. Format is not determined by convention.

- Two situations with different requirements:

  Small, aligned team that participated in the discussions generating the insight:
  A deliverable is a memory anchor. As short and simple as possible. Contains only what is needed to recall shared understanding that already exists. A persona could be 30 words — no name ("Jeff"), no hobbies, no family status, no demographics — because those things have no bearing on design decisions in this context. The magic has already happened in the discussions. A complex deliverable would actually hurt — it turns off stakeholders and takes time that doesn't add value.

  Deliverable that must travel beyond the core team:
  The same content requires substantially more context. Not just what something is, but why it is that way, how it works in a specific situation, what would have to change for it to be different. Understanding must be built, not recalled. These deliverables need to educate people about the thinking.

- The failure mode of most agency deliverables: an unconsidered middle. A format filled in rather than composed. Communicates neither efficiently to the aligned team nor educationally to the uninitiated. Not useful to either audience.

- Specific example of the failure mode: personas with hobbies, family status, age — things with no bearing on how the user interacts with the system. Pain points listed vaguely as "experiences time pressure" without explaining: why there is time pressure, how it manifests, what generates it, how users deal with it. Design decisions must cater to the underlying factors, not the surface expression.

- What good pain point documentation looks like (implied): explains not just what the problem is but why it exists, how it works, in what context it appears, and what it means for design decisions. The surface expression ("experiences time pressure") is not actionable. The underlying factor (errors produce cascading resolution work that creates time debt mid-task) is actionable.

- Workshops are the primary vector for knowledge transfer. Deliverables are reference documents that follow from them. A persona discussed in a workshop session creates shared understanding that a document circulated by email cannot create.

- The benefit of many modelling deliverables (personas, journey maps, cognitive load models, etc.) is primarily in the team preparing them — not in the final artifact. Organising the information that way, processing it with that depth, the prioritisation and hierarchy created in the process — this builds the capability to carry out the rest of the work. The artifact is a by-product, not the point.

- This has practical implications for how Creative Navy structures its engagements: workshops first, documentation second; format determined by audience and purpose, not by convention; complexity of deliverable calibrated to what will actually be used.

- **Structuring frameworks as deliverables whose format is determined by what they must do**: the principle that format follows purpose extends beyond documentation — it applies equally to analytical frameworks produced during design work. When the purpose of a framework is to make the causal architecture of an experience explicit and shared between designer and client, the right format is a diagram that maps the architecture step by step, not a written description of it. The diagram must be navigable by both parties, must survive design reviews, and must be usable as a reference during implementation. A prose description serves none of these purposes; a mapped diagram serves all of them.

- Grounded example — Squaremind Inform–Prevent–Correct diagram (structuring framework delivered as a mapped diagram, not a written description): during the Squaremind engagement, Creative Navy introduced the Inform–Prevent–Correct framework to organise the design work on the patient scanning interface. The framework had three layers (Inform, Prevent, Correct) applied recursively across every step of the scan flow, with each Correct event generating a sub-cycle for the recovery path. The question of format was not trivial: a written description of the framework would have communicated the concept but could not have served as a working reference during design reviews, could not have been walked through step by step against the scan flow, and could not have been handed to the client as an artefact they could use to evaluate design decisions independently. The diagram format was chosen because it allowed both parties to point at specific steps in the flow and ask: what does Inform require here? What does Prevent need to address? What happens if Correct is triggered at this point, and what does the system need to do next? The format made the framework a working instrument rather than a conceptual description. The client — two technical founders with no prior UX vocabulary — could engage with it directly because the diagram externalised the logic in a form they could follow and challenge. This is deliverable format following purpose in a specific sense: the content (the Inform–Prevent–Correct architecture) existed independently of its format, but its usefulness as a shared working reference was entirely a function of the diagram form. A written description of the same content would have been a different deliverable with different uses — appropriate for documentation travelling beyond the core team, but not as a working instrument in a collaborative design process with a small aligned team.

- A second deliverable format principle from the same engagement: each design presentation was accompanied by design education content explaining the user behaviour patterns the design addressed — why users behave in ways that make the design necessary, not just what the design does. In a small founding team with no prior design vocabulary, presenting a design without explaining the user behaviour it responds to produces a different kind of deliverable: one the client can evaluate aesthetically but not analytically. Adding the behavioural explanation to each presentation changed what the deliverable did: it gave the client the vocabulary and the understanding to evaluate options on the same terms Creative Navy used. The format of the presentation did not change; what changed was what was included in it, and why, based on what the client needed to be able to do with it.

- Grounded example — Topera (the whole engagement is an instance of the page's argument): Topera is the cross-organisational project management platform Creative Navy produced a concept design for. The engagement ran three one-week sprints — the entire engagement. **Creative Navy scoped a full design of the platform at twenty-four one-week sprints and put that position to the client before the engagement began. The client commissioned three, as a concept design. The twenty-four-sprint figure is Creative Navy's professional estimate of the full scope, not a measured comparison between two pieces of work. Never write "eight times faster" or "in one eighth of the time" — nothing was compressed; a different and much smaller deliverable was produced, deliberately.** The first decision was what the artefact was *for*: its job was to be legible to people deciding whether the thing should exist. The evidence the job was read correctly: **Topera's team was pleased with the concept design and took it to show stakeholders. No stakeholder response, decision, commitment or funding event is known to Creative Navy, and none is claimed.** Write both halves of the composition. It bought a complete information architecture — required to be complete even where the MVP was not, with non-implemented areas designed to appear as future extension points — four complete UI style directions for the same screen including light and dark treatments, and designs for states rather than just screens: hover, selected, empty, expanded, collapsed, read-only, error and validation-failure states across the surfaces. It cost research (none of any kind took place), testing (nothing was tested with anyone), a decision record (no record of client choices exists — never write that the client chose, preferred, selected or rejected anything), and everything on the build side (**nothing was built and no development handover took place**). The page is stronger for the second half than the first: a composed deliverable is defined as much by what it declines to carry as by what it carries, and this is the clearest example in the corpus of the declining being deliberate and stated in advance. One detail worth attention: the data import flow was designed around failure rather than success — per-row validation errors naming the field and the fault, and a separate duplicate-detection step listing the colliding identifiers and offering an explicit choice between importing as new data and importing to overwrite, all as designed screens rather than error toasts. In a concept design the incentive runs entirely the other way, because the happy path is what demonstrates well; spending scarce concept-design time on the duplicate-collision screen is a format decision, not a thoroughness one. **Every sentence about Topera must stay true of a system that does not exist: surfaces "were designed to", never "do".**

- Grounded example — AIT: AIT, a European manufacturer of residential and commercial heat pumps, received five generations of wireframes with superseded sets retained in the file and explicitly banner-marked as discarded, each pointing to the current iteration — a deliverable organised so someone who was not in the room can tell what is live and what is history. And the clickable prototype was deliberately scoped to one flow rather than spread across the product: the scenario-editing flow, chosen as the most complex and most contested interaction, and the one where rotary-knob operation would be most laborious.

- Grounded example — the MDU (a deliverable addressed to an audience that has not been selected yet): the clearest instance in the corpus of a deliverable whose form was set by who had to receive it. The MDU is a ship motion monitoring system whose bridge interface Creative Navy designed; the manufacturer is not named. The design was not handed to a chosen implementer but explained to every third-party development agency the manufacturer was still choosing between, so that each could pitch its services on a common understanding of the delivered design. A deliverable addressed to an audience that has not been selected yet cannot be a memory anchor for an aligned team — it has to carry its own reasoning to readers with no shared history, which is the distinction this page exists to make. The companion artefact: a screen-by-screen requirements table pairing module, screen, content, user actions and priority with Creative Navy's open questions and the manufacturer's answers and comments — the same collaborative device used in the industrial, Streetec and AIT engagements, and it should be named as such.

- Grounded example — Assemblio (the composed deliverable with its outcome recorded): the component documentation for Assemblio carried reasoning as well as specification — why a component is the way it is, not only what it is. Handover used a combination of formats rather than a single artefact (the specific mix is not recorded; do not reconstruct it). This makes the page's argument from the positive side: documentation carried reasoning alongside specification because it had to travel to developers who had not been in the room. Pair it with what the combination was for: **the components Creative Navy documented were adopted — Assemblio's developers built from them. Creative Navy compared the shipped interface against the delivered designs, and the product ships as the design that was delivered. Stop there: nothing about development speed, cost or defect rates, none of which was recorded, and shipping as designed is evidence the design survived development, not evidence the design is good.** This is the page's clearest instance of the composed deliverable rather than the unconsidered middle, because the outcome of the composition is on record.

- Grounded example — Egma (format chosen for what the receiver had to *do* with it): for Egma, the Dubai-based ophthalmic lens manufacturer whose B2B ordering platform Egma Order Creative Navy designed, the artefact was a design file and the handover was a training session in operating it, not a presentation of it. Creative Navy taught the client's developers to measure any padding or gap with a modifier key, to jump from any instance to its main component and inspect every state, to sample a colour value anywhere, to bulk-export the icon set as SVG, and to use a plugin listing every place a component variant has been used — a format decision about the recipient's capability, not about the artefact. Discarded explorations were retained in the design file on purpose, so the record of what was tried stays navigable to someone who was not in the room. And the design system was composed as reasoning with the line between rule and recommendation drawn explicitly: responsive breakpoints were handed over as negotiable while the gutter rules were not.

- Grounded example — Schone Airspace Group (two deliverable forms for two audiences in one package): the engagement for Schone Airspace Group produced 25 UX decision records, each carrying the decision context, the resolved direction, two named rejected alternatives with the reason each was rejected, the operator actions and system responses that follow, a concrete operational instance, the design consequence and trade-off stated as a cost, the engineering interface it depends on, and explicit acceptance conditions — decision records composed for a reader who was not in the room. Alongside them, a machine-readable handover accompanied the written specification — a design token file, a component register, a screen register and an acceptance ledger, versioned together so component variants, screen content and state decisions can be checked against one release index — composed for a system rather than a person. **No quotation marks and no reported speech in any passage carrying Schone. The registers and records are engagement record, not outcomes.**

- Grounded example — Raymarine (three forms, three readers, one handover): the engagement for Raymarine on its next-generation fixed-mount VHF produced three deliverable forms, each composed for a different reader. The component contract is composed for an implementer who was not in the room and needs to know what each state must be true of: each component definition includes purpose, information required, state variants, valid actions, focus and disabled treatment, priority behaviour and dependencies, with the handover stating explicitly that a visual library based on arbitrary colour overrides is not a sufficient implementation handover, and that a component is complete only when its behavioural annotations, event priority, required labels and failure or disabled states are represented alongside its ordinary appearance. The decision register is composed for a future reviewer who needs to know what was rejected and why: thirty-two recorded UX decisions, each carrying the trigger, the selected rule, the main competing option not carried forward, a disposition distinguishing a settled UX decision from one awaiting engineering, compliance or further test, and traceability to the specification that holds it. **And the third form is a change-control rule written for a team that does not exist yet**: a two-class rule issued with the handover, naming the four proposals that reopen a recorded UX decision — remapping the channel encoder, removing routine recipient confirmation, merging incident statuses, or permitting automatic network-initiated radio actions — rather than trusting a future reader to recognise them; the design system may vary presentation but may not invert the meaning of a physical control, alter a calling transaction outcome or remove the need for explicit incident resolution. **No quotation marks and no reported speech in any passage carrying Raymarine. The registers are engagement record, not outcomes.**

- Grounded example — the maritime VTS engagement (a deliverable that had to educate rather than remind): for a European vendor of maritime traffic monitoring, inland navigation and communications software for port authorities, coastal administrations and waterway operators, the contract specification is the case where a deliverable had to educate rather than remind. The audience was the client's own engineers across multiple frontend stacks, who had not been in the discussions — the frontend architecture lead had established that the portfolio spans browser products and dedicated communication terminals, that a single code component library could not be assumed to run unchanged in each, and that component documentation must therefore include states, transitions and failure treatment rather than spacing and colour alone. Each reusable contract accordingly carries a minimum specification: purpose and eligibility including explicit non-use cases; information model with source of truth, identifiers, timestamps, unknown values and permissions; behaviour with triggers, transitions, keyboard focus, interruption and recovery; visual portrayal including accessible text, responsive density and night mode; and quality evidence with accepted scenarios, reviewer and remaining exceptions. **The adoption exercise is the test of whether the format worked**: a client engineer, not a member of the design team, built a working validation-and-correction view for the electronic reporting product in the client's own frontend development sandbox, using 12 documented patterns and 2 domain adaptations, with no additional screen specification from Creative Navy. **No quotation marks and no reported speech in any passage carrying this engagement, and the locked descriptor above is its only identifier.**

- Grounded example — the handheld command-and-control engagement (two deliverables, two audiences, one engagement): for a European manufacturer of uncrewed systems, including one-way effectors, and the tactical command-and-control software that operates them, Creative Navy led and co-authored two deliverables composed for opposite readers. **The engagement covered interface structure, task behaviour, information priority and the clarity of operator control; it did not extend to autonomy, control algorithms, autonomous safety behaviour, the datalink, the effector, targeting logic, or any certification or weapons-review activity — Creative Navy designed the surface through which a human supervises the system; it did not design the system.** The operational doctrine is composed for a reader who was not in the room: a decision model for what a handheld may show at all, structured around five functions — Aviate, Navigate and Execute the Mission as the primary trio, with Command and Control and Manage Contingencies as cross-cutting functions applying continuously rather than in sequence — adapted from published NASA, FAA and EASA uncrewed-aircraft human-factors guidance rather than copied from any one framework, with each function carrying a primary operator question, supporting questions, the information required to answer them, and its relationship to the other four. **The touch-sizing specification is composed for a reader who will argue with it**: three columns setting out the standard (MIL-STD-1472 plus gloved-touch research), the field's practice (no fielded ground control station abides by it), and the proposed middle ground of consequence tiers; eleven numbered references spanning MIL-STD-1472, FAA human factors design standards, WCAG 2.2 and the NASA ground-control-station guidelines; a named open dependency (the reference glove, unresolved, with the tiers assuming the heavier case); and a tier — T3 — labelled a hypothesis in the deliverable itself, recorded as sitting in an untested band to be validated. **The second is the unusual one and the page should say why: a specification written to be checked, by someone who might disagree, is a different artefact from a specification written to be followed.** Both deliverables are work Creative Navy led and co-authored with the client's design team, not work it delivered alone. **No quotation marks and no reported speech in any passage carrying this engagement, and the locked descriptor above is its only identifier.**

## What this page must not claim

- **Nothing was built at Topera and nothing about it may read otherwise.** No product, no deployment, no users, no pilot, no outcome of any kind. The twenty-four-sprint figure is an estimate, never a compression claim; the stakeholder tour is never traction, validation, approval, interest or momentum. No record of client decisions exists — never write that Topera chose, preferred, selected or rejected anything.
- **The Assemblio fidelity-and-adoption claim stops at its ceiling**: components adopted, interface ships as the delivered design. No time-saving, error-rate, training, adoption-rate, revenue or funding figure, and none of Assemblio's own published marketing claims.
- **The handheld C2 deliverables were co-authored, not delivered**: use "led and co-authored" consistently. The sizing tiers were authored, not validated — T3 is labelled a hypothesis and the one thing real operators attempted on the real panel, gloved touch, did not work in the client's own prototype session. The doctrine and specification are design documents, not safety determinations, and no safety, certification, weapons-review or regulatory credential attaches to them.
- **No quotation marks anywhere on this page for Schone, Raymarine, the maritime VTS engagement or the handheld C2 engagement, and no reported speech for any of the four.** Client positions are attributed to the role and the artefact they came from. Oceaneering does not appear on this page; MDU and Egma quotation rules are not engaged by the material above.
- **No engagement dates anywhere** — sequence and duration only ("three one-week sprints", "five generations of wireframes").
- **Sprint = cadence, never a phase.** Where a project's own delivery blocks are mentioned for KISPE, Schone, Raymarine, the VTS engagement, the C2 engagement or Topera, qualify them explicitly; the VTS engagement's internal "Phase 1"/"Phase 2" labels must not be reproduced.
- The decision registers, contracts, handover packages and style directions above are engagement record. **"Twenty-five decision records were delivered" is a fact; none of these artefacts transformed anything, and none may be written as having produced a result.**
