# `/evidence/outcomes/verifiable-performance-claims`

---

## What the outcome is

In most markets, product claims are assertions: "the fastest," "the most intuitive," "the leading platform." Buyers in sophisticated markets — enterprise SaaS, B2B industrial, regulated industries, professional tools — have encountered enough assertion-based marketing to treat it as background noise. The design-produced verifiable performance claim is a different class of commercial evidence. It is specific (not "easier to use" but "62% faster job discovery"), measured (in the live product under real operational conditions, not in a test), and verifiable (a prospect can assess it in a trial before purchase).

This type of claim is more durable than any brand position because it is grounded in the product itself. A competitor cannot reproduce it through copywriting. They can only match it by producing the same operational results, which requires producing the same quality of design.

The claim is not that Creative Navy produces sales outcomes. The claim is that designing for performance in reality — the specific principle in Creative Navy's method — produces results specific enough to become sales evidence when measured. The commercial consequence follows from the operational result.

---

## Domain vocabulary

- Verifiable performance claim — a measurable outcome claim that can be assessed by a prospective buyer in a product trial; distinguished from assertion-based claims
- Performance in reality (link to `/glossary/our-concepts/performance-in-reality`) — the design principle that specifies measuring and designing for operational conditions rather than test conditions; what produces measurable outcomes
- Product analytics as evidence — outcomes measured in the live system during real use; stronger evidential category than usability testing task times
- Production deployment measurement — outcomes measured across actual commercial deployments; the strongest evidential category in the portfolio
- Controlled experiment — methodology with defined baseline, defined population, directly measured outcome
- Randomised controlled A/B test — the strongest causal structure in the portfolio: a treatment and a persistent holdout control drawn from the same population, randomised at the user level, measured over a window in which both arms experience identical external conditions. Distinguished from a before/after comparison (which a controlled experiment or product-analytics figure may rest on) because the simultaneous control arm isolates the design change from external drift — in trading, from market movement specifically. Where present, it licenses a causal claim ("the redesign caused the change") rather than an attributional one ("the change is attributable to the redesign within a clean window")
- Credibility gap — the distance between a marketing claim and what a sophisticated buyer will believe without evidence; verifiable claims eliminate this gap
- Evidence-grounded sales argument — a competitive claim that survives contact with due diligence in a sophisticated buying process
- Clean attribution window — a period following a product change in which no other changes were made, allowing the measured outcome to be attributed to that change specifically; the presence or absence of this condition determines whether a client-measured figure can be stated with attribution confidence or only directionally
- Owned business metric — an operational performance metric the client organisation already maintains for its own internal monitoring (e.g. compliance-failure rate, report-preparation time, throughput, OEE, MTTR, fault-diagnosis time), used to measure a design outcome. Distinguished from UX-specific metrics (NPS, task time, completion rate) because the metric pre-exists the engagement, belongs to the client's own performance apparatus, and is evaluated on the client's terms rather than on UX terms — which makes it both more credible to a sceptical buyer and harder for the design partner to influence in its own favour

## Evidence

### Triopsis workforce management (multiple productivity metrics — live system, most commercially developed)
- Three productivity metrics, all measured in product analytics from real users operating the live system — not usability testing task times:
  - **62% faster job discovery**
  - **83% faster job sequence optimisation**
  - **58% faster weekly planning**
- These figures were available as sales evidence specifically because they came from the live system under real operational conditions. A prospect can verify them in a trial.
- **Sales conversions multiplied by four; company began winning clients 4–5× larger. Client-reported by CEO.** The conversion improvement was directly attributable to the interface becoming a visible competitive advantage in demos — prospective customers commented on its clarity unprompted.
- **Tender scores improved by 10–20% attributable to design quality. Client-reported from formal tender evaluation documents.** This is the strongest commercial feedback mechanism in the portfolio: the improvement was noted in the evaluation documentation itself, not just in informal feedback.
- The commercial transformation: before the redesign, sales calls required explaining the interface. After, the interface demonstrated the product's quality without explanation. In a market where buyers evaluate competing workforce management platforms, a claim verifiable in a trial is the most credible differentiator available.

### Gexcon CFD simulation (four production-measured metrics in one engagement)
- Four metrics measured by Gexcon across real deployment locations — not usability testing:
  - **Time to first successful simulation: 4 days → 6 hours (93% reduction)**
  - **Configuration errors per simulation: 5–8 → 1–2**
  - **Corrective load per error: 4–6 hours → approximately 20 minutes**
  - **Active users per team: 1 → 3–4**
- The combination of four metrics in one engagement is unusual in the portfolio. It documents not just that performance improved but the scope: speed, accuracy, error recovery, and team utilisation, simultaneously and measurably.
- These figures are directly usable as sales evidence in procurement conversations with industrial, scientific, and safety-critical buyers — exactly the audiences for whom assertion-based marketing carries least weight. An engineering manager evaluating CFD simulation software can assess calibration time and error rates in an evaluation; a claim grounded in production deployment data survives that assessment.

### Torqeedo maritime HMI (controlled experiment and field measurement)
- **Energy state identification: 50% faster than the legacy interface. Controlled environment experiment, 24 subjects.** Defined baseline (legacy interface), defined population (professional captains), directly measured outcome.
- **Glance reduction during manoeuvres. Measured via eye tracking, 7 subjects, during actual sea trials.** This measurement was conducted in the operational environment, not a simulation — which is what makes it credible to the professional maritime market.
- In a market where safety-critical maritime electronics are evaluated by experienced mariners, claims grounded in sea trial measurement carry the authority that lab-based testing cannot achieve. A professional captain evaluating two helm displays can assess which produces faster state identification in actual manoeuvres.

### Beissbarth automotive calibration (production deployment measurement)
- **Calibration time: 18 → 12 minutes per vehicle. Client-measured across 8 production deployment locations — not usability testing.** Production deployment measurement at 8 sites is a commercial-grade evidence base: it documents the outcome across a real operational distribution, not a controlled test.
- In a market where automotive calibration equipment is evaluated by workshop managers assessing throughput, a verifiable calibration time improvement is a commercially usable claim. A prospect can assess it in a pilot deployment.
- **Training eliminated.** A deployment model change with direct commercial value: the cost of deploying to a new workshop no longer includes training delivery.

### Polymatica OLAP analytics (product analytics measurement)
- **Independent task completion: 2% → 56%. Measured via product analytics.** Task definition: import data, slice and dice data, answer a specific business question, create a report. Before: 2% of users could complete these without assistance. After: 56%.
- This figure was measured in the live product — not in usability testing — which gives it the same category of evidential strength as the Triopsis productivity figures.
- The commercial consequence: HSBC and Barclays became UK clients following international expansion. At their data volumes, the benchmark performance advantage (50–100× faster than competitors) became experientially perceptible for the first time — but only because users could now access that capability without personal training from the founder. Client-reported.

### eToro multi-asset social trading (randomised controlled A/B — the portfolio's strongest causal evidence)
- **Discovery-to-trade conversion rate: 5.1% (control) → 7.4% (treatment), a 45% relative uplift. Median time to first trade: 11.8 minutes (control) → 8.6 minutes (treatment), a 27% reduction. Client-measured by eToro via a user-level randomised A/B test with a persistent holdout.** Conversion is the percentage of sessions originating in discovery/explore that result in at least one completed trade within a 72-hour attribution window; time-to-first-trade is measured from exploration-entry session start to the first executed trade in-session.
- This is the only **randomised controlled A/B** in the set, and it is the strongest *causal* structure in the portfolio. The design (50/50 split, randomised and stable at the user level to prevent cross-exposure contamination, 1,625 users per arm, a 2-week stabilisation window excluded, then a 6-week measurement window chosen to span both high- and low-volatility market phases) means both arms experienced identical market conditions over the same period. The differences are therefore attributable to the redesigned decision surfaces rather than to market movement. In retail trading — where short windows are easily distorted by earnings seasons, crypto volatility, or macro announcements — isolating interface-driven from market-driven behavioural change is exactly what a credible claim requires, and the holdout is what provides it.
- The measurement is independent of Creative Navy in a way that strengthens it: eToro's own product and analytics team defined the success metrics (drawn from indicators they already used internally) and owned the mechanics — the attribution window, session stitching, and significance thresholds. Creative Navy asked eToro to nominate its own metrics rather than proposing design-led proxies. A metric the client defines, owns, and evaluates on its own terms is one the design partner cannot shape in its favour — the same attribution-discipline property that makes the UNICEF and Gericke owned-metric cases strong, here combined with a randomised control.
- **The claim must be framed as decision coherence, not trading volume.** The headline figures (conversion up, time-to-trade down) read, unframed, as a dark pattern that pushes users to trade more and faster — the opposite of the investor-protection story, and precisely what MiFID II financial-promotion and SEC/FINRA fair-balance rules guard against. The data refutes that reading: the time-to-trade reduction occurred **without an increase in early-session drop-off and without a reduction in exploration depth**. Users converged faster *and* explored as much — efficiency in decision formation (fewer hesitation loops caused by ambiguous signals), not impulsivity. State the figures only with this framing; the drop-off and exploration-depth conditions are what make the claim defensible in a regulated context.
- Commercial usability: the verifiable claim a prospect (or eToro itself, internally) can interrogate is not "the redesign is clearer" but "a randomised A/B with a persistent holdout showed a 45% relative lift in discovery-to-trade conversion and a 27% reduction in time-to-first-trade, with no increase in drop-off and no reduction in exploration depth." In a market where the design change must not be confounded with a favourable market window, the holdout is what makes the claim survive scrutiny.

### Tetra/Prism (adoption and NPS — client-measured)
- **Mobile app adoption: 12% → 64% over the year following redesign launch. Client-measured.**
- **Web NPS: 72% → 85%, measured approximately 4 months post-launch. Client-measured.**
- These are measured outcomes, not satisfaction survey responses. Mobile adoption is a behavioural metric: the percentage of eligible users actually using the app. NPS changes of this magnitude are commercially significant in enterprise SaaS procurement — NPS appears in vendor evaluation documentation.

### Enhesa legal compliance platform (NPS with clean attribution window — client-measured)
- **NPS: 68% → 84%, measured two months post-launch. Client-measured by Enhesa across the full user base.** The 68% baseline was measured two months before the engagement began — it was one of the factors that prompted Enhesa to seek design help. No other changes were made to the product between the baseline measurement and the two-month post-launch measurement.
- The clean attribution window is the analytically significant feature of this entry. Most client-measured NPS improvements in the portfolio are stated with directional confidence but without a confirmed no-other-changes condition. Here, Enhesa confirmed that the redesign was the only change made in the window between the 68% baseline and the 84% post-launch figure. The NPS improvement is attributable to the design change specifically, not to a combination of changes made concurrently.
- **NPS at two years post-launch: 87%. Client-measured.** After the 84% measurement, other changes were made to the product. The two-year figure should not be attributed to the design work specifically — subsequent development contributed. It is directional evidence of sustained platform quality, not a design attribution claim.
- The product is a web-based legal compliance intelligence platform used by regulatory affairs managers, compliance officers, and legal teams. Users are primarily domain experts navigating dense legislative content across multiple jurisdictions. NPS in this context reflects platform usefulness and usability under professional working conditions — a different weight of evidence from consumer NPS scores.
- **Corroborating signal from the same survey:** 45% of pre-redesign users said they had watched training videos; 81% of those said the videos were not helpful. Only 21% of users onboarded after the redesign sought training videos at all. The training video finding and the NPS improvement come from the same survey period and corroborate each other: both indicate that users found the redesigned platform sufficiently self-explanatory that external supplementation — whether measured through satisfaction or through help-seeking behaviour — was substantially reduced.
- Evidence calibration: all figures client-measured by Enhesa. The NPS survey methodology and full instrument were not shared with Creative Navy in detail. The clean attribution condition (no other changes between 68% and 84%) was confirmed by Enhesa.

### UNICEF planning, approval, and reporting tool (owned business metrics measured against a pre-established baseline — client-measured)

- The UNICEF case introduces an evidential category not otherwise represented in this set: outcomes measured against **owned business metrics** — operational performance metrics the client organisation already maintained for its own internal monitoring, rather than UX-specific metrics introduced for the engagement. The two figures are not NPS, task time, or completion rate. They are the organisation's own measures of reporting quality and reporting efficiency.
  - **Compliance issues: 45% reduction. Client-measured against a pre-established baseline, nine months post-rollout.** "Compliance issues" are reporting submissions that failed UNICEF's established reporting standards — missing information, incomplete approval chains, incorrect categorisation, missing supporting documentation, inter-field inconsistencies — and therefore required headquarters follow-up before acceptance.
  - **Report-preparation time at headquarters: 42% reduction. Client-measured against the same baseline.** The time headquarters needed to prepare consolidated reports once submissions were received, reduced because cleaner, more consistent submissions required less manual validation, correction-chasing, and inconsistency resolution.
- Why this category is evidentially strong, and in some respects stronger than client-measured NPS: the metrics pre-existed the engagement and belonged to UNICEF's own performance-monitoring apparatus. UNICEF identified the baseline before the redesign began, using data it already collected to monitor reporting quality and organisational performance. Creative Navy's role was limited to helping identify which of the client's existing operational metrics were most relevant to the problems surfaced during research — the measurements themselves were produced entirely through UNICEF's internal reporting and performance-monitoring processes. A metric the client already owns, already trusts, and evaluates on its own terms is one the design partner cannot shape in its own favour, which is exactly the property a sceptical buyer is looking for.
- The measurement structure has clean-attribution properties worth stating: a defined baseline established before the redesign, a defined nine-month post-rollout measurement window, and metrics owned and operated by the client rather than constructed for the engagement. This is not the same as the Enhesa no-other-changes condition — UNICEF's rollout was a large system replacement, so the redesign is the dominant change rather than a confirmed sole change — but the use of pre-existing, client-owned operational metrics is its own form of attribution discipline.
- **Causal framing — state precisely.** The compliance and time figures are not independent outcomes; both follow from reducing the volume of defective submissions requiring correction. And the design mechanism behind them was the redesign of role interactions and workflow handoffs — resolving incompatible mental models across roles and tiers, and embedding an agreed reporting standard structurally in the system — not screen usability improvement alone. The verifiable claim is the reduction in the client's own business metrics; the mechanism is the workflow and standards redesign.

### Gericke industrial HMI (owned business metrics measured within a confirmed clean attribution window — client-measured, three sites)

- The Gericke case is the strongest *combined* attribution case in the set: it pairs **owned business metrics** (the UNICEF property) with a **confirmed clean attribution window** (the Enhesa property), across **three independent deployment sites**. Each property on its own is strong; together, across multiple sites, they are the cleanest design-attribution structure in the portfolio. The metrics are the plants' own operational performance measures — availability, OEE, MTTR, fault-diagnosis time, manual interventions per shift, repeat-alarm rate, unplanned downtime, operator-caused stoppages — captured by Gericke (client-measured) four months after the new HMI went live.
- The clean attribution window is the decisive feature. Gericke confirmed that over the measurement period **no other variables changed** — no new hardware or sensors, no mechanical upgrades, no training programmes, no recipe or process changes. The HMI was the only variable that moved, which makes the deltas interface-attributable rather than merely co-occurring. This is the no-other-changes condition the Enhesa entry relies on, established here independently at three sites.
- Matched before → after pairs (client-measured; sites described by type and geography, never named):
  - **Swiss pharmaceutical site (continuous manufacturing):** fault-diagnosis time 24 → 8 min; MTTR 65 → 42 min; repeat alarms 42% → 18%; operator-caused stoppages 3 → 1 per month; OEE 79% → 84%.
  - **Italian food site (infant-formula production):** fault-diagnosis time 38 → 12 min; MTTR 105 → 60 min; repeat alarms 58% → 28%; operator-caused stoppages 7 → 3 per month; OEE 71% → 78%.
  - **Swiss specialty-chemical site (powder coatings):** fault-diagnosis time 68 → 20 min; MTTR 165 → 90 min; repeat alarms 73% → 35%; operator-caused stoppages 15 → 6 per month; OEE 61% → 72%.
- **Which figures are the verifiable claim.** Lead with fault-diagnosis time (roughly two-thirds faster at every site), operator-caused stoppages (roughly halved), and repeat alarms (more than halved) — these map directly to what the redesign targeted (state visibility, root-cause alarm hierarchy, contextual explanation), so the causal story is coherent, not merely correlational. Availability and OEE moved less and are supporting, not headline.
- Why this is commercially usable in the industrial-process market: an engineering manager or plant director evaluating a dosing or conveying supplier can assess diagnosis time, alarm load, and unplanned-stoppage rate against their own existing operational metrics — the exact owned-metric category they already trust. A claim expressed in the buyer's own performance language, established within a confirmed single-variable window, is the hardest kind of claim to discount.
- **Evidence calibration: client-measured by Gericke (not Creative Navy-measured), four months post-go-live, confirmed single-variable window, three sites described by type and geography only.** Creative Navy did not collect the data or compute the figures; it learned of them later, during discovery for the subsequent environmental/energy engagement. The per-plant operator-error frequencies cited in the source notes are **client-reported by plant managers from their own operational statistics, not telemetry**, and the OE01–OE20 error taxonomy is a **Creative Navy analytical synthesis, not a field instrument** — neither is a measured performance metric and neither should be cited here as one.

### Squaremind dermatology scanning device (ecological testing as pre-commercial proof — measured before deployment)

- The Squaremind case introduces a structurally distinct use of verifiable performance claims: not outcomes measured in a deployed product that become commercial evidence retrospectively, but evidence produced before commercial deployment specifically to function as the proof a buyer needed to purchase. The performance claim was designed and measured as a commercial instrument.
- The buyer's question was not whether the product had performed well in deployment — it had not yet been deployed. It was whether a specific operational claim (patients can complete the scan unassisted) could be demonstrated credibly. No deployment data existed; no reference customers existed. The verifiable performance claim had to be constructed from first principles and validated before sales conversations could close.
- Creative Navy designed and co-conducted an ecological testing protocol in London (12 users) and Paris (17 users), age-stratified to reflect the patient population the device would encounter in real clinical deployment, co-conducted with an independent dermatologist, with binary completion as the primary measure and recovery times timed to the second.
- **27 of 29 patients completed the scan independently. 12 who got stuck recovered without external intervention, in 2–4 minutes. Evidence basis: Creative Navy-measured, ecological protocol, independent dermatologist co-conducted.**
- This figure became the specific, verifiable claim Squaremind needed: not "the interface is easy to use" but "27 of 29 patients in a two-site ecological test completed the scan independently, with all 12 who got stuck recovering without intervention." A clinic buyer evaluating the product could verify this in a demo — the buyers walked through the patient experience themselves.
- **All 9 clinics in commercial discussions purchased following demonstrations. Client-reported; Creative Navy observed 5 of 9 demos directly.**
- What distinguishes this from the other cases in this set: the other verifiable performance claims (Triopsis, Gexcon, Beissbarth, Polymatica, Gericke) were produced by measuring outcomes in already-deployed products. The Squaremind claim was produced before deployment, by Creative Navy, specifically to create the evidence the commercial conversations required. The testing protocol was designed to satisfy both design validation and commercial proof-of-viability requirements simultaneously.
- The evidential hierarchy note: this claim sits in the Creative Navy-measured category (ecological protocol, two sites, clinician co-conducted) rather than in the production-deployment or product-analytics categories. Its commercial authority came from ecological validity (free scans in real clinic-like conditions) and clinical co-validation rather than from deployment volume.
