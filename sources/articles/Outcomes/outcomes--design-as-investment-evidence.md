# `/evidence/outcomes/design-as-investment-evidence`

---

## What the outcome is

This outcome is distinct from `/positioning-through-interface-quality`, which concerns interface quality functioning as a market signal within a competitive landscape. This page concerns interface quality functioning as evidence in a resource-commitment decision before a competitive landscape has been fully entered — when there are no deployment volumes, no customer references, and no market adoption data to present. In this context, the designed interface is often the most credible evidence available that the product's capability has been thought through.

The mechanism: those deciding whether to commit resources to an early-stage product face a specific question — not whether the backend technology is strong (technical demonstrations can answer that) but whether the team has solved the harder problem of making that capability accessible to the users who need it. A well-designed interface communicates this without requiring assertion. The qualities that make an interface effective for users — clear information hierarchy, resolved structural tensions, coherent workflow logic — are the same qualities that communicate considered product judgment to a reader using the interface as evidence. This holds whether the reader is a private investor deciding whether to back a company or a public commissioner deciding whether to fund a build: each is asking a version of the same question, and the designed artefact answers it in the same way.

The framing constraint: the claim here is not that Creative Navy produces fundraising or commissioning outcomes. The claim is that design of sufficient quality communicates product readiness in ways that become relevant in resource-commitment contexts. The decision involves many factors outside design's control; what this page establishes is that design quality entered the evidentiary record in each case.

---

## Three structurally distinct mechanisms

The three private-investment engagements in this evidence set illustrate three different ways that design quality functions as investment evidence. Each mechanism is worth naming:

**1. Interface as dominant pitch content** — in a pre-revenue product with approximately ten beta users, the designed interface comprises the majority of investor pitch content. Evidence: Veecle.

**2. Interface as answer to a specific due diligence question** — the prototype directly demonstrates that a named investor concern (can expert AI capability be made accessible to non-expert users?) has been structurally resolved. The investment thesis could not close without this demonstration. Evidence: Owkin/K.

**3. Interface enabling commercial traction that enables investment** — the interface did not appear in an investor pitch; it made the product saleable to customers first, and the commercial traction that followed created the conditions for investment. The causal chain runs through sales performance rather than through investor demonstration directly. Evidence: Hudex.

These three mechanisms are worth distinguishing because they represent different stages, different investor questions, and different commercial contexts.

Two further extensions of the outcome are documented below: a **public-commission register**, where the backer is a government or public-private consortium rather than a private investor (Neugo), and **durability as investment evidence**, where the design retains value across ownership changes (the longitudinal set). Both are the same outcome class — design as material evidence in a resource-commitment decision — operating in a different register, and each is kept distinct so its calibration is not blurred into the three private-investor cases.

## Domain vocabulary

- Design as investment evidence — design quality functioning as a material factor in investment decisions
- Investment readiness — the degree to which a product demonstrates the qualities investors use to assess fundability: that the hard structural problems have been solved, that the team understands the user, that there is a viable paradigm
- Due diligence question — the specific concern an investor needs answered before committing; in AI and deep-tech contexts, often: "can this capability be made accessible to the intended user?"
- Prototype as pitch artefact — the designed interface used as the primary communication vehicle in an investment demonstration; answers questions that verbal description and technical specification cannot
- Public commission / commissioning decision — the public-sector analogue of an investment decision: a government or public-private consortium deciding whether to fund a build. The artefact's job is the same as in investor contexts — making an opportunity legible enough to commit resources to (Neugo)
- Product thinking — what the design communicates about the team's quality of judgment; not a visual quality but a structural one — evidence that the hard problems have been considered and resolved
- Pre-revenue context — the stage where verifiable commercial performance data doesn't exist; design communicates what deployments eventually would
- Commercial enablement as investment mechanism (Hudex) — interface enabling commercial traction, which creates the conditions for investment, rather than design functioning as pitch material directly

## Evidence

### Veecle automotive embedded development IDE (interface as dominant pitch content)
- Context: cloud-based IDE for automotive and embedded software engineers; beta stage, approximately ten users, no revenue at engagement start
- **Designs used in investor demonstrations where the interface comprised approximately 70% of the pitch content. Client-reported.**
- **£2M development funding unlocked. Client reported the engagement as instrumental in this outcome. Client-reported. No independent verification of the funding figure or the attribution.**
- The mechanism: in a pre-revenue product, the interface is the most credible available evidence that the product's ambition has been matched with execution quality. The 70% figure is a client's characterisation of pitch composition — not a precise measurement, but the direct statement from the person who gave the pitch.
- What the interface had to demonstrate: not just that something was built, but that the team understood the complexity of embedded software engineering workflows and had made considered decisions about how to structure them. Investors reading the IDE interface could assess the quality of those decisions without needing to understand the technical domain themselves.
- All designs were implemented by Veecle's development team. Client-reported.

### Owkin / K biomedical AI (interface as due diligence answer)
- Context: Owkin preparing an investment pitch for K, its AI copilot for biomedical research built on biology-specific datasets and reasoning models
- The central investor question: not whether the backend technology was strong — it demonstrably was — but whether Owkin had solved the harder problem of making expert AI capability accessible to a broader clinical user base. This was the named investor concern.
- Creative Navy produced a prototype serving as the lead pitch artefact. The prototype was the first thing investors saw; it demonstrated directly that a viable user paradigm existed.
- **£5M investment. Owkin attributed this to the design work. Client-reported; figure is approximate. Causal attribution made directly by the client.**
- The mechanism differs from Veecle: in the Veecle case, the interface was a large share of pitch content — a signal of execution quality in a pre-revenue product. In the Owkin/K case, the interface was answering a specific investor concern that determined whether the investment thesis held. The prototype was not evidence of a polished product; it was the demonstration that a named structural problem had been solved.
- **Evidence calibration: client-reported to Creative Navy. Causal framing stated by client. No independent verification of the funding figure.**

### Hudex intelligence analysis platform (commercial enablement as investment mechanism)
- Context: Hudex v2 platform for AI-powered intelligence analysis; existing users and demonstrable backend algorithmic capability, but version 1 interface was not compelling to non-expert users or in demos
- The redesign addressed this structurally: progressive disclosure entry layer, project overview as "book cover," accessible visual language that projected credibility without requiring analyst mediation to communicate value
- **£3M investment received 3 months into the growth phase following platform launch. Client explicitly attributed the design as critical and foundational to the product's ability to sell and to establishing the commercial growth phase. Client-reported.**
- The mechanism differs from both Veecle and Owkin/K: the interface did not appear in an investor pitch, and the investment question was not a single named due diligence concern. The redesigned interface made the product sellable to customers; customers' commercial adoption created the traction; the traction created the conditions for the £3M investment three months later. The causal chain runs through sales performance rather than directly through investor demonstration.
- **Evidence calibration: client-reported causal link. The client stated the design was key to the product's commercial growth phase. The three-month gap between launch and investment reflects this mechanism: the design enabled commercial traction, commercial traction enabled investment, not design → investment directly.**

### Neugo UK visa application case-management platform (public-commission register)
- Context: a public-private consortium, together with the UK Home Office, deciding whether to commission a net-new visa application case-management platform. No product existed; the decision was whether to fund the build. The backer here is a government / public-private consortium, not a private investor.
- Creative Navy's first engagement (7 weeks) produced a visionary, conceptual design — a value/desirability mapping and a clickable Figma prototype — as the artefact that made the opportunity legible and fundable. (See `/contexts/emerging-products` and `/method/sandbox-experiments` for the origination and value-elicitation detail.)
- **The design contributed to the system being commissioned. Stakeholders reported the design accounted for roughly 30% of the decision factors, and that dedicated demo sessions were built around the prototype. Client-reported.**
- The mechanism is structurally closest to the Veecle case — the prototype as the dominant artefact in the room where the funding decision is made — but the audience differs: a public commissioner deciding whether to fund a build, not a private investor deciding whether to back a company. The commissioner's question is the public-sector analogue of the investor's: is the opportunity real and well enough conceived to commit resources to building it?
- Distinctive among the cases on this page: Neugo is the only one where the originated concept can be followed downstream into a deployed system. The design that helped win the commission became a platform that, at a post-launch audit roughly a year after go-live, 15 legal firms were relying on (Creative Navy-observed; see `/evidence/longitudinal`). Caveat: that reliance validates the *built* product and ran through a separate build engagement — it is not a measure of the commissioning artefact itself. But it is the one case here where "the design helped unlock the commitment" can be connected to "the thing got built and depended on."
- **Evidence calibration: client-reported. The ~30% figure and the demo-session arrangement are the stakeholders' account to Creative Navy. Do not state the design as having secured the commission — it was a substantial, client-reported component of a multi-factor public decision. The Home Office and the platform name (Neugo) may be named; the consulting company and the development company must not be.**

### The three mechanisms together

The three private-investor cases establish that design quality can function as investment evidence through structurally different causal chains:

- **Pitch content** (Veecle): the interface demonstrates execution quality in the absence of deployment evidence
- **Due diligence answer** (Owkin/K): the interface resolves a specific named investor concern that determines whether the thesis holds
- **Commercial enablement** (Hudex): the interface makes the product saleable, commercial traction follows, investment follows the traction

Each mechanism is appropriate to a different stage and a different investor question. Together, they establish that this is not a single mechanism but a class of outcomes that design quality can produce across varying contexts.

### The public-commission register (a distinct backer, not just a distinct mechanism)

The three mechanisms above vary by causal chain but share an audience: a private investor deciding whether to back a company. Neugo extends the outcome to a different audience entirely — a public commissioner, a government or public-private consortium, deciding whether to fund a build. The underlying outcome is the same (design of sufficient quality becomes material evidence in a resource-commitment decision), and the mechanism is closest to Veecle's (the prototype as the dominant artefact in the room), but naming the register matters because the backer's incentives, evaluation process, and decision criteria differ from a private investor's: a public commission runs through governance and lobbying processes, not a funding round. The framing constraint holds identically and is, if anything, more important here — Creative Navy did not "win the commission." The design was a client-reported ~30% factor in a multi-factor public decision built partly around demo sessions of the prototype.

### Durability as investment evidence (longitudinal — a further mechanism)

Beyond funding unlocked at the point of engagement, the `/evidence/longitudinal` set evidences the design as a *durable asset* — one that holds value across years and across changes of ownership. In two documented cases the system was preserved by a third-party acquirer: Chemical Watch (acquired at a 24x EBITDA multiple roughly a year after launch; the acquirer, Enhesa, kept the Creative Navy design system near-intact and built later work within it) and Greenlight (built out, launched, and sold; the acquirer kept the system in its portfolio). An acquirer choosing to preserve rather than replace an inherited system — having both the authority and the usual incentive to absorb it into its own design language — is a revealed-preference signal that the design was an asset worth keeping.

This is a distinct mechanism from the funding and commissioning cases above: there, the design helped *unlock* a resource commitment; here, the design *retained* value through the product's commercial life and ownership transitions. Both belong on this page; keep them separate.

Calibration: the acquisitions are stateable; the preservation facts are observed/client-reported. The 24x EBITDA multiple is client-reported (the design's role in it is the client's account of the causal chain, not an independent attribution). For Greenlight the sale is stateable but the acquirer is not named. Exact denominator and per-lineage detail are the source of record at `/evidence/longitudinal`.
