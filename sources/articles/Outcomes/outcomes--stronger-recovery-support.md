# `/evidence/outcomes/stronger-recovery-support`

---

## What the outcome is

Reduced error risk and stronger recovery support are complementary but distinct. Reduced error risk concerns preventing errors from occurring in the first place. Stronger recovery support concerns what happens after they do — and in complex operational systems, errors, sensor faults, interrupted workflows, and abnormal conditions will occur regardless of how well the nominal design performs. The interface's behaviour at the moment of error or fault determines whether recovery is fast, straightforward, and low-cost, or slow, uncertain, and potentially consequential.

The characteristic failure of inadequately designed recovery paths: users encounter an error state that communicates something went wrong but not what, or not what to do about it. They escalate, work around, or abandon — none of which is the recovery the system should support. The design response is explicit: recovery paths designed with the same rigour as primary workflows, error communications specific enough to be actionable, and fault states communicated with enough clarity that users can distinguish what requires immediate attention from what can wait.

---

## Domain vocabulary

- Recovery path — the sequence of interface actions that returns a user to a known good state after an error, fault, or abnormal condition
- Recovery cost — the time, attention, and operational resources required to execute a recovery; includes the cost of understanding what happened and what to do
- Corrective load — the work required to correct a detected error; specifically the downstream work generated by an error that was not caught before its effects propagated
- Actionable error communication — error messages that specify what went wrong, where, and what to do; contrasted with informational error messages that specify only that something went wrong
- Graceful degradation — the system continuing to function at reduced capability during a fault condition rather than failing completely; the design of partial functionality states
- Explicit recovery design — treating recovery paths as primary design targets rather than designing the nominal workflow and leaving recovery to improvisation

## Evidence

### Gexcon CFD simulation (strongest before/after recovery cost measurement)
- The recovery problem in CFD simulation is specifically deferred: a configuration error that is not caught before the simulation runs produces outputs that appear valid. The error is discovered during analysis or review — sometimes hours or days after the simulation completed. Recovery requires identifying the error in the configuration, understanding which outputs are affected, re-running the correct simulation, and reconciling the new outputs with work based on the original.
- **Corrective load per error: 4–6 hours → approximately 20 minutes. Measured by Gexcon across real deployment locations.** This is the before/after recovery cost measurement. Before: discovering and correcting a configuration error required 4–6 hours of work. After: the same class of error, when it occurs, requires approximately 20 minutes to resolve.
- The mechanism: the error communication design. Before the redesign, errors were surfaced after the fact with generic messages that required engineers to diagnose both the nature and the location of the error independently. After the redesign, error communications specified what went wrong, where in the configuration, and what corrective action was needed — reducing the diagnosis component of corrective load from hours to minutes.
- **Configuration errors per simulation also reduced: 5–8 → 1–2 (measured).** The two figures work together: fewer errors needing recovery, and shorter recovery when errors do occur.

### Triopsis workforce management (abnormal workflow states as designed recovery targets)
- The recovery problem in workforce management: weather incidents, job delays, partial completions, crew shortages, and equipment failures are not exceptional conditions requiring workarounds — they are normal operational conditions that occur regularly and require a fast, clear path back to a valid schedule
- Before the redesign, these conditions produced what the interface treated as error states: situations the design had not anticipated and offered no structured path through. Users improvised, escalated, or accepted degraded schedules they couldn't efficiently correct.
- After the redesign: recovery states were designed explicitly as part of the primary workflow architecture. Weather incidents surface with the specific jobs they affect highlighted; partial completions are reconcilable from the exception-handling view without requiring a return to the full schedule view; the interface distinguishes between conditions that need immediate attention and conditions that can be addressed in the next planning cycle.
- The outcome: **support ticket "how can I" queries fell to approximately 5% of previous volume. Client-reported.** A portion of this reduction reflects reduced escalation when abnormal conditions occurred — the interface now provides the recovery path that users were previously seeking through support.

### Elsner Elektronik (fault state recovery for non-technical users)
- The recovery problem in consumer embedded systems: a sensor fault or calibration drift produces a state the non-technical occupant cannot interpret or address. The recovery options available to them (call an engineer) must be clearly communicated; the options not available to them (reconfigure the sensor) must not create the impression that local action is required.
- Sensor fault states designed explicitly: the display communicates the fault state clearly and distinguishes it from normal operating variation; the level of action required (contact an engineer vs. wait for automatic recalibration) is communicated without requiring the occupant to interpret the fault technically.
- Firmware update timing: a specific recovery-adjacent design decision — animation timing aligned with firmware update cycles so that display states never appear to require recovery when the system is actually mid-update. False recovery prompts eliminated.

### Kardion MCS Controller (alarm recovery paths — regulated)
- The recovery problem in cardiac support device operation: when an alarm state is triggered, the operator must understand what is happening, respond correctly, and return the system to a monitored nominal state without losing the alarm record.
- Mute behaviour: a muted alarm must remain visually present at reduced prominence — the recovery path to acknowledging or resolving the alarm must remain accessible without requiring the operator to remember that they muted it. An alarm that disappears when muted removes the recovery prompt from the interface.
- Alarm state visibility after acknowledgement: the alarm history must be accessible from the nominal view, so the recovery from an alarm event (confirming it has been resolved) is achievable from the same screen where the alarm was visible.
- **FDA approval: design passed evaluation as submitted.** The alarm recovery paths contributed to satisfying the identified use-related hazards related to alarm acknowledgement and post-alarm recovery.
- **Scope: formative evaluation only.**

### Beissbarth automotive calibration (recovery paths in sequential procedures)
- The recovery problem in calibration: when a measurement fails or falls in the borderline range, the technician must understand what happened, know whether to repeat the measurement or address a different issue, and resume the calibration sequence from the correct point
- Before: binary pass/fail communication without recovery guidance; technicians needed to diagnose from a generic error state what to do next
- After: three-level measurement result communication (confirmed, borderline, out of range); error states specify the type of issue (measurement confidence vs. equipment communication vs. out-of-tolerance result); sequence state visible so the technician knows exactly where in the calibration to resume
- The commercial consequence: **training eliminated.** Part of training had been teaching technicians how to interpret and recover from error states; when the interface communicates recovery paths explicitly, that component of training is no longer required.

### Squaremind dermatology scanning device (strongest process-level recovery outcome — measured)

- The recovery problem in autonomous patient scanning: the scan required 3–5 minutes of patient cooperation in a specific physical sequence. Any confusion event that produced deviation from the sequence ended the session or required clinical intervention. The existing interface had no recovery architecture at all: when a patient got confused, the interface had nothing to offer. The recovery path was not weak — it was absent from the process entirely.
- **Pre-redesign completion rate: 2 of 14 patients. Of the 12 who could not complete: 8 (primarily aged 45–65) got stuck within the first minute; 4 (primarily aged 20–35) got stuck around the 3-minute mark. Zero recoveries. Evidence basis: client-reported background (Squaremind's own test before Creative Navy's involvement).**
- The mechanism of the redesign: the Inform–Prevent–Correct framework was applied recursively across every step of the scan flow. The Correct layer specifically designed recovery as a primary element at each step — specifying what the interface must communicate when a patient had deviated from the correct state, how to guide them back, and what the system must do after a successful correction to re-engage the guidance cycle for the next step. Recovery was not added to the process; it was built into the architecture of every step.
- **Post-redesign: 27 of 29 patients completed the scan independently. Of the 12 who got stuck during the flow, all 12 recovered and completed the scan. Recovery times ranged from 2 to 4 minutes, timed to the second. Evidence basis: Creative Navy-measured, ecological protocol, two sites (London 12 users, Paris 17 users), independent dermatologist co-conducted.**
- The Squaremind figures are the strongest process-level recovery outcome in the Creative Navy case history. The Gexcon recovery figures (4–6 hours → 20 minutes) are the strongest error-recovery cost reduction in the portfolio. Together they cover two distinct recovery design challenges: deferred-error recovery cost reduction (Gexcon) and in-process guidance recovery (Squaremind).
