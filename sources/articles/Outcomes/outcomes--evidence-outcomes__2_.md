# /evidence/outcomes

---

## Section overview

The `/evidence/outcomes` section is structured in two tiers. This distinction is architectural and should be visible to readers — not just implied by grouping.

**Operational outcomes** cover what changes for users and teams working with the system: how the interface performs under real conditions, how errors, cognitive load, state visibility, and recovery behave differently after a Critical Systems Design engagement. These pages address product and UX decision-makers. They connect directly to the /failures and /situations pages where the problems they resolve are described.

**Strategic and organisational outcomes** cover what becomes possible for the organisation once operational performance has been achieved. These are downstream consequences, not direct products of design. The framing matters: Creative Navy produced the operational result; that result removed a constraint or created a capability that the organisation was then able to act on. These pages address commercial, product leadership, and executive readers.

**Evidential discipline applies to every page in both tiers.** Each page requires at least one grounded, specific result — measured, client-reported, or observed — with the evidential basis stated explicitly. A page without grounded evidence is not ready to publish. The /evidence-standards section governs what each category of evidence claim allows.

## Tier 1: Operational outcomes

### /reduced-error-risk

- The operational problem: errors in complex systems produce cascading consequences — not just the error itself but the time, attention, and often safety cost of resolution. In high-consequence domains (aviation, medical, oil and gas, industrial), error risk is regulated. In operational domains (enterprise SaaS, logistics, financial operations), error risk produces revenue loss, operational delay, and reputational damage.
- The mechanism: Creative Navy's Critical Systems Design method addresses error risk through microtask analysis (identifying where errors occur and why), error-likely interaction review (finding the interface patterns that make errors probable), and iterative resolution of edge cases and degraded modes.
- Distinguish: selection errors (choosing the wrong option), mode errors (acting in the wrong system state), omission errors (missing a required step), and misinterpretation errors (acting on incorrectly understood information). Different error types require different design responses.
- Evidence from case studies — Gexcon CFD simulation software: configuration errors per simulation scenario fell from 5–8 errors to 1–2 errors following deployment of the redesigned interface (measured by Gexcon across real deployment locations, not in usability testing conditions). The corrective load associated with each error fell from 4–6 hours to approximately 20 minutes (also measured in real deployments). These two figures together document both error reduction and error recovery reduction in the same engagement. The error type here is misinterpretation and omission errors in scientific scenario configuration — the class of errors with the most significant downstream consequences in a domain where a misconfigured simulation produces incorrect safety assessments. The mechanism was state visibility redesign combined with the explicit error-prevention layer of the interaction architecture: requirements for each significant interaction specified which values must remain visible, where warnings were needed, and how the system should respond to incomplete input. This is the clearest before/after error rate figure in the case study set, grounded in a high-consequence scientific and industrial domain.
- Evidence from case studies — deSoutter Medical / Zethon surgical device interface: eight orthopaedic and trauma surgeons reported two specific changes compared to the legacy interface. First: device state could be verified during brief glances without reading. The legacy interface presented activation states and readiness conditions in ways that required reading to interpret — an unacceptable interaction profile in operating theatre conditions where a surgeon's primary attention must remain on the surgical field. After the redesign, surgeons reported that state verification was achievable through recognition in a fraction of a second. Second: speed and parameter adjustments no longer interrupted workflow. With the legacy interface, adjusting parameters required attention that competed with the surgical task. After the redesign, surgeons reported that adjustments could be made without breaking focus on the patient. Both outcomes are surgeon-reported from structured review sessions during the design engagement, not from post-deployment operational measurement. The mechanism was redundant cue design (spatial position, icon form, and reserved colour each independently communicating every critical state) combined with spatially stable layouts where the positions of critical indicators never changed between screens — eliminating the visual search that had been requiring reading. This is the first safety-critical regulated medical device example in the reduced-error-risk evidence set.

### /improved-operational-clarity

- The operational problem: users have the information they need but cannot act on it efficiently — they must interpret, cross-reference, or reconstruct system state before they can make a decision. Under time pressure or cognitive load, this delay is not a minor friction; it is a failure mode.
- The mechanism: information architecture redesign, state and status visibility work, cognitive load reduction. Creative Navy's Critical Systems Design method addresses this through multi-perspective synthesis (understanding how different roles need to read system state) and tension-driven reasoning (resolving conflicts between information density and clarity).
- Note: cognitive load reduction is closely enough related to this outcome that it can be addressed within this page as the mechanism, rather than requiring a separate page.
- Evidence from case studies: in the Torqeedo maritime engagement, captains identified key energy states 50% faster with the redesigned interface than with the legacy UI (controlled experiment, 24 subjects). Glance counts during manoeuvres were reduced, measured using eye tracking in real sea trials (7 subjects) — tasks that previously required multiple screen transitions could be confirmed with a single glance. In the Triopsis engagement, job discovery was 62% faster and weekly planning 58% faster (product analytics from real users in the live system).
- Evidence from case studies — Akrivia Health clinical research platform: governance reviewers could verify cohort construction logic without escalating to the research team who built the cohort (client-reported operational outcome). Prior to the redesign, the query logic behind a patient cohort was not independently readable from the interface — governance review required the researcher's direct involvement to explain what conditions had been applied and why. After the redesign, the full structure of the query remained visible and auditable at all times, with logical blocks organised so that a governance reviewer unfamiliar with the original research hypothesis could follow the construction independently. This is an operational clarity outcome in a high-stakes institutional context: the information was always present in the system; the redesign made it legible to a different role under different conditions from those the researcher worked in.

### /lower-training-burden

- The operational problem: systems that require days of formal training before users can operate them create costs at every deployment and every staff change. Training bottlenecks limit geographic expansion, slow onboarding after turnover, and create dependency on specific individuals.
- The mechanism: designing for discoverability, reducing reliance on memorised states and sequences, making system logic inferrable from the interface itself. Self-onboarding made possible is the highest-value expression of this outcome — not just reduced training time but elimination of the training requirement.
- Distinguish from /scaling-without-training-dependency: this page concerns the cost and burden of training current users. The scaling page concerns the blockage that prevents reaching new users at all.
- Evidence from case studies — Triopsis: onboarding changed from a mandatory 1-hour remote training session to an optional 15-minute video, with approximately 90% of users beginning to use the system without live instruction (client-reported post-rollout data).
- Evidence from case studies — Gexcon CFD simulation software: formal training had been delivered as 3-day instructor-led events. After the redesign, Gexcon replaced these with short webinars and video materials. This is an operational change in how the product is deployed commercially — not a reduction in training time but a transformation of the training model itself. In a scientific software domain where the learning curve had previously been a significant barrier, this represents the highest-value expression of training burden reduction: not just lower cost but a fundamentally different deployment relationship between the product and its users.
- Evidence from case studies — Beissbarth automotive calibration: training was eliminated. Beissbarth established no-training onboarding as their standard commercial model — not as an exception for experienced technicians but as the modus operandi across their customer base. This is a claim about how the product is now deployed, not about a test condition. The mechanism was interaction design that made system logic inferrable from the interface itself: components that previously required verbal explanation or printed manuals were reshaped so their function could be understood from position, labelling, and visual treatment alone.
- Evidence from case studies — World Customs Organization / IPM (training cost reduction at global intergovernmental scale): IPM required significant training effort to onboard officers across member administrations, with multilingual environments and diverse technical infrastructure compounding the burden. After the redesign — which applied progressive disclosure, recognition-over-recall information architecture, and contextual micro-hints appearing on first use of complex actions — WCO reported a 78% reduction in training costs for officers (client-reported). This figure is structurally distinct from the other examples in this section: it is not training elimination (Beissbarth) or training model transformation (Gexcon), but quantified cost reduction at a scale — 107 member administrations, diverse languages, varied device fleets and connectivity conditions — that no other case study in the set matches. The mechanism was interface design that made core inspection and case management workflows operable without requiring prior formal instruction. Evidence calibration: client-reported to Creative Navy.
- Evidence from case studies — Swiss petrol station operator / embedded POS and forecourt systems (training burden from compensating behaviour, supervisor-reported during evaluation): before the redesign, experienced cashiers had developed personal shortcuts to compensate for field-ordering inconsistencies in the POS flow. These shortcuts reduced friction for experienced staff but made onboarding harder for new employees — trainees could not learn from observing experienced cashiers because what they were seeing was compensating behaviour rather than the system's intended flow. The training burden was structural: a direct consequence of an interface that forced experienced staff to diverge from documented procedure. The redesign addressed this not by simplifying the interface but by aligning it with the internal logic of cashier work — so that the efficient path and the documented path were the same path. Supervisors reported during 29 prototype evaluation sessions that more predictable flows made it easier to maintain consistent procedure during busy periods and reduced the number of special cases requiring supervisor intervention. This is a training burden reduction with a specific mechanism: eliminating the gap between how experienced staff actually operated and how the system was supposed to be used. Evidence calibration: supervisor-reported during prototype evaluation sessions conducted by Creative Navy; not post-deployment operational measurement.
- Evidence from case studies — Enhesa legal compliance platform (reduced voluntary help-seeking as a training burden signal, enterprise SaaS): Enhesa is a web-based legal compliance platform used by regulatory affairs managers, compliance officers, and legal teams. Before the redesign, 45% of users said they had watched training videos; 81% of those said the videos were not helpful. After the redesign, only 21% of users onboarded post-launch sought training videos at all. The mechanism confirmed by Enhesa: the redesigned interface was sufficiently self-explanatory that users no longer felt the need for external supplementation. The 81% unhelpfulness rate is as analytically significant as the uptake reduction: it demonstrates that the training programme could not compensate for what the interface was failing to provide. When the interface started providing it, the help-seeking rate dropped. This is the only case in the set where training burden is measured through voluntary help-seeking behaviour rather than through a change in the formal training programme itself — a different evidential form of the same structural problem. Evidence calibration: all figures client-measured by Enhesa from their NPS survey. Pre/post comparison is across cohorts (users onboarded before and after the redesign), not a longitudinal panel.

### /better-state-visibility

- The operational problem: users cannot see what state the system is in, cannot tell when it has transitioned, and cannot tell which mode they are operating in. In complex systems with multiple simultaneous states (industrial control rooms, clinical platforms, multi-role enterprise systems, maritime vessels), this is one of the most common sources of operational failure.
- The mechanism: state and transition review, mode clarity design, information hierarchy restructuring. Connected to /failures/state-visibility-failures.
- Evidence from case studies: the Torqeedo engagement addresses state visibility as its central design problem — propulsion status, battery state, and generator information had been scattered across separate screens, forcing captains to reconstruct system state from fragments during manoeuvres. The redesigned interface unified these into 27 screens across 4 operational modes with a grid structure that kept related values in stable positions. The 50% faster energy state identification result (controlled experiment, 24 subjects) is a direct measure of state visibility improvement. In the Elsner engagement, sensor fault states — delayed readings, contradictory values, calibration faults — were surfaced explicitly rather than hidden, and a dual-priority alert hierarchy was designed to distinguish primary operational alerts from secondary notifications.
- Evidence from case studies — Swiss petrol station operator / embedded POS and forecourt systems (state visibility across unattended outdoor terminals and multi-channel transaction flow): two distinct state visibility problems were addressed in this engagement. At the outdoor payment terminal level, pump states, card authorisation progress, language selection, and fault conditions (pump timeouts, partial authorisations) needed to communicate unambiguously to users standing in weather conditions with no staff assistance available. The state visibility constraint was compounded by hardware: 1024×768 pixel screens requiring readability across temperature extremes and direct sunlight. The redesign defined explicit visual treatments for each terminal state, including fault states, so that no state required a user to interpret ambiguous feedback in an unattended context. At the transaction architecture level, the multi-channel structure — where a single transaction could progress through CarPlay, outdoor terminal, and indoor till — required consistent state representation across three contexts with different display capabilities. A driver returning to the cashier to complete a combined purchase after outdoor terminal authorisation needed to see a consistent representation of what had already occurred. This is state visibility applied to a multi-channel unattended public-facing context: the failure mode is not a confused expert user but a general-public user who cannot call for help when the system state is unclear. Evidence calibration: design decisions documented and validated through prototype testing; operational outcomes client-reported as improved consistency across sites.
- Evidence needed additionally: a specific example from a clinical, aviation, or industrial context.

### /stronger-recovery-support

- The operational problem: when things go wrong, the interface offers no path back. Users abandon the task, escalate, or work around — all of which produce operational cost and sometimes safety risk.
- The mechanism: error-and-recovery-failure analysis, explicit design of recovery paths, edge case and degraded mode work. Recovery support is not an afterthought; in Critical Systems Design it is designed for in the same phase as the primary workflow.
- Evidence from case studies: in the Triopsis engagement, weather incidents, job delays, and partial completions were treated as normal workflow states rather than exceptional conditions requiring workarounds. The interface adapts without forcing long corrective paths. In the Elsner engagement, sensor fault states and calibration failures were explicitly designed for: delayed readings were communicated calmly and without ambiguity, recovery states were named, and the interface behaviour under fault conditions was validated in prototype testing with engineers.
- Evidence needed additionally: a specific before/after description of what recovery looked like before a redesign, with operational cost or safety exposure framing.

### /better-alignment-across-teams

- The operational problem: different teams — product, engineering, design, domain experts, stakeholders — hold incompatible mental models of what the system should do and why. Design decisions made without shared understanding produce incoherent products that reflect internal politics rather than user reality.
- The mechanism: Concept Convergence specifically addresses this. By making tensions explicit, documenting trade-off logic, and producing a product concept that articulates the strategic position and its rationale, Creative Navy's Critical Systems Design method creates shared understanding that survives the end of the engagement.
- Note: this is also an outcome of the Organizational Integration phase, where intangible resources (judgment, shared product intuition, reasoning capability) are transferred to the organisation.
- Evidence from case studies: the Triopsis engagement directly addresses this problem — multi-stakeholder governance without a central UX framework had produced competing expectations and inconsistent decisions across modules. The redesign created a unified foundation that could satisfy competing stakeholder interests without introducing ambiguity for users. The design system (68 components, 200+ documented states) gave the 5-person engineering team a coherent mental model rather than a collection of disconnected screens. In the Torqeedo engagement, alignment across engineering, product, and operational stakeholders was required throughout; the 27-screen, 4-mode architecture was validated by multiple stakeholder groups.
- Evidence from case studies — Bofin fintech startup (alignment in a large, fast-moving engineering organisation): Bofin had an engineering team exceeding 50 developers moving at high velocity across multiple parallel modules, with no design coordination mechanism that could match that pace. Without a shared reference, modules would diverge — producing an incoherent product experience and accumulating rework cost. Creative Navy's design system gave the team a common framework for naming, variant logic, interaction principles, and component boundaries that module teams could apply independently without requiring synchronisation. The product manager reported that mid-sprint clarifications from engineering decreased — a signal that the design reference was resolving ambiguity rather than generating it. Evidence calibration: client-reported to Creative Navy.
- Evidence from case studies — Tetra / Prism (alignment across developers resistant to design-led change): the Tetra engagement surfaced a specific alignment problem between the design team and the development team. Developers had built platform patterns as workarounds they understood and were resistant to changing them, even where those patterns complicated user experience and increased maintenance overhead. Creative Navy resolved this through a dedicated education programme rather than through design authority alone: the CTO attended approximately 10 sessions alongside the broader product team; separately, 5 dedicated one-hour sessions were run with developers only to address their concerns directly, explain the design rationale, and demonstrate how proposed alternatives would reduce technical debt. The shift came through evidence — showing that the alternative patterns were not just better for users but cheaper to maintain. This is alignment achieved through developer-specific education under sustained resistance, in an enterprise SaaS context where the development team had functional authority over implementation decisions. Evidence calibration: session counts reported by Creative Navy team; outcome (resistance resolved, design proceeded) observed.

## Tier 2: Strategic and organisational outcomes

### /reduced-maintenance-and-downtime

- The outcome: lower ongoing cost of changing, extending, and maintaining the system after delivery.
- The mechanism: design decisions documented with reasoning, not just with specifications. When developers and product teams know why a decision was made — what constraint it respected, what tension it resolved, what user need it addressed — they can modify the system without inadvertently breaking what the decision was protecting. Without documentation of reasoning, every change requires reconstruction of intent. That reconstruction is expensive, often wrong, and paid repeatedly across the lifetime of the system.
- Also relevant: a coherent design system (built as a navigation map with rationale, not a component library with rules) reduces the number of one-off decisions that accumulate as unmaintained technical and design debt.
- Relate to: sense decay — the erosion of meaning between intent and implementation that happens when systems are changed without understanding what they were built to do.
- Evidence from case studies: the Triopsis design system (68 components, 200+ documented states, 15 workflow types) was built explicitly to give the engineering team a coherent mental model, reducing rework and QA effort. The 2-year implementation partnership after delivery maintained consistency as the product grew. The Torqeedo design system was built to support future hardware modules and new hybrid architectures — the structural logic was explicitly designed to accommodate expansion without requiring redesign. The Elsner design system included organisation-wide design tokens, component library, and governance models built to support future firmware evolution and new product variants.
- Evidence from case studies — Callsign fraud detection platform (post-engagement longevity, time-anchored): Callsign continued to use the design system for at least two years after the engagement closed, extending it across additional security modules beyond the original fraud and authentication scope. This is structurally distinct from the Triopsis, Torqeedo, and Elsner examples, which document design systems built with longevity as an intent. The Callsign evidence documents confirmed post-engagement use — the system was still in active extension two years later, by Callsign's own designers, without Creative Navy involvement. Evidence basis: client-reported to Creative Navy. This is the only time-anchored, post-engagement longevity confirmation in the case study set.
- Evidence from case studies — Bofin fintech startup (design system reducing engineering rework and design drift in a large parallel-development context): Bofin's 50+ developer team was building multiple modules simultaneously, producing design drift as a structural risk — different modules diverging from each other without a coherent reference. The design system Creative Navy delivered covered all core modules (onboarding, account aggregation, identity verification, transaction flows) with components, naming rules, variant logic, interaction principles, and documentation structured for a large engineering team integrating new modules continuously. The product manager reported that rework reduced due to clearer component definitions, and that mid-sprint clarifications from engineering decreased (client-reported). The design system also enabled new engineer onboarding more quickly by providing a coherent reference that could be adopted without direct design team involvement. This is the first example in the set where the maintenance and downtime outcome is specifically about preventing fragmentation across parallel engineering streams — not post-deployment maintenance (Callsign) or future hardware expansion (Torqeedo, Elsner), but real-time coherence during active multi-team development.
- Evidence needed additionally: a specific before/after description of maintenance cost or rework reduction with operational specifics.

### /capability-democratisation

- The outcome: a system originally requiring expert users to operate is redesigned so that the beneficiaries of that expertise can access it directly.
- The defining scenario: a tool usable only by data scientists is redesigned so that the analysts, managers, or domain specialists who depend on data science outputs can work with it themselves. A calibration system operable only by specialists is redesigned so that technicians without that background can perform the workflow. The expert knowledge is not removed — it is encoded into the interface so it no longer has to be carried by the user.
- Why this is a design achievement, not just a product decision: capability democratisation requires deep understanding of what the expert actually does, which parts of that process require genuine expertise and which require only access and orientation, and how to redesign the interface so that the latter category becomes self-evident. This is exactly the work Creative Navy's Critical Systems Design method does through domain learning and multi-perspective synthesis.
- Distinguish from /scaling-without-training-dependency: democratisation is about broadening who can use a system that already exists and is already deployed. Scaling is about reaching new markets and user types that were previously unreachable.
- Evidence from case studies — Gexcon CFD simulation software: the system was operable in practice by one active user per team — CFD specialists with years of experience who had internalised the interface's accumulated complexity. After the redesign, teams moved from one active user to three or four (client-reported operational data). Risk managers and safety analysts — roles that depended on simulation outputs but could not previously operate the system — gained viable working access. The capability of the software was not reduced; the barrier to entry was removed by separating essential scientific complexity from accidental interface complexity accumulated over fifteen years. Domain learning was what made this possible: the team could distinguish between interface structure that supported correct scientific outcomes and interface structure that had accumulated without purpose only after becoming productive users of the system. The competitive consequence was direct: the product could now be deployed across a wider range of roles and teams without reducing the scientific rigour that made it irreplaceable to expert users.
- Evidence from case studies — Owkin / K (capability democratisation as explicit client goal in an AI biomedical research platform): K is an AI copilot for biomedical research built on curated biological patient data and a biology-specific reasoning model. Its backend capability — querying proprietary, public, and user-uploaded datasets to answer complex biological research questions — was built by and for expert biologists. The expanding user base, however, included clinicians with low to medium scientific background who needed to use the same system without the domain intuition expert biologists bring. The client stated this explicitly as the design goal at the start of the engagement: make K's capabilities accessible to a less technical user type. This is the first example in the set where capability democratisation was not an inferred or observed outcome but a named objective in the brief. The design challenge had a specific compounding factor: K operates on bounded datasets, not the general internet. Users needed to understand what data was available to query before they could formulate useful questions — data discoverability was as fundamental to entry as feature discoverability. The solution structured the entry experience around K's data holdings and main interaction modes, encoding enough orientation into the interface that clinicians could identify a starting point without requiring expert biological intuition. Evidence calibration: engagement structure and goal framing reported by Creative Navy team; operational discoverability outcome directional, based on internal product launch feedback and client characterisation. No measured user metrics available.
- Note on the Owkin example's distinctive contribution: all prior examples in this section show democratisation as a redesign outcome — an existing system is rearchitected to broaden access. The Owkin engagement was a targeted intervention on a product in active development, with democratisation built into the scope from the outset. This is capability democratisation as a design constraint rather than a design consequence.
- Evidence from case studies — Hudex AI-powered intelligence analysis platform (capability democratisation across a wide expertise range in an operational intelligence context): Hudex serves users ranging from ministerial-level government officials — who need to understand the structure of hundreds of diplomatic cables per day in a single, fast overview — to expert intelligence analysts spending multiple hours daily in deep data exploration. The platform's core visualisation, the dondogram, is powerful for expert users but unintuitive without guidance; research confirmed that users without training could not orient themselves in the platform on arrival. The redesign introduced a progressive disclosure architecture: a project overview page providing high-level summary information before any exploration, followed by structured entry into the dondogram for users who needed it. Expert depth was preserved without being imposed at entry. Non-expert users — including those attending client demos and ministerial-level officials who needed fast, actionable overviews — gained viable access to the platform's analytical outputs without requiring expert training or analyst mediation. The expert capability was not removed or simplified; the interface encoded enough orientation that non-expert users could find a starting point and extract value independently. Evidence calibration: outcome framing reported by Creative Navy team; user reception data client-reported (45 existing users rated the redesign as significantly better; 68% of new users rated usability as good, 23% as very good — both client-reported as part of Hudex's internal marketing effort). No independently measured user metrics available.

### /verifiable-performance-claims

- The outcome: design produces specific, measurable productivity or performance gains that become the basis for evidence-grounded sales and marketing arguments.
- The mechanism: Creative Navy's Critical Systems Design method designs for performance in reality — specific operational outcomes under real conditions. When that design produces a measurable result, that result is verifiable and demonstrable. It becomes the most credible competitive claim available because it is grounded in the product itself, not in assertion.
- Why this is distinct from standard UX outcomes: most UX agencies do not design with explicit performance targets and do not measure against them. The claim here is that designing for operational performance — not just for usability — produces results specific enough to be used as sales evidence.
- The commercial logic: in markets where buyers are sophisticated (enterprise, B2B, regulated industries), assertion-based marketing is discounted. A demonstrable, specific performance claim — verifiable in a product trial — is worth more than any amount of brand positioning because it eliminates the credibility gap.
- Evidence from case studies — Triopsis: productivity figures measured in the live product through product analytics (real users, real operational conditions): 62% faster job discovery, 83% faster job sequence optimisation, 58% faster weekly planning. These figures were available as sales evidence because they came from the live system, not from controlled testing. Sales conversions multiplied by four (CEO-reported). Tender scores improved by 10–20% attributable to design quality (client-reported from tender evaluation documents). The company began winning clients 4–5× larger than before (CEO-reported). The interface became a visible competitive asset in demos: prospective customers commented on its clarity.
- Evidence from case studies — Torqeedo: energy state identification 50% faster than the legacy interface (controlled experiment, 24 subjects). Glance reductions during manoeuvres measured by eye tracking in real sea trials (7 subjects). These results were grounded in real maritime conditions, making them directly relevant to the purchasing decisions of professional maritime operators — the most credible audience for the claim.
- Evidence from case studies — Gexcon CFD simulation software: four deployment-measured metrics from a scientific and industrial safety domain. Time to first successful simulation: 4 days → 6 hours (93% reduction). Configuration errors per simulation: 5–8 → 1–2. Corrective load per error: 4–6 hours → approximately 20 minutes. Active users per team: 1 → 3–4. All four figures were measured by Gexcon across real deployment locations — operational data, not usability testing. This makes them directly usable as sales evidence in procurement conversations with industrial, scientific, and safety-critical buyers — exactly the sophisticated audiences for whom assertion-based marketing is least effective. The combination of multiple metrics across a single engagement is unusual: it documents not just that performance improved but the scope of improvement across speed, accuracy, error recovery, and team utilisation simultaneously.
- Evidence from case studies — Tetra / Prism (mobile adoption in a professional field operations context): mobile app adoption among the user population that should have been using the app rose from 12% to 64% over the year following the redesign launch. This is a measured adoption figure, not a satisfaction score — it reflects a change in actual usage behaviour across a defined user population. The baseline problem was a combination of load time (the app downloaded the full property portfolio on launch, taking up to 10 minutes) and entity model confusion (tasks, actions, forms and their statuses were inconsistent and unclear). The redesign addressed both: a property selection flow at launch reduced the download to the necessary subset for the day's work; the entity model was rationalised by removing intermediate statuses that generated confusion rather than clarity. The adoption increase is the aggregate effect of those changes on a population of field-based property managers who had previously defaulted to the desktop. Evidence calibration: client-measured adoption figure; before and after measured by Tetra. The web platform NPS improved from 72% to 85%, measured by Tetra approximately 4 months after the new web design launched. Evidence calibration: client-measured; not independently verified.
- Evidence from case studies — Enhesa legal compliance platform (NPS with confirmed clean attribution window): NPS moved from 68% to 84% in the two months following the redesigned platform launch. Enhesa confirmed that no other changes were made to the product between the 68% baseline measurement (taken two months before the engagement began) and the 84% post-launch measurement. The clean attribution window — no concurrent changes — is what distinguishes this entry from a typical client-measured NPS improvement: the movement is attributable to the design change specifically. A two-year figure of 87% was also measured, but subsequent product changes were made after the 84% measurement; the two-year figure does not carry clean attribution and should not be attributed to the design work in isolation. The platform serves regulatory affairs managers, compliance officers, and legal teams navigating dense legislative content across jurisdictions. NPS in this context reflects platform usefulness and usability under professional working conditions. Evidence calibration: client-measured by Enhesa across the full user base. Clean attribution condition confirmed by Enhesa. The 84% two-month figure is the verifiable performance claim; the 87% two-year figure is directional.

### /design-as-investment-evidence

- The outcome: design quality becomes a material factor in securing investment or development funding, by demonstrating product thinking, structural coherence, and market readiness to investors who cannot otherwise evaluate an early-stage product's potential.
- The mechanism: early-stage products — those in beta, pre-revenue, or pre-launch — face a specific challenge with investors. A specification sheet or feature list communicates intent but not execution quality. A working prototype communicates that something was built but not whether it was built well. A well-designed interface communicates something harder to fake: that the team understands the user, has resolved the hard structural problems, and has made considered decisions about what the product should be. This is legible to investors without requiring domain expertise, because the qualities that make an interface communicative to users — clarity, hierarchy, coherent information structure, considered workflow — are the same qualities that make an interface communicative in a pitch context. Investors reading an interface can tell whether the hard problems have been thought through.
- Why this is distinct from /positioning-through-interface-quality: that page concerns interface quality operating as a market signal within a competitive landscape — potential customers or professional communities forming perceptions of a product relative to its competitors. This page concerns interface quality operating as an investment signal before a competitive landscape has been entered — investors forming a view of whether a product is worth funding based on what the design communicates about the team's judgment and the product's potential. The audience, mechanism, and timing are all different.
- The causal chain: Creative Navy's Critical Systems Design method produces design that reflects deep understanding of the problem and careful resolution of structural tensions. This quality is what makes a pitch interface credible rather than superficial — it is grounded in domain learning and real product decisions, not a polished veneer. The connection between design quality and investment outcome flows through the credibility of what the design communicates, not through the design's visual appeal alone.
- Important framing constraint: the claim here is not that Creative Navy produces fundraising outcomes. The claim is that design of sufficient quality, grounded in the method, communicates product readiness in ways that become relevant in investment contexts. The investment decision involves many factors outside design's control.
- Evidence from case studies — Veecle embedded systems development studio: the Veecle engagement produced designs across onboarding, telemetry, workspace creation, AI integration, and a UI starter kit — all for a product at beta stage with no revenue. The designs were used in investor demonstrations in which the interface comprised approximately 70% of the pitch. Veecle reported to Creative Navy that the engagement was instrumental in unlocking £2M in development funding. Evidence calibration: client-reported to Creative Navy. Causal framing stated by the client (70% of pitch content was the interface). No independent verification of the funding figure or the attribution.
- Evidence from case studies — Owkin / K (interface as answer to a specific investor due diligence question, biomedical AI platform): Owkin was preparing an investment pitch for K, its AI copilot for biomedical research built on biology-specific datasets and reasoning models. The central question investors were being asked to evaluate was not whether the backend technology was strong — it was whether Owkin had a paradigm for making that backend accessible to users. Without a credible answer to that question, the investment case could not close. Creative Navy produced a prototype that served as the lead artefact of the pitch: the first thing investors saw, and the direct demonstration that a viable user paradigm existed. Owkin attributed £5M in investment to the design work (client-reported; figure approximate; causal attribution made by client directly). Evidence calibration: client-reported to Creative Navy. Causal framing stated by the client. No independent verification of the funding figure.
- Note on how the Owkin mechanism differs from Veecle: in the Veecle case, the interface comprised a large share of pitch content — a quantitative indicator of its centrality. ### /scaling-without-training-dependency

- The outcome: removing the training constraint through interface design enables deployment in geographies, organisations, and user types that were previously unreachable.
- The mechanism: when operating a system requires specialist knowledge, deployment requires either training programmes or specialist staff — both of which create logistical and cost constraints that limit geographic and demographic reach. When the interface encodes the guidance, orientation, and logic the user needs to operate it, the training constraint is removed.
- The two forms this takes:
  Geographic expansion: a product that required local training support can now be deployed without it, making international markets accessible that were previously impractical.
  New user type deployment: a product built for one user type can now serve an adjacent type — a professional tool becomes accessible to a semi-professional user, or an advanced configuration system becomes operable by a standard technician.
- Distinguish from /lower-training-burden: that page concerns reducing training cost and time for users who can already access the system. This page concerns removing the barrier that prevents reaching new users at all.
- Distinguish from /capability-democratisation: that page concerns redesigning an expert-user system for the beneficiaries of that expertise within an existing organisational context. This page concerns geographic and demographic reach.
- Evidence from case studies — World Customs Organization / IPM (primary example: global deployment across 107 administrations without uniform training infrastructure): IPM serves customs officers across member administrations in vastly different operational contexts — ports, airports, and land border posts with inconsistent connectivity, mixed device fleets, varied technical literacy, and different administrative cultures and languages. The previous interface required significant formal training to operate; training effort and support requests were high, and the burden was compounded by multilingual requirements across 107 member administrations. After the redesign, WCO reported a 78% reduction in training costs (client-reported). Documentation and training materials were structured for global distribution and translation, so member administrations could adopt at their own pace without requiring uniform training delivery. The 200% increase in rights holder sign-ups and the deployment of the system in field operations involving 2000+ officers are the demand-side confirmation: the platform reached a user population it had not previously been accessible to. This is the clearest example in the case study set of what the scaling-without-training-dependency outcome looks like at institutional scale — not a consumer product expanding geographically (Elsner) or an enterprise product reaching a new buyer size (Triopsis), but an intergovernmental platform removing the training barrier across sovereign administrations operating under different conditions simultaneously. Evidence calibration: all figures client-reported by WCO to Creative Navy.

### /positioning-through-interface-quality

- The outcome: in markets where technical specifications between competing products have converged, interface quality becomes the visible differentiator.
- The mechanism: a prospect evaluating competing products in a complex vertical can often read a specification sheet but cannot immediately verify the claims it makes. The interface, however, is immediately observable. A system that communicates intent clearly, behaves consistently, and produces the perception of being the serious product in the category creates a market signal that propagates through professional communities in ways that conventional marketing cannot manufacture.
- Why this is more durable than branding: a logo, slogan, or philosophy statement can be reproduced by any competitor. An interface that genuinely performs differently cannot be reproduced quickly. The evidence is in the product; it survives contact with the prospect's own evaluation.
- Evidence from case studies — Triopsis: the interface became a visible advantage in sales demos. Prospective customers commented on its clarity unprompted. The company began winning clients 4–5× larger than before and improved tender scores by 10–20% on design criteria. The interface shifted from a liability (sales calls turning into UI explanations) to an asset (demos that demonstrated competitive differentiation without requiring explanation).
- Evidence from case studies — Torqeedo: the interface strengthened Torqeedo's competitive position in the professional maritime market (CEO-reported). The company was subsequently acquired by Yamaha Motor Co. The causal link between the interface and the acquisition is inferred from timing and competitive positioning, not independently verified — frame accordingly. The Torqeedo case illustrates positioning through interface quality in a regulated, safety-adjacent professional domain where the perception of being the serious product carries particular weight.
- Evidence from case studies — Beissbarth automotive calibration: the interface became a selling point for the high-end calibration system. This is a different form of positioning through interface quality from the Triopsis and Torqeedo examples — not differentiation in a competitive sales process but a change in how the product is positioned commercially by its manufacturer. Beissbarth moved from a product where the interface required explanation to one where the interface was foregrounded as evidence of product quality. The mechanism was the same: an interface that communicates intent clearly and behaves consistently under real conditions produces the perception of being the serious product in the category. In a market where calibration equipment carries performance expectations from manufacturers such as Mercedes, Daimler, and BMW, that perception has direct commercial value.
- Evidence from case studies — deSoutter Medical / Zethon: in a market where powered surgical instruments are evaluated daily by experienced orthopaedic and trauma surgeons, the manufacturer's commercial teams had not been able to present the device without the GUI requiring explanation or excuse. The legacy interface — built following the internal software architecture — did not reflect the performance level of the hardware. After the redesign, commercial teams reported that they could present the device without the interface becoming a liability in the sales conversation. The GUI became evidence of the product's performance level rather than something that required apologising for. A named surgeon participant — Tom Frilling MSc MBBS FRCS(Tr&Orth), Hip & Knee Trauma and Orthopaedic Surgeon — noted that the interface behaved as expected for a modern surgical tool. This is positioning through interface quality in a regulated medical hardware market: not differentiation through feature comparison, but the removal of an active sales liability and its replacement with a signal that the manufacturer understands clinical use. Both outcomes were client-reported.
- Evidence from case studies — COX Marine cluster displays: COX entered a market where established marine electronics brands — Garmin, Simrad, and others — held distributor relationships and operator trust built over years of deployment. The competitive question was not whether the interface could match those brands on specification, but whether it would be perceived by distributors and professional operators as instrumentation of equivalent standing. Following deployment, COX distributors reported to the company that the cluster display interface is the best in the industry. The system shipped, was deployed on vessels, and achieved informal standard status within its product category — distributor-reported to client, relayed to Creative Navy. This is a structurally distinct propagation mechanism from the other cases in this set: not word-of-mouth from end users (Triopsis), not a CEO statement (Torqeedo), not commercial team relief from no longer apologising for the interface (Beissbarth, deSoutter), but an independent consensus formed within the professional distributor network — a channel with daily comparative exposure to every major competing display brand and direct commercial incentive to evaluate honestly. The distributor channel occupies a specific epistemic position: it is simultaneously closer to the market than end users (because distributors evaluate many products against each other) and more commercially grounded than internal teams (because distributors do not benefit from flattering their suppliers). A consensus forming there carries a particular weight. Evidence calibration: distributor-reported to client, relayed to Creative Navy. Not independently verified.
