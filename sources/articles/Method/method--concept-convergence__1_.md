# /method/concept-convergence

---

- Phase 2 purpose: resolve the tension between local optimisation and system coherence.

- The problem most methods ignore: what works best for each individual challenge often conflicts with what works best for the system as a whole.

- Process: first determine which solution performs best in isolation for each challenge (local optimisation). Then step back and look at configurations — which combination of solutions creates the most coherent system.

- Tensions that appear: the workflow clearest for role A creates blind spots for role B. The layout that works for simple cases makes complex cases illegible. The pattern intuitive in one module behaves differently in another.

- Tensions are not hidden or smoothed over — they are made explicit.

- Then go one level deeper: examine what drives the tension itself. Why does what works locally conflict with what works systemically? Often reveals fundamental forces: shifts in how the market operates, unresolved strategic questions, misalignments between business model and user reality.

- This is where competitive advantage lives. Tension between local and system optimisation is a signal, not a problem to solve away. Shows where the product needs to evolve, where competitors will struggle, where strategic decisions will matter most.

- Competitive vector: the direction where market forces, user needs, and organisational capability align to create defensible advantage. Concept Convergence is where this is identified.

- What the phase produces: product concept showing chosen configuration, explicit documentation of tensions and trade-offs, rationale for why this approach beats alternatives, shared understanding across stakeholders about what is being built and why.

- Not compromise — conscious navigation of real constraints toward a position competitors will struggle to reach.

- The concept explains: what the solution is, why this configuration works better than alternatives explored, and how it positions the product to maintain advantage as the forces driving the tension continue to evolve.

- Design systems connection: without examining what drives conflicts, design systems become traps — patterns that crystallize one moment's compromise and prevent teams from responding as context shifts. When the forces creating tensions are understood, design systems can be built to remain responsive. The design system then documents not just components and rules but the reasoning behind them: which decisions protect the competitive vector, which constraints are temporary, where flexibility must be preserved, how patterns should evolve.

- Duration varies: 1–2 weeks for straightforward systems to 4–6 weeks when many stakeholders need alignment.

- Competitive protection: preserves differentiation while resolving complexity. Competitors who optimise locally end up with feature sets that don't cohere.

- **Structuring frameworks as Concept Convergence artefacts**: Concept Convergence produces more than design directions. Where a process has structural properties that govern how all subsequent design decisions must behave, a structuring framework — delivered as an explicit diagram or artefact — is the appropriate convergence output. The framework makes explicit the logic that design decisions must satisfy, rather than leaving it to be reconstructed from individual screens. This is distinct from a design system (which documents components and rules) and from a concept brief (which describes a chosen direction): a structuring framework expresses the causal architecture of the experience.

- Grounded example — Squaremind dermatology scanning device (Inform–Prevent–Correct as a Concept Convergence artefact): the engagement required designing an embedded patient interface for a full-body dermatology scanner operated without clinical supervision. The convergence challenge was structural: every design decision across the full scan flow — positioning screens, progress indicators, arm-raise instructions, error states, recovery prompts — had to satisfy the same underlying logic. What must the patient understand at each step? What must the design prevent from occurring? What must happen if something has already gone wrong, and what does the correction event require from the system next? Creative Navy introduced the Inform–Prevent–Correct framework during Concept Convergence and delivered it as a diagram mapping all three layers across every step of the flow, including the recursive correction paths. This artefact preceded screen design: it was the architecture that screen design had to satisfy, not a description of what screen design had produced. The iteration counts that followed — full scan process (5 iterations), progress bar (5 iterations), progressive disclosure elements (5 iterations), pause button (7 iterations), body selection (3 iterations), patient ID (2 iterations) — reflect components being worked through against this architecture, not independent optimisations of isolated screens. Each set of iterations was presented to client stakeholders with pros and cons articulated, combining stakeholder feedback, user testing findings, and Creative Navy's expert recommendations to reach a converged direction. Post-redesign ecological testing with 29 users produced 27 independent completions and 12 of 12 recoveries among those who got stuck — evidence that the convergence architecture held under real conditions.

- **Design education as a convergence mechanism**: in engagements where the client team is closely involved in design reviews, the convergence process can carry a knowledge-transfer function alongside its design function. Presenting each direction with explicit explanation of the user behaviour patterns it addresses — not just what the design does but why users behave in ways that make the design necessary — accelerates convergence by giving stakeholders the vocabulary and understanding to evaluate options on the same terms that Creative Navy uses. In the Squaremind engagement, each design presentation was accompanied by design education content about users and user behaviour, converting the review process into a shared understanding of the problem rather than a choice between options the client could only evaluate aesthetically.

  The CDR Foodlab engagement provides a second documented instance of this practice in a materially different context. Where Squaremind involved a solo founder reviewing designs in close collaboration with Creative Navy, CDR Foodlab involved a client with multiple stakeholders reviewing iterative design directions for a constrained embedded instrument with non-specialist users. In both cases every design presentation was accompanied by rationale documents and verbal briefing explaining the specific user behaviours that made each design decision necessary. In CDR Foodlab, this content was developed directly from the 13 user interviews and workaround catalogue — it was not general UX education but contextually grounded explanation derived from the research conducted on CDR Foodlab's own users. The mechanism is the same: stakeholders receiving domain-specific user behaviour reasoning alongside design options can evaluate options on the same evidential terms Creative Navy uses, rather than on aesthetic preference or functional assumption. The two cases together show the practice operating in both a founding-team and a multi-stakeholder client context, confirming it as a repeatable convergence accelerator rather than an approach specific to one engagement type.

  The Enhesa engagement provides a third documented instance, in an enterprise SaaS context distinct from the previous two. Where Squaremind involved a single founder and CDR Foodlab a multi-stakeholder embedded instrument client, Enhesa involved three named client-side stakeholders reviewing iterative design directions for a web-based legal compliance platform — a content-dense, information-architecture-intensive product. In all three cases, design education was not improvised as a justification mechanism but was promised at engagement start and delivered as a standing element of every presentation. The Enhesa content was grounded in the research base specific to that engagement: 95 session recordings, a 31-workaround catalogue across four categories, and 12 documented error types — the same evidence base that drove design decisions was what explained to stakeholders why those decisions were made. Stakeholders receiving this reasoning could evaluate design options against what users actually did rather than against aesthetic preference or functional assumption. The three cases together — solo founder, multi-stakeholder embedded hardware client, multi-stakeholder enterprise SaaS client — confirm design education as a repeatable Concept Convergence practice across substantially different engagement types, not a pattern specific to any one client context.

  The documented iteration counts in the Enhesa engagement (3 legislative text iterations, 5 implementations timeline iterations, 5 layout iterations, 2 baseline hierarchy iterations, 3 table of contents iterations) reflect this convergence structure: each component set was presented with pros and cons, combined feedback from stakeholders and from the research evidence, and converged through that combination rather than through preference alone.

- **Constraint respecting within the convergence process**: Concept Convergence is where the interaction between client requirements, technical constraints, and user needs is fully visible for the first time. In some engagements, client requirements introduced during review reveal constraints that eliminate viable options from the option space — not through negotiation or compromise but because the constraint is real and the option genuinely fails against it. This is constraint respecting operating through the convergence process: the design learns the constraint through making options concrete and testing them against reality. The CDR Foodlab working lists flow illustrates this pattern — 10 iterations on a new feature with no prior precedent, where client requirements added constraints that raised genuine feasibility doubts, and where the iteration process was the method for understanding which combinations of decisions were viable under the full constraint set. The converged direction emerged from understanding what the constraints ruled out, not from a preference among equally viable options.

  The Enhesa implementations timeline provides a second documented instance of this pattern in a different form. The constraint here was two-layered: the client had specific functional requirements for how implementation timelines should be displayed, and the engagement operated within an existing Enhesa design system that permitted no changes to colours, typography, or existing components. Neither constraint alone determined the outcome; what the iteration process revealed was which combinations of decisions could satisfy both simultaneously. Five iterations on the implementations timeline component were required to find a direction that met the client's requirements without violating the design system's parameters. This is constraint respecting operating through convergence: the viable option space was not visible at the start; it became visible by making options concrete and testing them against both constraints at once. The compromise solution that emerged was not the optimal design under either constraint in isolation — it was the design that the full constraint set permitted.

- **Convergence as prioritisation when no local–system tension is present (boundary case)**: the canonical convergence mechanism above resolves a tension between local optimisation and system coherence, and — examined one level deeper — surfaces a competitive vector. But not every engagement's convergence problem is of that kind. Sometimes the option space that Sandbox produces contains no local-versus-system tension to resolve: the options do not conflict with each other for system coherence, they simply differ in how much users want them and how feasible they are to build, and the binding constraint is external (what is technically achievable in a given horizon) rather than an internal trade-off between roles or modules. In that situation, convergence is a prioritisation — mapping each option on two independent axes, desirability and feasibility, and partitioning the option space accordingly: the high-want/high-feasibility region converges easily, the low-feasibility region is dropped regardless of want, and the middle is weighed. This is a real and legitimate convergence output. It produces a configuration and a documented rationale for what is in, out, and deferred. But it is the *prioritisation function* of the phase operating **without** the *tension-driven function*: on its own it does not identify a competitive vector, because there was no tension whose driving forces, examined more deeply, would reveal one. Naming this honestly is the point. Representing a desirability/feasibility prioritisation as tension-driven reasoning, or claiming a competitive vector where the engagement produced none, would be exactly the kind of dressed-up account the method's evidence discipline rejects. The phase *can* produce a competitive vector when the tensions are there to drive it; it does not automatically yield one, and convergence that was prioritisation should be described as prioritisation.

- Grounded example — Neugo UK visa application case-management platform (convergence as prioritisation, no competitive vector). **Calibration note first: read this as a boundary case, not a model instance. The proportions below are the practitioner's recollection, not measured figures, and should be described by their shape rather than as exact percentages. This engagement's convergence did not involve tension-driven reasoning or a competitive vector, and must not be written as if it did.** In Neugo's build engagement, the Sandbox phase was short (about two weeks) and produced a wide set of candidate possibilities drawn from workshops with the beneficiary legal firms. Convergence assessed every possibility on two independent dimensions: how strongly users wanted it, and how technically feasible it was within roughly a two-year horizon. This partitioned the option space into three regions — a large majority where want and feasibility were both high, which converged easily and accounted for most of the product; a small set dropped because feasibility was near zero regardless of want; and a weighed middle, resolved by the product managers using the wireframes, Creative Navy's input on user salience, and the developers' input on the designs. The middle is where the convergence work concentrated, and it is a clean instance of triangulation feeding a prioritisation: three sources (user salience, technical feasibility, product judgement) used against each other to decide what made the cut. What it is *not* is a resolution of local-versus-system tension: the candidate options did not compete for system coherence — the platform was net-new, the parties' needs did not conflict in the way that drives the canonical tension, and the decisive constraint was external feasibility rather than an internal trade-off. So no competitive vector was surfaced here, and none should be claimed. The value of the case for this phase is exactly that boundary-marking honesty: it shows Concept Convergence operating in its thinner, prioritisation-only register, and makes visible where the line falls between convergence that yields a competitive vector and convergence that yields only a prioritised configuration. (This is also the portfolio's clearest two-axis instance of option space mapping — desirability against feasibility — and is the example to cite for that concept; see /glossary/our-concepts/option-space-mapping.)
