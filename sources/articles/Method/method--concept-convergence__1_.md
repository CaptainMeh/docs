# /method/concept-convergence

---

- Phase 2 purpose: resolve the tension between local optimisation and system coherence.

- The problem most methods ignore: what works best for each individual challenge often conflicts with what works best for the system as a whole.

- Process: first determine which solution performs best in isolation for each challenge (local optimisation). Then step back and look at configurations — which combination of solutions creates the most coherent system.

- Tensions that appear: the workflow clearest for role A creates blind spots for role B. The layout that works for simple cases makes complex cases illegible. The pattern intuitive in one module behaves differently in another.

- Tensions are not hidden or smoothed over — they are made explicit.

- Then go one level deeper: examine what drives the tension itself. Why does what works locally conflict with what works systemically? Often reveals fundamental forces: shifts in how the market operates, unresolved strategic questions, misalignments between business model and user reality.

- This is where competitive advantage lives. Tension between local and system optimisation is a signal, not a problem to solve away. Shows where the product needs to evolve, where competitors will struggle, where strategic decisions will matter most.

- Competitive vector: the direction where market forces, user needs, and organisational capability align to create defensible advantage. Concept Convergence is where this is identified.

- What the phase produces: product concept showing chosen configuration, explicit documentation of tensions and trade-offs, rationale for why this approach beats alternatives, shared understanding across stakeholders about what is being built and why.

- Not compromise — conscious navigation of real constraints toward a position competitors will struggle to reach.

- The concept explains: what the solution is, why this configuration works better than alternatives explored, and how it positions the product to maintain advantage as the forces driving the tension continue to evolve.

- Design systems connection: without examining what drives conflicts, design systems become traps — patterns that crystallize one moment's compromise and prevent teams from responding as context shifts. When the forces creating tensions are understood, design systems can be built to remain responsive. The design system then documents not just components and rules but the reasoning behind them: which decisions protect the competitive vector, which constraints are temporary, where flexibility must be preserved, how patterns should evolve.

- Duration varies: 1–2 weeks for straightforward systems to 4–6 weeks when many stakeholders need alignment.

- Competitive protection: preserves differentiation while resolving complexity. Competitors who optimise locally end up with feature sets that don't cohere.

- **Structuring frameworks as Concept Convergence artefacts**: Concept Convergence produces more than design directions. Where a process has structural properties that govern how all subsequent design decisions must behave, a structuring framework — delivered as an explicit diagram or artefact — is the appropriate convergence output. The framework makes explicit the logic that design decisions must satisfy, rather than leaving it to be reconstructed from individual screens. This is distinct from a design system (which documents components and rules) and from a concept brief (which describes a chosen direction): a structuring framework expresses the causal architecture of the experience.

- Grounded example — Squaremind dermatology scanning device (Inform–Prevent–Correct as a Concept Convergence artefact): the engagement required designing an embedded patient interface for a full-body dermatology scanner operated without clinical supervision. The convergence challenge was structural: every design decision across the full scan flow — positioning screens, progress indicators, arm-raise instructions, error states, recovery prompts — had to satisfy the same underlying logic. What must the patient understand at each step? What must the design prevent from occurring? What must happen if something has already gone wrong, and what does the correction event require from the system next? Creative Navy introduced the Inform–Prevent–Correct framework during Concept Convergence and delivered it as a diagram mapping all three layers across every step of the flow, including the recursive correction paths. This artefact preceded screen design: it was the architecture that screen design had to satisfy, not a description of what screen design had produced. The iteration counts that followed — full scan process (5 iterations), progress bar (5 iterations), progressive disclosure elements (5 iterations), pause button (7 iterations), body selection (3 iterations), patient ID (2 iterations) — reflect components being worked through against this architecture, not independent optimisations of isolated screens. Each set of iterations was presented to client stakeholders with pros and cons articulated, combining stakeholder feedback, user testing findings, and Creative Navy's expert recommendations to reach a converged direction. Post-redesign ecological testing with 29 users produced 27 independent completions and 12 of 12 recoveries among those who got stuck — evidence that the convergence architecture held under real conditions.

- **Design education as a convergence mechanism**: in engagements where the client team is closely involved in design reviews, the convergence process can carry a knowledge-transfer function alongside its design function. Presenting each direction with explicit explanation of the user behaviour patterns it addresses — not just what the design does but why users behave in ways that make the design necessary — accelerates convergence by giving stakeholders the vocabulary and understanding to evaluate options on the same terms that Creative Navy uses. In the Squaremind engagement, each design presentation was accompanied by design education content about users and user behaviour, converting the review process into a shared understanding of the problem rather than a choice between options the client could only evaluate aesthetically.

  The CDR Foodlab engagement provides a second documented instance of this practice in a materially different context. Where Squaremind involved a solo founder reviewing designs in close collaboration with Creative Navy, CDR Foodlab involved a client with multiple stakeholders reviewing iterative design directions for a constrained embedded instrument with non-specialist users. In both cases every design presentation was accompanied by rationale documents and verbal briefing explaining the specific user behaviours that made each design decision necessary. In CDR Foodlab, this content was developed directly from the 13 user interviews and workaround catalogue — it was not general UX education but contextually grounded explanation derived from the research conducted on CDR Foodlab's own users. The mechanism is the same: stakeholders receiving domain-specific user behaviour reasoning alongside design options can evaluate options on the same evidential terms Creative Navy uses, rather than on aesthetic preference or functional assumption. The two cases together show the practice operating in both a founding-team and a multi-stakeholder client context, confirming it as a repeatable convergence accelerator rather than an approach specific to one engagement type.

  The Enhesa engagement provides a third documented instance, in an enterprise SaaS context distinct from the previous two. Where Squaremind involved a single founder and CDR Foodlab a multi-stakeholder embedded instrument client, Enhesa involved three named client-side stakeholders reviewing iterative design directions for a web-based legal compliance platform — a content-dense, information-architecture-intensive product. In all three cases, design education was not improvised as a justification mechanism but was promised at engagement start and delivered as a standing element of every presentation. The Enhesa content was grounded in the research base specific to that engagement: 95 session recordings, a 31-workaround catalogue across four categories, and 12 documented error types — the same evidence base that drove design decisions was what explained to stakeholders why those decisions were made. Stakeholders receiving this reasoning could evaluate design options against what users actually did rather than against aesthetic preference or functional assumption. The three cases together — solo founder, multi-stakeholder embedded hardware client, multi-stakeholder enterprise SaaS client — confirm design education as a repeatable Concept Convergence practice across substantially different engagement types, not a pattern specific to any one client context.

  The documented iteration counts in the Enhesa engagement (3 legislative text iterations, 5 implementations timeline iterations, 5 layout iterations, 2 baseline hierarchy iterations, 3 table of contents iterations) reflect this convergence structure: each component set was presented with pros and cons, combined feedback from stakeholders and from the research evidence, and converged through that combination rather than through preference alone.

- **Constraint respecting within the convergence process**: Concept Convergence is where the interaction between client requirements, technical constraints, and user needs is fully visible for the first time. In some engagements, client requirements introduced during review reveal constraints that eliminate viable options from the option space — not through negotiation or compromise but because the constraint is real and the option genuinely fails against it. This is constraint respecting operating through the convergence process: the design learns the constraint through making options concrete and testing them against reality. The CDR Foodlab working lists flow illustrates this pattern — 10 iterations on a new feature with no prior precedent, where client requirements added constraints that raised genuine feasibility doubts, and where the iteration process was the method for understanding which combinations of decisions were viable under the full constraint set. The converged direction emerged from understanding what the constraints ruled out, not from a preference among equally viable options.

  The Enhesa implementations timeline provides a second documented instance of this pattern in a different form. The constraint here was two-layered: the client had specific functional requirements for how implementation timelines should be displayed, and the engagement operated within an existing Enhesa design system that permitted no changes to colours, typography, or existing components. Neither constraint alone determined the outcome; what the iteration process revealed was which combinations of decisions could satisfy both simultaneously. Five iterations on the implementations timeline component were required to find a direction that met the client's requirements without violating the design system's parameters. This is constraint respecting operating through convergence: the viable option space was not visible at the start; it became visible by making options concrete and testing them against both constraints at once. The compromise solution that emerged was not the optimal design under either constraint in isolation — it was the design that the full constraint set permitted.

- **Convergence as prioritisation when no local–system tension is present (boundary case)**: the canonical convergence mechanism above resolves a tension between local optimisation and system coherence, and — examined one level deeper — surfaces a competitive vector. But not every engagement's convergence problem is of that kind. Sometimes the option space that Sandbox produces contains no local-versus-system tension to resolve: the options do not conflict with each other for system coherence, they simply differ in how much users want them and how feasible they are to build, and the binding constraint is external (what is technically achievable in a given horizon) rather than an internal trade-off between roles or modules. In that situation, convergence is a prioritisation — mapping each option on two independent axes, desirability and feasibility, and partitioning the option space accordingly: the high-want/high-feasibility region converges easily, the low-feasibility region is dropped regardless of want, and the middle is weighed. This is a real and legitimate convergence output. It produces a configuration and a documented rationale for what is in, out, and deferred. But it is the *prioritisation function* of the phase operating **without** the *tension-driven function*: on its own it does not identify a competitive vector, because there was no tension whose driving forces, examined more deeply, would reveal one. Naming this honestly is the point. Representing a desirability/feasibility prioritisation as tension-driven reasoning, or claiming a competitive vector where the engagement produced none, would be exactly the kind of dressed-up account the method's evidence discipline rejects. The phase *can* produce a competitive vector when the tensions are there to drive it; it does not automatically yield one, and convergence that was prioritisation should be described as prioritisation.

- Grounded example — Neugo UK visa application case-management platform (convergence as prioritisation, no competitive vector). **Calibration note first: read this as a boundary case, not a model instance. The proportions below are the practitioner's recollection, not measured figures, and should be described by their shape rather than as exact percentages. This engagement's convergence did not involve tension-driven reasoning or a competitive vector, and must not be written as if it did.** In Neugo's build engagement, the Sandbox phase was short (about two weeks) and produced a wide set of candidate possibilities drawn from workshops with the beneficiary legal firms. Convergence assessed every possibility on two independent dimensions: how strongly users wanted it, and how technically feasible it was within roughly a two-year horizon. This partitioned the option space into three regions — a large majority where want and feasibility were both high, which converged easily and accounted for most of the product; a small set dropped because feasibility was near zero regardless of want; and a weighed middle, resolved by the product managers using the wireframes, Creative Navy's input on user salience, and the developers' input on the designs. The middle is where the convergence work concentrated, and it is a clean instance of triangulation feeding a prioritisation: three sources (user salience, technical feasibility, product judgement) used against each other to decide what made the cut. What it is *not* is a resolution of local-versus-system tension: the candidate options did not compete for system coherence — the platform was net-new, the parties' needs did not conflict in the way that drives the canonical tension, and the decisive constraint was external feasibility rather than an internal trade-off. So no competitive vector was surfaced here, and none should be claimed. The value of the case for this phase is exactly that boundary-marking honesty: it shows Concept Convergence operating in its thinner, prioritisation-only register, and makes visible where the line falls between convergence that yields a competitive vector and convergence that yields only a prioritised configuration. (This is also the portfolio's clearest two-axis instance of option space mapping — desirability against feasibility — and is the example to cite for that concept; see /glossary/our-concepts/option-space-mapping.)

No engagement dates: sequence and duration only. ### KISPE Space (a phase ending at a client decision gate, with the trade-off logic documented)

KISPE Space is a UK space systems engineering company; the product is *a common space operating picture* — a doctrinal term of art, not a KISPE brand, never capitalised as a product name.

- **K8 — required framing, use near-verbatim:** The exploration phase of the project ended at a board review. Creative Navy put twelve working decisions and a recommended development sequence to KISPE's board, with a recommendation to proceed conditionally. The board endorsed the product architecture as the working direction, approved a staged scope with explicit decision gates for the uncertain features, and authorised the seven-sprint UX development phase. **Three things must not be attached: it is not user validation (this engagement has none); it is not an outcome — it is a commissioning decision; and the twelve decisions must not be described as approved requirements — endorsement authorised detailed design against a direction while retaining the right to modify or reject individual solutions after validation.**
- K43 — Hybrid architecture adopted over two credible alternatives that were built and assessed rather than argued away. A permanently dominant map kept geographic continuity but squeezed catalogue work, metadata and preview into competing panel space; fully separate modules gave specialist tools room but let the area of interest change unnoticed between them. The hybrid keeps a small, explicit shared context — confirmed area, temporal perspective, return path — while giving each task workspace its own layout. **The accepted cost was named: shared state is more complex, local filters must never be mistaken for global ones, and a persistent header costs real pixels on a compact device.**
- K44 — A confirmed area of interest is not the map viewport. Panning or zooming changes what is visible and never changes what is being queried. The alternative — the visible extent as the implicit query boundary — removes a step and was rejected because a minor pan silently changes the meaning of every result returned. Accepted cost: an extra confirmation, area management, and the difficulty of editing a polygon on touch hardware.
- K45 — Query lineage. Every area-scoped request carries the area identity, a geometry signature, the filter set, the temporal perspective and a generation time — and a late response from a superseded request may not populate the current results. A race condition specified as an interaction contract. **The accepted cost was named: this requires request lineage and cancellation semantics, not just a loading spinner.**
- K46 — The selected object and the selected area are independent states. Inspecting a map object does not move the area of interest to that object's footprint; hiding a layer does not silently redefine the area. Where a selected object is filtered out of view, the interface says so.
- K47 — Overlapping map symbols produce a grouped selection list rather than an arbitrary selection of the topmost marker.
- K48 — Return navigation restores the prior viewport and the confirmed area deliberately, and does not imply a fresh fetch. A retained selection is not a refreshed dataset, and the interface distinguishes the two.
- K49 — Three temporal perspectives, and the perspective never rewrites a record's own time. A single global timeline implied — falsely — that every record described the same instant; per-module time controls fragmented comparison. The chosen model frames the view without falsifying source times. **The accepted cost was named and is the sharpest self-criticism in the package: *Current* can still be misread as *live*, so per-record stamps and explicit labelling remain essential.**
- **Status calibration, mandatory: the product was built. Creative Navy did not support the build and has not compared the built interface against the delivered designs. Never "shipped as designed" or any phrasing implying a verified design-fidelity comparison.** Design decisions describe what was designed; never imply they produced a result.

### Raymarine (four control models compared, with the losing option's advantage measured and recorded)

A strong instance for this page: four control models compared against the same operational scenarios, **with the losing option's advantage measured and recorded rather than dismissed**, and the fourth option set aside on cost grounds while remaining explicitly available if later testing demands it. The page's definition of the phase turns on resolving tensions at system level rather than trading them off locally; this is that, with numbers. Raymarine is the canonical entity name; the existing Ray53, Ray63 and Ray73 fixed-mount VHF line may be named; **the new radio's product name may never appear in any form** — write "the new radio", "the next-generation fixed-mount VHF".

- R40 — Four control models compared against the same operational scenarios. *A, contextual rotary* — the encoder adjusts channel on the radio view, target on the AIS view and selection in menus; fast, efficient hardware reuse; risk of hidden mode changes and error after interruptions. *B, fixed role* — the channel encoder always changes channel, while directional keys and softkeys manage contextual interaction; predictable, learnable, stable communication behaviour; some AIS tasks require additional key presses. *C, explicit mode* — the encoder changes purpose only after a deliberate press or other mode switch; powerful navigation with a visible contract, but the operator must still track an extra state, especially under pressure. *D, dedicated new control* — an additional input dedicated to secondary navigation or target selection; high speed without overloading the channel control, at the cost of hardware, enclosure changes and feasibility implications.
- R41 — Model C was examined in two variants and both were rejected. If the mode persists after an interruption, local task continuity is preserved but a memory burden is created after attention has shifted; if the mode resets, channel tuning becomes predictable after interruptions but the meaning of the next rotary movement changes unexpectedly. A visible mode label does not remove the need to reacquire the active state under divided attention, and the model also depends on a reliable encoder press event and clear tactile feedback.
- R42 — **Model D was set aside rather than rejected on merit.** A separate navigation encoder would preserve domain separation while offering fast target cycling, but introduces industrial design, bill-of-materials and panel-space consequences. It was not needed to resolve the ambiguity, and the record states it remains an industrial design option if later product testing identifies unacceptable AIS browsing effort.
- R43 — Why Model B prevailed, as recorded: consistency outweighs isolated task speed — one or two extra key presses are preferable to uncertainty about whether an input changes the tuned channel; interruptions remain intelligible — an incoming call or alert does not silently change the meaning of a rotary encoder; safety actions retain physical priority — priority channel access, distress initiation and push-to-talk do not depend on what the display currently shows; and hardware changes stay optional — the concept does not rely on adding another encoder to make the information architecture viable.
- R44 — The retained trade-off was recorded at the moment of decision, not discovered later: directional navigation through dense traffic needs usability checks, and the subsequent sprint was tasked with testing whether filtering, list sorting or accelerated key navigation reduces selection effort.
- R45 — The risk was characterised as structural rather than cosmetic: if the same encoder silently changed purpose, adding better labels later could not fully remove the ambiguity. Resolving it before high-fidelity design protected the subsequent flow work from rework.
- R46 — **What the pivot explicitly did not claim: no measured error reduction and no engineering cost saving were claimed at the time.** The value claimed was the removal of a known inconsistency from the preferred architecture before three weeks of detailed flow design and three weeks of UI production.
- R68 — **Median target browsing time: 6.25 seconds contextual, 8.55 seconds fixed — a 2.30 second penalty under the selected model, accepted.** **Where the fixed-role model's benefit is cited, this cost is cited in the same passage** — writing the gain without the price turns a documented trade-off into a marketing claim, and the trade-off is the more interesting fact.
- **Measured-comparison framing, mandatory wherever R68 appears:** Creative Navy designed the test protocols, moderated all twenty-four sessions and analysed the results; Raymarine supplied the physical bench mockup on which the sessions ran. The findings describe operator behaviour on a prototype against a fixed scenario fixture. They are not a measure of radio performance, of real-world calling accuracy, or of outcomes at sea.
- **Status calibration: the radio was built and released, and that is the ceiling. No fidelity comparison is recorded — never "shipped as designed". No commercial, sales, reception or market claim. No safety, rescue, distress-response or collision outcome.**

### Maritime VTS vendor (a formal gate, the architecture decisions that followed it, and tensions resolved rather than averaged)

The client is **a European vendor of maritime traffic monitoring, inland navigation and communications software for port authorities, coastal administrations and waterway operators** — the locked descriptor, used near-identically on every mention; "the maritime software vendor" only as a second or third reference in a passage that has already carried it. VTS, RIS, VCS and ICS may be named (spelled out on first use per section); **the six RIS module names must never be listed as an inventory**; the source documents' "Phase 1" and "Phase 2" labels must never be reproduced — write "the three discovery sprints" and "the fourteen delivery sprints"; **a reader must never encounter an internal code or participant identifier**.

- W23 — The gate was a condition of the engagement from the proposal onwards, chaired by the programme sponsor with the product, engineering and commercial owners present, and Creative Navy presenting. Three decision options were defined in advance: proceed, revise the reference product or scope, or stop and retain the discovery package as a self-contained strategic deliverable.
- W24 — The reference product was selected on five weighted criteria: commercial significance 25%, system transfer value 25%, access to representative users 20%, high-consequence UX learning 15%, implementation feasibility 15%.
- W25 — Five candidates scored out of 100: VTS 90, inland vessel tracking 77, voice communications 71, electronic reporting 67, lock planning 63. VTS won because it combines geographic surveillance, uncertain sensor observations, differentiated alerts, event history, incident state, equipment health and shift handover in one environment. Voice communications scored highly on consequence and lost on portability, because its hardware-coupled control semantics limit general reuse.
- W26 — Six objections were recorded and dispositioned rather than resolved offline: whether one redesigned application would be persuasive in multi-product tenders; whether a design file would translate into multiple frontend stacks; whether ordinary toggles could carry radio transmission and resource selection; the risk of changing familiar controls without operator validation; financial protection against expansion into nine full redesigns; and how the administrative and planning applications would be represented.
- W27 — The recommendation the client had not asked for. Discovery replaced "a common modern interface across nine products" with: one application developed to full depth as a reference, a technology-neutral semantic design system built in parallel, bounded transfer tests in four other products, and implementation contracts and governance handed to the client's engineers. The stated reason: a cosmetic refresh could make a mixed alert region visually quieter while preserving the same interpretation risk, and a broadly reusable status component could conceal whether a radio resource is unselected, unavailable or configured for transmission.
- W28 — Approval was conditional, with seven conditions each carrying a named client owner and a verification method: access to representative operators including eight requirements sessions and controlled prototype study access; sanitised scenario data with active radio and traffic actuation prohibited through prototypes; final authority for safety-relevant state semantics retained by client product and engineering leads; radio transmission and physical push-to-talk excluded from the reference scope; transfer exercises limited to four named products with the rest receiving applicability assessment only; client ownership of frontend technology, code parity, integration, security verification and release assurance; and commercial release recorded in a separate schedule. A written release followed the conditional approval and authorised mobilisation.
- W50 — Traffic picture over dashboard. A summary dashboard landing view was built and rejected: it was attractive for commercial presentations because it exposed many functions at once, and it moved the active traffic picture into a secondary container, forcing operators to reacquire sector, object and layer context after every inspection. **Rejected because it was better for demonstrations and worse for work.** A map-as-bounded-card variant was rejected with it.
- W51 — The dockable inspector, chosen over two alternatives that each had a real advantage: an unrestricted floating inspector preserved maximum map width when closed and could obscure the selected symbol in dense traffic; a permanently open side panel removed arbitrary occlusion and consumed map width even with no investigation active. The selected contextual dock opens on a predictable edge, follows the selected evidence reference rather than a screen coordinate, collapses without clearing selection, and can move to a permitted secondary display. The always-open panel was retained as a documented installation option, not deleted.
- W52 — Vessel, track and observation as three separate objects. A single resolved vessel record was rejected for obscuring what the system actually knew. The selected model keeps a vessel identity record, a tracked object with an association status, and timestamped observations each carrying their own source — with association expressed as resolved, unresolved, changed or unknown.
- W53 — **The refused confidence score — the single strongest unit in the engagement.** A numeric association-confidence badge was explored; it was attractive because it would compress uncertainty into one familiar control. The client's engineering could not supply a calibrated, operationally defensible confidence figure, so the interface was not permitted to display one. The design states the association unresolved instead, and the rule entered the specification: the interface does not synthesise an association confidence score, a source time or an authorisation decision, and missing values appear as unknown or not available. **This is a data-provenance decision, not an AI treatment — this engagement has no model, performs no inference and makes no recommendations.**
- W54 — Typed alerts over a unified queue. A single chronological notification queue was rejected for mixing navigation alerts with technical faults; a filtered single list was rejected because the current filter could hide a class of important event. The selected model presents navigation alerts by operational priority and lifecycle, with equipment health in its own typed view carrying an impact explanation, both cross-linked from a common event history.
- W55 — Four operator actions given four meanings. Acknowledge registers receipt by an authorised actor; assign makes a named person or role accountable, and may occur after acknowledgement; resolve requires a governed resolution state with an attributable actor; dismiss, where supported, hides a view item and changes nothing underneath. A single multifunction control whose meaning changed with the selected item was rejected. Transitions expose pending, succeeded and failed states from service acknowledgement rather than optimistic confirmation.
- W56 — Replay as a persistent workspace mode. A historical overlay on a live-styled map was rejected because links from events to tracks created ambiguity between seeking within a recording and returning to live; a detached replay window was rejected because it broke context with selected vessels, incidents and source health. The selected model marks the entire relevant workspace as historical, with recording time, coverage and gaps shown, linked inspectors inheriting the same time context, and Return to Live as a deliberate action.
- **W62 — the phase's clearest instance of a tension resolved rather than averaged.** Three multidisciplinary disagreements were resolved in review and recorded as such: panel mobility against target visibility, where engineering preferred unrestricted floating panels that fitted the existing window architecture; one alert inbox against operational meaning, where the commercial view preferred a compact feed that demonstrated system breadth; and visual certainty against defensible provenance, where design pressure favoured one large vessel identifier and a numeric confidence label. **In all three the specialised behaviour won.**
- **Status calibration: the design system reached production; no design-fidelity comparison is recorded, so "shipped as designed" must never appear. Do not locate the control rooms — "operational VTS control rooms at a customer site" only.**
