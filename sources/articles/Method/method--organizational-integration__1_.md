# /method/organizational-integration

---

- Phase 4 purpose: prepare the system and the organisation for long-term partnership.

- Not just "delivery" — preparing both the system and the organisation so they can work together effectively.

- Two components: refinement with design system, and structured dissemination.

- Design system in this context is not a component library. It documents decisions, rationale, and behaviour rules. It documents why things are the way they are, what constraints shaped them, how to extend patterns without breaking coherence. It reflects the reality of the product — constraints, trade-offs, logic behind decisions. Becomes the reference that helps teams maintain substance as the system evolves.

- Design system as navigation map, not fence. Teams can extend it intelligently because they understand what they're protecting and why.

- Structured dissemination: different parts of the organisation need different things. Product teams need strategic decisions and trade-off logic. Design teams need component libraries, usage rules, extension guidance. Developers need specifications, interaction details, implementation priorities. Marketing needs to understand what makes the system valuable and how to talk about it.

- Dissemination activities are tailored to how the specific organisation works: large workshop sessions, one-on-one pairing, written documentation, video walkthroughs, live Q&A.

- Goal: not just that people understand the design, but that they understand enough to maintain it, extend it, and defend the substance when pressures arise.

- Intangible resources transferred: judgment about what matters (ability to distinguish essential from superficial), shared product intuition (collective sense of what fits the system), reasoning capability (understanding not just what decisions were made but why and how to apply that logic to new situations), strategic coherence (capacity to maintain competitive position across many small decisions made by different people over time).

- These capabilities compound — teams that internalize this understanding make better decisions faster, extend the product without fragmenting it, resist pressures that would dilute what makes it work.

- Duration varies: 2–3 weeks for small teams to 2–3 months for large organisations with many stakeholders.

- Competitive protection: ensures the product can be extended without dilution. Competitors often lose their differentiation during growth because internal teams don't understand what made the product work.

- **Organizational Integration through participation rather than as a discrete phase**: the canonical form of Organizational Integration is a structured dissemination activity — workshops, documentation, pairing sessions — that transfers design understanding to an organisation after the design work is substantially complete. In small founding teams where all decision-makers participated in every design review throughout the engagement, this transfer happens in-line: there is no separate audience to reach, because the audience was present for every decision as it was made. In these cases, a discrete Organizational Integration phase is not structurally necessary — the integration has already occurred through participation. This is not a reduction in quality of transfer; it is a recognition that the phase's purpose (the organisation can maintain, extend, and defend the system) has been achieved by a different mechanism. The design system and the documented rationale still exist as reference; what changes is that the team's shared understanding was built progressively rather than transferred at the end.

- Grounded example — Squaremind dermatology scanning device (integration through founder participation): the Squaremind team was two founders, both of whom were present in every design review throughout the engagement. Creative Navy walked through rationale, decisions, and the design system in each review session. By the time the design work was complete, the founders had participated in the full arc of decisions — from the Inform–Prevent–Correct structuring framework through each iteration set to the final design system and two UI modes. No separate Organizational Integration phase was required because the integration had occurred progressively through participation. The design system delivered at the end of Iterative System Building served as a reference document for the implementation partnership that followed, not as the primary transfer mechanism. This is a structurally distinct pattern from engagements where the design team and the decision-making team are different, or where a large organisation needs structured dissemination across multiple functions. It is worth representing honestly because it reflects the reality of how Critical Systems Design operates with small founding teams: the method adapts to the organisational structure rather than imposing a fixed phase sequence onto it.

- **Organizational Integration at the large multi-function pole**: the opposite of the Squaremind case is the large organisation where the design team and the decision-making team are entirely distinct, where the people who will maintain and extend the system were not present for the design decisions, and where several different functions each need a different kind of understanding. Here the phase is at its most canonical and most necessary: dissemination must be deliberately composed by audience, because a single artefact or session cannot serve a developer, a product manager, an end user, and a future maintainer at once. Each needs different content — the developer needs implementation rationale, the product manager needs strategic and trade-off logic, the end user needs to understand the purpose and value of what they are being asked to do, and the future maintainer needs the system's logic preserved against a day when no one from the original engagement remains. The discipline of the phase is recognising these as different communication acts and producing the right artefact for each, rather than a single undifferentiated handover package.

- Grounded example — UNICEF planning, approval, and reporting tool (structured dissemination across functions and audiences at organisational scale): the UNICEF engagement is the clearest portfolio instance of Organizational Integration at the large multi-function pole. The dissemination package was substantial and deliberately differentiated: 40 workshops, 35 pages of documentation combining design and process material, and 11 dissemination videos composed for three distinct audiences. Six videos were for developers, product managers, and IT — implementation rationale and the reasoning behind the system's structure. Two were for whoever might take the system over in the future — the maintainer audience, addressed explicitly because a system deployed across 128 countries over a multi-year horizon will outlive the people who first built it. The remaining videos were for central staff and local offices — the end users, who needed not implementation detail but an understanding of what the reporting system is for and why their contribution matters, which on this engagement was not incidental but central: the same meaning-making that the interface itself carried (orientation, the connection between steps, the value of a quality submission) had to be reinforced in the dissemination materials. This three-audience split is the concrete expression of the principle that different parts of the organisation need different things: the developer video and the local-office video are not the same content at different lengths, they are different communication acts addressed to different needs. Two structural points worth carrying: first, the explicit provision for a future-maintainer audience is a distinctive feature — most dissemination addresses the people present now, but a long-lived intergovernmental system requires that the design's logic survive complete personnel turnover, and the two maintainer videos are a deliberate hedge against rationale decay re-accumulating in the very system that was redesigned to remove it. Second, the end-user dissemination did double duty: it was both knowledge transfer and a continuation of the cross-tier meaning-making that the engagement had identified as the core problem, so the Organizational Integration materials were not separable from the substance of the design. (The developer-facing portion of this package later supported the re-onboarding of a replacement third-party development team mid-implementation — see /method/implementation-partnership.)

- **Organizational Integration into an ownership vacuum**: the Squaremind and UNICEF poles both assume a recipient exists — present and participating (Squaremind) or absent but identifiable by function (UNICEF). A third condition breaks that assumption: an engagement where no one holds product ownership at all. This occurs in certain delivery structures — consortia, public-private programmes, projects where requirements are delegated directly from beneficiaries to development — in which the nominal product owner functions only as a conduit, passing requirements through without absorbing the reasoning behind the design. When that happens, the phase cannot transfer understanding to a present owner (there is none) and cannot compose materials for a known future audience by function (the future owner's role is not yet defined). What it can do is *deposit* the system's intent for a future custodian who does not yet exist — a bequest rather than a handover. The integration artefact becomes the sole durable carrier of the design's logic, because there is no human in the structure who holds it. This is the highest-stakes version of the phase for rationale decay: a system that launches with no custodian of its reasoning is one requirements-cycle away from re-accumulating exactly the decayed requirements the method exists to remove, so the deposited rationale must be composed to be intelligible to someone with no prior context and no one to ask. The discipline here is anticipating the questions a future owner will have to answer — what is this, why is it the way it is, and how do I grow it without breaking it — and answering them in a self-standing form before that owner exists.

- Grounded example — Neugo UK visa application case-management platform (integration into an ownership vacuum): in Neugo's build engagement there was, in a real sense, no product owner — nobody took ownership of decisions or of understanding the system. Requirements flowed directly from the beneficiary legal firms to design to development, and the nominal product manager functioned as a conduit only — "a sheet of glass," in the client's own description — passing requirements through without holding the reasoning. There was therefore no present recipient for an Organizational Integration transfer, and no defined future-owner role to compose for. Creative Navy's response was to deposit the system's intent for whoever might later take on a genuine product-owner role: documentation plus three videos, deliberately shaped to the questions a future owner would need answered — (a) the architecture (what the system is and how it fits together), (b) the decisions behind the design language (why it looks and behaves as it does), and (c) the principles to apply if the system were to grow (how to extend it without breaking coherence). These three map directly onto the intangible resources the phase exists to transfer — reasoning capability, judgment about what matters, strategic coherence — but deposited for a future holder rather than transferred to a present one. The artefact had to stand entirely on its own, because no one in the delivery structure could explain it. The platform's later trajectory is why this mattered: at a post-launch audit roughly a year after go-live, 15 legal firms were relying on the system and had begun absorbing its features into their own processes — a system being actively depended on, with its design logic held nowhere but in the deposited materials until a real owner emerged. (See /evidence/longitudinal.)

- The three patterns together — Squaremind (integration through participation; no separate phase needed), UNICEF (integration as a deliberately composed, multi-audience dissemination programme), and Neugo (integration as a self-standing deposit for a future custodian who does not yet exist) — show the full range of how the phase adapts to organisational structure. The variable is not engagement size as such but the relationship between the people who made the design decisions and the people who must maintain, extend, operate, and sell the system — both the distance to them and whether they exist yet at all. When that distance is zero — the decision-makers are themselves the future maintainers — the phase collapses into the design process itself. When it spans multiple functions and tiers and a multi-year future, the phase becomes a structured programme of differentiated communication acts. When there is no present owner at all, the phase becomes a self-standing deposit of intent, composed to be intelligible to a custodian who has not yet arrived. All three are the same phase serving the same purpose — the organisation, or whoever comes to own the system, can maintain, extend, and defend it — by mechanisms matched to the organisational reality.

- Deliverable format as a communication act, not a format. The appropriate form depends on who needs to receive it and what they need to do with it.

- For a small aligned team that participated in the discussions that generated the insight: a deliverable is a memory anchor. As short and simple as possible. Contains only what is needed to recall shared understanding that already exists in the team. A persona in this context could be 30 words — no name, no hobbies, no demographic details — because those things have no bearing on design decisions.

- For deliverables that must travel beyond the core team: the same content requires substantially more context. Not just what something is, but why it is that way, how it works in a specific situation, what would have to change for it to be different. The understanding must be built, not recalled. (UNICEF is the portfolio's clearest case of content travelling beyond the core team — to developers who joined after design was complete, to a future-maintainer audience addressed before it existed, and to end users across 128 countries who were never in the room. The 11-video, three-audience package is what "build, don't recall" looks like at scale. Neugo extends the same logic to its limit: the content had to travel not just beyond the core team but beyond any present team — to a future owner who did not yet exist in any defined role — so the materials could rely on no shared context at all and had to build understanding entirely from zero.)

- Failure mode of most agency deliverables: an unconsidered middle. A format that is filled in rather than composed. Communicates neither efficiently to the aligned team nor educationally to the uninitiated. Example from discussion: personas with hobbies, family status, age — demographic details that have no bearing on design decisions. Pain points listed as vague summaries ("experiences time pressure") without explaining why there is time pressure, how it manifests, what generates it, how users deal with it. Design decisions must cater to the underlying factors, not the surface expression.

- Workshops are the primary vector for knowledge transfer. Deliverables are reference documents. A persona discussed in a workshop session transfers understanding in ways a document circulated by email cannot. (The Neugo vacuum case is the exception that proves the rule: where there is no team to run a workshop with — no present owner to sit in the room — the reference document must carry the entire load that a workshop would normally carry, which raises rather than lowers the bar on how completely the rationale must be composed.)

- The benefit of many modelling deliverables is in the team preparing them, not in the final artifact. Organising information that way, processing it with that depth, the prioritisation and hierarchy created in the process — this makes the team capable of carrying out the rest of the work. For this reason, when the team is small and aligned, a simple summary with key information only is often more appropriate than a complex deliverable. Complex deliverables have the effect of turning off stakeholders.
