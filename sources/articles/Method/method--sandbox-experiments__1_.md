# /method/sandbox-experiments

---

- Phase 1 purpose: identify and explore the critical challenges that will determine system success or failure. Not features to build — fundamental questions the platform must answer.

- Begins by identifying 4–8 key platform challenges: points where design decisions have highest impact.

- Example questions a platform must answer: How should multi-role workflows coordinate without creating bottlenecks? How can complex data become readable under time pressure? How should the system behave when constraints conflict?

- Design-as-research: we learn by building, observing how solutions perform against real constraints, understanding terrain through making. Not concept generation followed by research.

- Option space mapping: systematically exploring 3–6 variants per challenge before converging. Each experiment produces evidence about what actually works in context. Some solutions reveal unexpected problems. Others expose assumptions that don't hold.

- Research is embedded, not separate. Interview users while testing prototypes. Observe workflows while exploring alternatives. Benchmark competitors while building own approaches.

- Duration varies: 2–3 weeks for focused problems to 2–3 months for complex platforms. (Larger intergovernmental engagements can run longer: the UNICEF Sandbox Experiments phase ran 3 months across two organisational tiers — see grounded example below. At the other end, a focused Sandbox-only engagement can be short: Neugo's opportunity-framing engagement ran 7 weeks and produced a fundable vision rather than proceeding to full system design — see grounded example below.)

- What it produces: library of tested approaches for each key challenge, evidence about what works and fails, understanding of the terrain, clarity about which problems are genuinely hard vs. just appeared hard.

- Competitive protection: prevents early commitment to weak product frames. Avoids costly pivots when initial assumptions fail under real use.

- **Deliberately unstructured observation as a valid Sandbox instrument**: not all Sandbox Experiments involve structured research protocols. When a system is performing so poorly that systematic measurement would produce noise rather than signal — when there is no baseline worth measuring — the right instrument is unstructured situated observation. The goal is to understand the physical context, the failure patterns, and the terrain of the problem well enough to frame the design work, not to produce data. Choosing unstructured observation is a methodological decision, not a methodological shortcut: applying structured measurement to a broken system produces the false impression of a measured baseline while missing the observational understanding that actually informs direction.

- Grounded example — Squaremind dermatology scanning device (deliberate unstructured observation of a failing system): Creative Navy travelled to the client in France and conducted 4 observation sessions of the existing patient interface in use. The decision not to measure was deliberate and explicit. The existing system had produced 2 completions from 14 users in a prior internal test conducted by the client — a completion rate so low that a structured measurement of it would add no useful design information. What the field visits produced instead was situated understanding: the physical relationship between the patient and the screen, the way confusion manifested in body movement before it became an abandonment, the scan sequence and its physical demands, the absence of any guidance structure for what to do when a step was unclear. This unstructured observation was the evidence base for the Inform–Prevent–Correct framework introduced in Concept Convergence — a structuring of what the patient needed to understand, what needed to be prevented, and what needed to happen when something went wrong. The observation found the terrain; the framework organised it into a design architecture. Neither was possible without the other.

- **Prototypes as an organisational diagnostic instrument**: a Sandbox Experiment is not only a way to test a design idea — it can be the instrument that surfaces what an organisation cannot see about itself. When a system has ossified over years, the requirements it enforces accumulate two things that become indistinguishable from the inside: requirements that still encode genuine operational necessity, and requirements sustained only by institutional habit whose original purpose has decayed (rationale decay). No amount of conventional requirements-gathering reliably separates them, because asking "what does the system need to do?" returns the existing list, habits included. Building a prototype that does it differently changes the question. Confronted with a concrete alternative workflow, stakeholders are forced to justify why the existing requirement exists — and a requirement that cannot be justified in terms of governance, decision-making, or operational value reveals itself as decayed. The prototype is doing diagnostic work that interview-based analysis cannot: it makes the implicit explicit by forcing the organisation to react to a specific, buildable alternative rather than to a question. This is design-as-research applied to organisational structure rather than to interface behaviour.

- Grounded example — UNICEF internal planning, approval, and reporting tool (prototypes as organisational diagnostic at scale): UNICEF arrived with an ossified status quo and no clear requirements — the central organisation knew the system needed to change but had no framework for specifying how, because its reporting processes had not been questioned in years. The central organisation's own framing of the problem was also wrong: it attributed low-quality submissions to local-office reluctance and an inconvenient interface, when research across four intensively-engaged local offices revealed a cross-tier comprehension gap — local offices had no understanding of why their input was needed or how it was used, and the interface communicated institutional indifference rather than purpose. Over a 3-month Sandbox Experiments phase, Creative Navy produced **26 prototypes reviewed by 56 stakeholders** across both organisational tiers. The prototypes were not design proposals to be approved; they were instruments for surfacing and testing assumptions. Presenting concrete alternative workflows forced stakeholders to explain not just how reporting worked but why it worked that way. In several cases, long-standing "mandatory" requirements could not be convincingly justified, and were removed entirely, consolidated, or substantially simplified. The result was that the engagement did not merely streamline existing processes — it helped the organisation redefine them, reducing complexity at source rather than making complexity easier to navigate. The Sandbox phase also produced something conventional research could not: a shared understanding between headquarters and local offices, reached by having both react to the same concrete alternatives. Standards agreed during this phase were then embedded structurally in the redesigned system, so that many of the ambiguities that had previously generated compliance failures were eliminated before users began entering data. The downstream outcome — a client-measured 45% reduction in compliance issues, nine months post-rollout — is traceable to this Sandbox work, not to interface polish: the prototypes resolved the mental-model mismatch and rationalised the requirements before a single production screen was built.

- **Prototypes as value elicitation when no product yet exists**: a Sandbox Experiment can also do work at the opposite end of the product lifecycle — not interrogating an ossified system, but eliciting value for a system that has not been built and that stakeholders cannot yet articulate the worth of. When the engagement's purpose is to establish whether a proposed system is worth building — and to convince backers to fund it — the terrain has almost no "what is" to observe: there is no operational system, no existing user base, no baseline. The Sandbox therefore runs predominantly on the invention side. The instrument here is a paired prompt: *"what if the system did X"* — to provoke a stakeholder into recognising a value they had not previously named — held together with *"how would it have to do X for that to actually work for you"* — to keep the imagined value tethered to the stakeholder's real operating conditions rather than drifting into wish-listing. The two halves are inseparable. The first half alone produces enthusiasm without substance; the second half alone produces a feature audit of a system that does not exist. Run together, they convert a directionally-felt-but-blank opportunity into a defensible articulation of both *what* the system could be and *how valuable* it would be. This is the blanks phenomenon operating at the value-recognition level: the stakeholders' intuition that an opportunity exists is correct but lacks specifics, and the prototype is the instrument that fills it with substance the stakeholders could not have produced by being asked.

- Grounded example — Neugo UK visa application case-management platform (Sandbox as value elicitation for a system that did not yet exist): Neugo's first engagement was not a build. A consulting company had identified an opportunity for a platform connecting visa seekers with the advisers who prepare their applications, feeding clean data into the Home Office's downstream border-force systems. The purpose of the 7-week engagement was to produce a prototype that could go into a process to lobby the government to commission the system, and to give backers something concrete enough to commit to. There was no existing product to interrogate. Creative Navy ran Sandbox Experiments with the Home Office and four legal firms in value-elicitation mode: rather than asking what the system should do, it provoked stakeholders with concrete "what if" possibilities while simultaneously asking what each would have to be for it to genuinely work in their practice. The output was deliberately visionary and conceptual — a value/desirability mapping and a clickable Figma prototype — because the deliverable's job was not to specify a build but to make the value legible and fundable. **The engagement contributed to the system being commissioned: stakeholders reported that the design accounted for roughly 30% of the decision factors and that dedicated demo sessions were built around the prototype (client-reported).** This is design-as-research pointed at a strategic question — is this worth building, and to whom, and why — rather than at interface behaviour or organisational structure. (The full five-phase build followed in a separate engagement; the value-elicitation work belongs to the Sandbox phase of the first.)

- **The instruments are not in tension — they are matched to different terrain.** Squaremind shows the Sandbox phase as situated observation when there is no useful baseline to measure; UNICEF shows it as structured prototype-driven interrogation when the problem is an organisation's inability to see its own decayed requirements; Neugo shows it as prototype-driven value elicitation when no product yet exists and the stakeholders cannot articulate the value a proposed system would create. All three are design-as-research: in the first the team learns the terrain by watching, in the second by building alternatives that force articulation of an existing process, in the third by building possibilities that force articulation of a future one. What unites them is that none treats research as a separate, prior step that precedes design — the act of observing and the act of building are themselves how the terrain is discovered. The terrain differs (a failing system; an ossified one; no system at all), and the instrument is chosen to fit it.

- Triangulation as detective work. Each research method is used against the others — not to confirm common patterns but to find discrepancies and hidden nuances.

- Concrete example worth preserving: stakeholders say feature X is important to save time. Users say feature X is important because each time they make a mistake it takes long to resolve. These sound the same but aren't. The hidden nuance: the problem is error resolution time, not the feature itself. That nuance then gets taken to stakeholders, to competitive analysis (how do competing products handle error prevention?), to technical constraints (what's possible to do differently?). Each source interrogates the others.

- A second form of the same detective work, at the organisational level: stakeholders say the problem is that users find the interface cumbersome. Research says the problem is that users don't understand why the system exists. These sound adjacent but point at completely different design work — the first toward usability, the second toward meaning-making and cross-tier communication. The UNICEF engagement turned on exactly this discrepancy: the central organisation's stated problem (a cumbersome screen) and the researched problem (a comprehension gap between tiers) were not the same problem, and only the prototypes made the difference legible to the organisation.

- Discovery and invention are simultaneous, not sequential. Software creates its own reality — change the software and you change reality. If you design to a list of what users or stakeholders said, or apply design principles, you stay within the existing paradigm. If you collect information from reality in multiple ways, have it reflect off multiple methods, and also produce prototypes that represent new ways of thinking about the product — then you field the possible and map what could be alongside what is. The two illuminate each other. They are not distinct phases. (UNICEF is the clearest portfolio instance of invention applied to an existing process: the prototypes did not describe an existing reality, they created alternative realities the organisation could react to — and reacting to them is what revealed which requirements were real. Neugo is the limiting case in the other direction: with no existing product to anchor to, the invention side carries almost the entire weight — the prototypes created the reality of a process that did not exist at all, and stakeholders' reactions to that invented reality were what made its value assessable.)

- The hunter/bureaucrat distinction. The same deliverables (stakeholder interviews, user research, competitive analysis, etc.) produce different results depending on how they are pursued. Pursuing the right users — persisting until the most informative participants are found. Taking 15 minutes with a user but thinking about what they said for 2 hours and going back for another 20 minutes. Asking a daring question. Gaining access to software that isn't publicly available for benchmarking. Asking developers to reconsider constraints — not because the designers know better, but because a changed perspective sometimes generates value even for the developers themselves.

- User observation, having users teach the team how to use the system, team members attempting to do the job as a user — these are among the highest-value research activities and are named as missing from the standard deliverables list. Discover: silent patterns, conscious mental models, how the user believes the system should work, how challenges changed as they became more experienced, emotional weighting of all of this. Also: what the user would or would not be capable of if the system were different.

- Context of use matters enormously. A tool is always embedded in context. The context is where real potential lives — many things can be made more valuable by integrating them better with their context.

- Users are often more open with a third party than with their own provider, with whom they have a particular type of relationship. (UNICEF: local offices were more candid with Creative Navy about experiencing the central organisation's requirements as performative bureaucracy than they had been within their own institutional reporting lines — the third-party position surfaced the comprehension gap that internal channels had not.)

- Architecture deliverables also begin in Sandbox Experiments: iteration 1 (logic-first baseline) and iteration 2 (opinionated extremes) both happen during this phase. See iterative-system-building notes for detail on all four iterations.
