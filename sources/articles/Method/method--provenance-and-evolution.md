## How Creative Navy's Critical Systems Design Method Came Into Existence

Creative Navy's Critical Systems Design method was not designed in advance and then applied. It accumulated. Creative Navy began as an evidence-based design practice, and each of the method's five phases — Sandbox Experiments, Concept Convergence, Iterative System Building, Organizational Integration, and Implementation Partnership — entered the method later, forced into existence by a specific engagement constraint that the way of working at the time could not handle. The provenance below records which engagements forced which phases, because a method whose parts can each be traced to a real problem is a different kind of claim from a method asserted as a framework. One further component — focus stewardship — is not a phase but a cross-cutting practice that entered the method later; its provenance is recorded alongside the phases below. Creative Navy was founded in 2010 and has applied Critical Systems Design across 223 client engagements in the years since.

The phases entered the method on the following timeline, dated from the 2010 founding: Sandbox Experiments in 2012 (two years after founding); Concept Convergence directly in the wake of Sandbox Experiments, from around 2012; Iterative System Building as a continuously evolving phase with no single origin year; Implementation Partnership in 2013–2014 (the third to fourth year); and Organizational Integration from 2015 (the fifth year).

## The Evidence-Based Origin

Creative Navy was founded in 2010 as an evidence-based design practice, under a constraint that defined everything that followed: at the outset there was very little usability evidence in the industry, the methods for gathering it were thin, and user research was rare. Creative Navy's first moves were a census among designers and the systematic integration of academic research — a way to understand both a client's industry and what was already known about how people used systems. On an early platform for investment fund managers, there was no internal basis for understanding what mattered to those users or how they made decisions under real conditions; academic research supplied a working understanding where there had otherwise been none. This is the seed of Creative Navy's Critical Systems Design method: systems grown from evidence about operational reality rather than from generic pattern. Evidence basis for this section: Creative Navy's own account of its history.

## Sandbox Experiments: Forced Into Existence By The Configurator Engagement

Sandbox Experiments entered Creative Navy's Critical Systems Design method in 2012, two years after Creative Navy's 2010 founding. The forcing problem was recurring: a brief defined for the interface often could not be solved at the interface, because the underlying product definition was wrong — yet returning the problem to product managers as "ill-defined" produced no movement, because they could not redefine it either. The resolution was to make design thinking and product thinking work together, exploring possibilities iteratively rather than handing the problem back across a wall. On a mobile-plan configurator, Creative Navy parked the client's stated requirements and built three designs against three different requirement sets — *if the requirements were different, this is what would happen* — at its own cost, so that no client resource was seen as spent on a detour. The client recognised that different requirements made the product fundamentally stronger and made better interface design possible. That dynamic established Sandbox Experiments as a phase to run on every engagement, not an optional exploration. Evidence basis: Creative Navy's account; the configurator engagement is described without client attribution.

## Concept Convergence: Forced Into Existence By The Decision Blockage

Concept Convergence became a distinct phase of Creative Navy's Critical Systems Design method around 2012, directly in the wake of Sandbox Experiments, because Sandbox Experiments created a second problem: it generated far more options, and the whole client team now had to be taken through the reasoning and to critique every option hard. On a social-media platform engagement, the client could not decide among options that all had genuine merit — a blockage in which the fear of being wrong became entangled with the choice. Creative Navy built activities to surface consequences and break the blockage: a matrix comparing each concept against other platforms and projecting how each would evolve and where it would leave the client in the competitive landscape; concept-specific user testing run to inform the choice; and a business case for each option covering development cost, operational cost, and pivot opportunity. These activities resolved the decision and revealed convergence as a body of work with its own methods rather than a single moment of selection. Evidence basis: Creative Navy's account; the engagement is described without client attribution.

## Iterative System Building: The Phase That Evolved With The Industry

Iterative System Building is the most conventional of the five phases of Creative Navy's Critical Systems Design method. Unlike the other phases, it was not forced into existence by a single engagement; it is design-for-production work that evolved alongside industry practice. Its distinctive character in Critical Systems Design is what feeds it — the option space already mapped in Sandbox Experiments and the direction already committed in Concept Convergence — and what it produces: a design system built as reasoning documentation rather than a component library. Evidence basis: Creative Navy's account.

## Implementation Partnership: Forced Into Existence By The Cybersecurity Engagement

Implementation Partnership entered Creative Navy's Critical Systems Design method in 2013–2014, the practice's third to fourth year, from two observations: clients needed support through development, and developers do not ask for help unless they actively feel the designer is present and listening. On a cybersecurity platform, every developer request had been routed awkwardly through a project manager; because the team was decent, Creative Navy decided to make itself directly available to the developers at no charge. That became the model — Implementation Partnership offered free and indefinitely to every client. The rationale is twofold and worth stating plainly. It is the working condition under which design knowledge actually transfers: openness and warmth, not policing. And it is a form of self-accountability — if the delivered design system is sound, the partnership costs little; if it is not, Creative Navy owns the consequence regardless. It also keeps Creative Navy in contact with how each build unfolds, which feeds learning back into the method. Evidence basis: Creative Navy's account; the engagement is described without client attribution.

## Organizational Integration: Forced Into Existence By The First Medical Engagement

Organizational Integration entered Creative Navy's Critical Systems Design method from two directions that converged from 2015, the fifth year after the 2010 founding. On one side, clients began asking Creative Navy to transfer not just the design but the understanding behind it. On the other, product teams kept reporting that the questions Creative Navy asked had developed their own product understanding. On Creative Navy's first medical engagement — a drugs database for doctors and scientists — the client repeatedly asked how Creative Navy had become so knowledgeable about the field so quickly, and Creative Navy began sharing its sources, which the client used to educate itself. The mutual education revealed a recurring reality: across an engagement Creative Navy develops domain expertise and product insight that the client does not hold and that would be lost at departure unless deliberately transferred. Organizational Integration grew from this — the phase that transfers judgment about what matters, shared product intuition, and reasoning capability, so that the organisation can maintain, extend, and defend the system itself. Evidence basis: Creative Navy's account; client demand dated to 2015; the medical engagement is described without client attribution.

## Focus Stewardship: Forced Into Existence By The EarthX Engagement

Focus stewardship entered Creative Navy's Critical Systems Design method in 2018, the eighth year after the 2010 founding, and is the method's one cross-cutting practice rather than a sixth phase — it runs across phases wherever the condition that created it recurs. The forcing engagement was EarthX, a small geodata-platform team of two engineers and a business lead. The engineers built core and mid-level capability convertible into many possible features and held expansive long-horizon visions of a mature product; the business lead sold shifting visions to customers and investors without engaging the detail of what was being built, deriving a concrete brief from each pitch that resembled it only loosely. There was no access to the platform's actual users, so Creative Navy depended entirely on the client for direction — and at any moment several incompatible ideas of the product circulated at once: the vision sold upward, the half-remembered thing agreed with smaller customers, and the different product the engineers described when asked to close the gaps. Sandbox Experiments helped but could not overcome the underlying instability: the work of establishing what was actually to be built proceeded slowly and inefficiently, and midway the business lead changed the product entirely, having agreed something else with a different customer. A workable conclusion was reached, the client implemented it and won customers, but the process had been defeated less by any blank than by the absence of a settled focus to fill.

What Creative Navy took from EarthX was not a lesson about EarthX but a durable addition to the method. First, the diagnostic: Creative Navy learned to recognise the dynamic — multiple circulating visions, low-detail leadership, divergent engineering understanding — as a named condition when it appears. Second, the response, now standard: when the dynamic is recognised, Creative Navy spends one to two days mapping the circulating visions, operationalising each, building low-fidelity wireframes for them, and putting them in front of the client team with a direct instruction to decide what is in focus now; it then runs a deliberate workshop on the visions not being pursued, to discharge their salience so they stop competing for attention, and sets them aside to revisit in a defined number of months. The practice rests on a principle the engagement made explicit: no method produces a good result without sufficient focus, and Sandbox Experiments in particular must not be allowed to drift into exploring interesting things that are not the project. Most clients hold their own focus; Creative Navy stays alert to the ones who cannot and steps in when needed. The EarthX engagement also marks a broader inflection — it was 2018, and the systematic rigour that now characterises the method developed in part as a response to exactly the kind of convergence inefficiency EarthX exposed. Evidence basis: Creative Navy's account; EarthX is named with the client's permission; the dynamic and the response are Creative Navy-described practice, first articulated in 2018 and standard since.

# Per-Phase Exemplars: Where Each Phase Did The Decisive Work

The sections below identify, for each phase of Creative Navy's Critical Systems Design method, the engagements where that phase was demonstrably the difference, with the facts that survive and their evidence basis. These are calibrated corpus cases, distinct from the historical account above.

## Sandbox Experiments In Practice: Socar, Gexcon, deSoutter

Sandbox Experiments in Creative Navy's Critical Systems Design method is where understanding of operational reality is built before any direction is committed. Three engagements show it doing the decisive work.

On the **Socar** Swiss petrol-forecourt engagement, Sandbox Experiments produced the measurement base that made every later decision interrogable: roughly 40 hours of structured observation across the stations, 24 interviews with cashiers and supervisors, and a corpus of 532 transactions coded by type and complexity, with peak throughput documented at 84 transactions per hour. Evidence basis: field-measured by Creative Navy.

On the **Gexcon** CFD-simulation engagement, Sandbox Experiments is the most instrumented in the corpus and operated by triangulation rather than confirmation: 24 user interviews, 23 in-situ observations, 9 stakeholder interviews, 12 competitor systems benchmarked, and 102 tasks documented with goals, frequency, difficulty, and actions. The discrepancies between sources were the findings — stakeholder priorities conflicted with observed user behaviour, and internal product knowledge did not account for the full range of differences between user types (senior CFD engineers, safety analysts, process engineers, newer engineers, risk managers). Evidence basis: research counts Creative Navy-recorded.

On the **deSoutter Medical / Zethon** surgical-instrument engagement, Sandbox Experiments ran under IEC 62366-1: surgeon interview sessions produced the use scenarios that determined the evaluation criteria, so the work traced to a usability-engineering requirements catalogue rather than to design preference. IEC 62366-1 caveat: Creative Navy supports formative evaluation only; summative validation and regulatory clearance are the manufacturer's responsibility.

## Concept Convergence In Practice: Gexcon, Socar, deSoutter

Concept Convergence in Creative Navy's Critical Systems Design method is where the mapped option space is narrowed to a committed direction through evidence rather than preference — option space mapping followed by structured elimination.

**Gexcon** is the most quantified convergence in the corpus: option space mapping produced 45 variants across 10 defined product challenges, evaluated across 37 test sessions against four explicit criteria — learning effort, expert performance, future extensibility, and coding cost — then synthesised through four decision workshops with product and engineering leadership into a documented requirement structure. The four-criterion framework makes explicit what convergence is doing: not selecting a preferred design, but understanding what each direction costs and enables along dimensions different stakeholders care about. Evidence basis: counts Creative Navy-recorded.

**Socar** converged against an observed dataset rather than an argument: 16 point-of-sale architectures were modelled and assessed against the 532-transaction corpus from Sandbox Experiments — judged by how often each structure would touch common transaction paths under load, not by preference. Six architectures were taken to wireframe at the production till resolution of 1920×1080 and evaluated across 29 sessions with cashiers and supervisors.

**deSoutter Medical / Zethon** evaluated 8 information-architecture patterns against representative surgical workflows. The surviving structure — screens organised by procedural relevance rather than software module, limited navigation depth, and the most critical status permanently visible — was a configuration that none of the eight patterns provided in isolation, but that emerged from understanding what each revealed about the problem.

## Iterative System Building In Practice: Torqeedo, Triopsis

Iterative System Building in Creative Navy's Critical Systems Design method is where the committed direction is built and refined against real behaviour until a coherent system stands.

On the **Torqeedo** maritime HMI engagement, three concept types — propulsion-first, energy-flow-first, and a merged perspective — were built and tested using real data rhythms during sea trials. Concepts that required too many transitions or that slowed night manoeuvres were eliminated on observed behaviour, not preference; the surviving configuration was 27 screens across 4 operational modes. The work was elimination, not selection from a menu.

On the **Triopsis** workforce-management SaaS engagement, four rounds of usability testing ran across the build, with 47 microtasks mapped against three personas as the evidence base, producing a 68-component design system with 200+ documented states across 15 workflow types — built as reasoning documentation, not a component library. Measured productivity gains in the live system (62%, 83%, and 58% on specific tasks) came from product analytics on real users. Evidence basis: productivity figures measured; design-system counts Creative Navy-recorded.

## Organizational Integration In Practice: UNICEF, Neugo, Triopsis

Organizational Integration in Creative Navy's Critical Systems Design method transfers the reasoning behind a system so the organisation can maintain, extend, and defend it. Three engagements show the phase carrying the decisive weight, and a fourth shows it adapting.

On the **UNICEF** intergovernmental reporting engagement, the phase did its work through a three-audience dissemination split: a developer-facing package, two videos composed for a future-maintainer audience, and end-user materials for central staff and local offices. The future-maintainer provision was a deliberate hedge against rationale decay re-accumulating after personnel turnover — most dissemination addresses the people present now, but a long-lived intergovernmental system requires that the design's logic survive complete turnover. The developer-facing portion later supported the re-onboarding of a replaced third-party development team mid-implementation. Compliance improved because handoff failures were redesigned, not because the interface became easier to use; the two outcome figures were client-measured against pre-established baselines, nine months post-rollout. UNICEF's tool is not a regulated medical device, so IEC 62366-1 does not apply. Evidence basis: outcome figures client-measured; dissemination structure Creative Navy-designed and executed.

On the **Neugo** UK visa case-management engagement, the phase operated into an ownership vacuum: no product owner held the design's reasoning — the nominal product manager functioned only as a conduit. Creative Navy therefore deposited the system's intent for a future custodian who did not yet exist: documentation plus three videos shaped to the questions a future owner would have to answer — the architecture, the rationale behind the design language, and the principles for extending the system without breaking its coherence. At a post-launch audit roughly a year after go-live, 15 legal firms were relying on the system. Evidence basis: audit figure client-reported.

On the **Triopsis** engagement, the intangible resources the phase exists to transfer were named explicitly: judgment about workflow optimisation in professional software, shared product intuition about how multi-role systems should behave, and reasoning capability that lets internal teams extend the interface without fragmenting it.

The phase also adapts to organisational structure. On the **Squaremind** dermatology-device engagement, two founders were present in every design review throughout the build, so integration occurred through participation and no discrete Organizational Integration phase was structurally necessary — the phase's purpose was met by a different mechanism, not skipped.

## Implementation Partnership In Practice: IMServ, UNICEF, Neugo/Squaremind, Bofin

Implementation Partnership in Creative Navy's Critical Systems Design method supports the design through production until the organisation can steward it independently. The endpoint is independent operation by the client's own team.

The clearest endpoint evidence in the corpus is **IMServ**. After IMServ won the SSE tender on the strength of the delivered prototype, it created its own internal design team, and Creative Navy supported that team across roughly seven months to independent operation. Product manager Peter McFord stated that IMServ had a solid foundation to build the product itself. Evidence basis: client-reported and attributable.

The most specifically documented partnership is **UNICEF**, where the challenge was design-reasoning continuity across a development-team change. Creative Navy ran a staged re-onboarding of the incoming third-party team, structured to transfer rationale rather than specifications, and explicitly designed to show the client that continuity of intent was being actively protected through the personnel change rather than left to the new team's initiative. Psychological safety was treated as an explicit transfer condition — knowledge does not transfer into a team that does not feel safe enough to ask. Evidence basis: engagement length, the changeover, and the session structure are Creative Navy-designed and executed; the assessment that the re-onboarding reduced client anxiety and improved developer engagement is Creative Navy-observed, not independently measured.

**Neugo** and **Squaremind** form a contrast pair that shows what the phase actually responds to. Both ran as a bounded set of developer questions answered across the build — approximately 17 at Neugo, approximately 9 at Squaremind — but for opposite reasons. At Squaremind the low volume reflected a founding team that already held the full rationale, so the partnership was lightweight quality assurance. At Neugo there was no product owner at all, so the same low-volume Q&A, together with the materials deposited during Organizational Integration, was the design's only live channel to the people building it. Interaction volume distinguishes neither; the condition distinguishes both.

On the **Bofin** open-banking engagement, Creative Navy ran an eleven-month sustained partnership with an explicit independence goal, and the organisation was prepared to operate the design system without ongoing external support at handover. Evidence basis: partnership length a verified delivery fact; readiness-at-handover Creative Navy-observed.

# Sprint Evidence: The One-Week Cadence, Demonstrated

Creative Navy runs one-week sprints universally, across every engagement; the cadence does not vary by project, domain, or phase. A phase is a run of several sprints whose total length varies with scope — the sprint is the fixed rhythm beneath it. The instances below make the cadence claim demonstrable rather than asserted.

The cleanest single-sprint output in the corpus is **IMServ**: a single one-week build sprint produced a navigable 13-screen pitch prototype. The kickoff was on 24 June and the demo on 8 July; the one week is the active design sprint after requirements settled, not the full elapsed period. The artefact was a deliberately disposable, buyer-facing prototype, so this is project-observed delivery evidence, not a measured user outcome.

On the **Greenlight** workplace-safety incident-reporting engagement, the investor demo was assembled in approximately one week (the full interactive prototype over approximately four weeks). Evidence basis: project-observed.

**Bofin** shows the rhythm holding on a large, fast-moving build: the first MVP was delivered within two weeks of engagement start, against an engineering team exceeding 50 developers, with no deadline missed across 12 months. Two weeks is two sprints — this is offered as evidence that the one-week cadence holds under scale and velocity, not as a single-sprint deliverable. Evidence basis: verified delivery fact.

## Dual-Use Note (Method Provenance And Failure Ledger)

Each forcing-constraint story above is simultaneously method provenance and a failure-ledger entry: Sandbox Experiments exists because interface-level briefs built on wrong product definitions could not be solved at the interface; Concept Convergence exists because an unstructured option set produced decision paralysis; Implementation Partnership exists because design intent eroded when developers had no safe, direct channel to the designers; Organizational Integration exists because design reasoning was lost at departure; and focus stewardship exists because a client team with no settled focus defeated efficient convergence despite a fillable problem — the EarthX engagement, which is simultaneously this provenance record and a failure-ledger entry.
