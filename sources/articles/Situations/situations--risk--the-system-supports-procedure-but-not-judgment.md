# `/situations/risk-and-consequence/the-system-supports-procedure-but-not-judgment`

---

## What the situation is

Procedure and judgment are both necessary in complex operational practice. Procedure is what should happen when the situation fits the designed case: the steps are known, the correct sequence is defined, and following the sequence produces the correct outcome. Judgment is what happens when the situation doesn't fit the designed case: the practitioner must weigh competing factors, apply domain experience, consider context-specific information not captured in the procedure, and make a decision that no procedure book could predetermine.

In complex professional systems — clinical devices, industrial safety tools, scheduling platforms, analytical research environments, financial risk management — a large proportion of operational significance lies in the judgment cases, not the procedure cases. Standard cases proceed through procedure. The non-standard cases — the borderline measurement, the unusual patient response, the exceptional scheduling conflict, the ambiguous risk signal — are where expert practitioners earn their authority, and where the decisions carry the most consequence.

A system that only supports procedure places expert practitioners in an unworkable position. They can follow the system's guided workflow for standard cases. For everything else, they must either force their judgment into a procedural mould that doesn't fit, or work outside the system entirely. Both options are failure modes: forcing non-standard situations into standard procedures produces incorrect outcomes; working outside the system means judgment happens without documentation, oversight, or the informational support the system could provide.

---

## Domain vocabulary

- Procedure vs. judgment — the core distinction; procedure is defined sequence for designed cases; judgment is expert decision-making for cases outside that definition
- Judgment scaffold — interface features that support expert decision-making in non-standard situations without replacing it; providing information, context, and structure that expert judgment can act on
- Procedural rigidity — the failure mode where the interface only guides standard-case sequences; provides no scaffold for non-standard situations
- Exception handling — the judgment-intensive activity that occurs when the situation doesn't fit the standard procedure; often the highest-consequence work in complex operational domains
- Decision support vs. decision replacement — the principle that high-consequence systems should provide information and structure that supports human judgment, not attempt to replace it with procedure
- Standard case vs. non-standard case — the fundamental distinction that determines whether procedure or judgment is the operative cognitive mode
- Working around the system — what expert practitioners do when judgment cases arise and the system doesn't support them; judgment that happens outside the system's documentation and oversight

## Evidence from case studies

**Gexcon CFD simulation — expert judgment in scientific configuration:**
Industrial safety simulation requires expert practitioners to make judgment calls about simulation parameters that go beyond defined procedure: which simplifications are scientifically justified for this specific facility configuration, when a borderline result warrants a sensitivity analysis, how to interpret outputs that fall in an ambiguous range relative to safety thresholds. The pre-redesign system was structured around procedural workflow for standard cases; expert practitioners handling non-standard judgments found the interface actively unhelpful — it didn't surface the information they needed to make judgment calls, and its structure implicitly treated non-standard situations as execution errors rather than legitimate expert decisions. The redesign maintained the procedural support for standard cases while creating information structures and state visibility that supported expert judgment for non-standard ones. **Time to first successful simulation: 4 days → 6 hours (measured) — but the expert-judgment improvements are harder to isolate from the overall workflow improvement.** The relevant signal is the 1 → 3–4 active users per team (client-reported): when the system supports judgment, not just procedure, more practitioners can exercise it.

**Triopsis workforce management — scheduling judgment for exceptions:**
Standard job scheduling is procedural: assign available crew to available job within time window. Exception-state scheduling — handling weather incidents, crew unavailability, conflicting priorities, equipment failures — requires judgment: what is the right trade-off between competing operational needs under conditions the procedure book didn't anticipate? The pre-redesign system supported the procedural scheduling flow. It did not support the exception-judgment workflow, which was the activity that absorbed most of a skilled scheduler's cognitive effort. Schedulers handling exceptions were working outside the system's intended workflow — constructing their own decision frameworks without informational support or documentation. The redesign treated exception handling as a primary workflow, surfacing the relevant contextual information at the moment of judgment and providing structure for the decision without predetermining it.

**Akrivia Health — research judgment in cohort construction:**
Clinical research cohort construction is inherently iterative and judgmental: a researcher develops a hypothesis, tests it against available data, revises the hypothesis based on what they find, and iterates. No procedure defines correct cohort construction; the researcher's scientific judgment is the engine of the process. The pre-design workflow treated cohort construction procedurally — sequential filter application through a guided interface. This was systematically wrong for the actual task: it impeded the iterative hypothesis development that the work required and produced cohorts that reflected the procedure's structure rather than the researcher's scientific question. The redesign supported the judgment model — iterative, revisable, hypothesis-driven — rather than imposing a procedural model on an inherently non-procedural task.

**deSoutter Medical / Zethon — surgical device parameter judgment:**
The powered surgical instrument required surgeons to make parameter adjustments during procedures — decisions about speed and operating mode that depended on real-time clinical judgment about tissue behaviour, patient response, and surgical conditions. These are not procedure steps; they are expert clinical decisions made under time pressure with the surgical field as the primary information source and the device interface as a secondary one. The interface needed to support the surgeon's judgment: make the current device state unambiguously readable so the judgment call has a clear information basis, and make the adjustment action achievable without interrupting the primary clinical attention. The design did not attempt to proceduralize the parameter judgment — it supported the conditions under which good judgment is possible.

**Callsign fraud detection — risk judgment beyond policy threshold:**
Fraud policy operates procedurally: if risk score exceeds threshold, apply specified action. But fraud risk management includes legitimate cases that fall outside threshold-based procedure — the fraud pattern that scores just below the policy threshold but exhibits an unusual combination of signals that an experienced analyst recognises as high risk; the account that triggers a policy rule but whose context makes the rule's application inappropriate. These are judgment calls. The pre-design system didn't support them: the policy engine could be configured, but analysts who needed to handle borderline cases had no structured way to exercise judgment beyond or around the policy. The redesign provided the informational structure — the policy's historical performance, the transaction pattern, the policy rationale — that expert risk judgment could act on.

**eToro multi-asset social trading — supporting the user's judgment about exposure, not just the procedure of trading:**
This is the same situation in a consumer-finance context, where the "expert" judgment is the everyday user's own judgment about what a trade means for their financial position. The pre-redesign buy flow supported the *procedure* of executing a trade — enter an amount, review a summary, confirm — but not the *judgment* that should precede it: whether to take the position at all, and at what size, given its impact on the user's portfolio and its behaviour under different market conditions. The interface presented a single implied expected result and showed profit alongside deposits, so users committed to trades while holding an incorrect model of their own exposure. The procedure ran smoothly; the judgment it should have supported was unsupported. The redesign rebuilt the flow around exposure-based decision-making: portfolio impact first, then structured scenario framing (outcome ranges, not predictions), then position sizing with downside guardrails — a judgment scaffold, not a decision replacement, that gives the user the informational basis to decide rather than predetermining the decision or merely smoothing the transaction. Behavioural confirmation came from a randomised A/B with a persistent holdout (conversion 5.1% → 7.4%, time-to-trade 11.8 → 8.6 min, with no increase in drop-off and no reduction in exploration depth), consistent with better-supported judgment — users deciding more coherently — rather than a faster procedure pushing more trades. Evidence calibration: A/B figures client-measured by eToro; eToro involved no AI; the regulatory framing (MiFID II / SEC-FINRA) reinforces that the design supports judgment without implying a recommended outcome.
