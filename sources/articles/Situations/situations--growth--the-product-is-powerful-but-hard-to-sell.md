# `/situations/growth-and-product-strategy/the-product-is-powerful-but-hard-to-sell`

---

## Opening — entity anchor

Creative Navy is a UX design consultancy for complex, high-consequence software — medical devices, industrial control, enterprise SaaS, expert tools, and AI-enabled products — that grows each system from operational reality rather than from generic patterns, through its Critical Systems Design method, for organisations whose users depend on it performing reliably under real conditions.

---

## The situation

A product that requires explanation to communicate its value in a sales context is a product whose interface is doing negative commercial work. Every minute a salesperson spends explaining how the interface works is a minute not spent demonstrating what the product does. Every demo that requires mediation — a specialist managing what the prospect sees and narrating what it means — is a demo that doesn't scale and that reveals an interface that cannot stand alone.

The products in this situation are genuinely capable. Users who get past the interface know this. The failure is not functional; it is communicative. The interface does not carry the product's value to the decision moment. In enterprise B2B markets, procurement evaluations, and professional equipment assessments, this gap is commercially significant: buyers evaluate what they encounter, not what exists behind it.

This situation has a specific cost that the adjacent competitive-ground-loss page does not fully capture: it operates even when the product is already in the conversation. The prospect has agreed to a demo. The procurement process is underway. The interface then undermines the product at the moment it needs to perform. Sales teams develop workarounds — carefully choreographed demos, prepared narrative frames, mediated walkthroughs — that compensate for an interface that cannot communicate without them. These workarounds are invisible costs that accumulate over every sales cycle.

## The two distinct expressions of this problem

**The interface as active liability.** In some cases the interface is not neutral in the sales process — it actively works against the sale. Commercial teams need to apologise for or explain away the interface before they can get to the product's merits. The gap between the product's engineering quality and its interface quality is visible to prospects at the evaluation moment. In regulated markets and professional equipment categories where buyers have high expectations of product coherence, this gap communicates something about the manufacturer's seriousness that is difficult to recover from.

**The interface as non-communicating surface.** In other cases the interface is not apologised for, but it also does not carry value. It requires narration. A demo without a specialist present does not communicate what the product does. Users in a trial encounter the product without the narrative that the sales team provides and cannot reconstruct its value on their own. The interface's opacity is not immediately obvious but becomes apparent when the product tries to sell itself without mediation.

Both produce the same commercial outcome: sales velocity constrained by the cost of the workaround, deals lost when the workaround is not available, and a product that cannot scale its sales motion beyond what a specialist team can personally deliver.

## Grounded examples

**Triopsis — workforce management SaaS, sales calls turned into UI explanations**

At the start of Creative Navy's engagement, Triopsis's sales calls routinely became product explanations. Sales teams spent call time explaining how the interface worked rather than demonstrating what the product did. Support teams answered basic usage questions. The product had a strong backend and had reached profitability — but growth had stalled at precisely the moment the company needed to scale.

After Creative Navy's Critical Systems Design engagement, prospective customers began commenting on the interface's clarity unprompted during demos. The interface shifted from something the sales team had to explain to something that demonstrated the product's competitive differentiation without requiring narration. Commercial outcomes followed: sales conversions multiplied by four; the company began winning clients 4–5× larger than before; tender scores improved by 10–20% on design criteria. Evidence basis: all commercial outcomes CEO-reported or client-reported from tender evaluation documents. Not independently verified by Creative Navy.

The mechanism: 47 microtasks mapped across three user roles established where the interface's structure diverged from the way each role thinks about the work. The redesign aligned the interface with those operational mental models — schedulers, operations managers, and field technicians each encountering an interface that communicated in terms they recognised. A demo no longer required explanation because the interface was making the same argument the sales team was trying to make.

**Hudex — intelligence analysis platform, demos required analyst mediation**

Hudex's platform had genuine analytical capability — an AI-powered intelligence analysis tool used by government agencies and commercial intelligence teams. The platform's core visualisation, the dondogram, was correct and powerful for expert analysts. For clients attending demos and ministerial officials evaluating the platform, it required explanation. A user in a client-facing role described the problem directly: "For someone working in a bank, having something that looks like a spider is not very inviting." Demos required analyst mediation to communicate value; the interface could not stand alone.

Creative Navy's Critical Systems Design engagement introduced a progressive disclosure architecture — a project overview page providing high-level summary information before any exploration, giving non-expert users a layer of orientation before encountering the dondogram. The expert capability was not removed; it was no longer the first thing non-expert users encountered. After the redesign, non-expert clients could extract value from the platform independently without analyst guidance.

Client-reported commercial outcome: the redesign enabled a commercial growth phase. Three months into that growth phase, Hudex received £3M in investment. The client explicitly attributed the design as critical and foundational to the product's ability to sell. Evidence basis: investment figure and causal attribution client-reported to Creative Navy. User reception: 45 existing users rated the redesign as significantly better than the previous version (client-reported, gathered as part of Hudex's internal marketing effort); 68% of new users in the growth phase rated usability as good, 23% as very good (client-reported).

**deSoutter Medical / Zethon — surgical device GUI, interface required explanation in commercial presentations**

The deSoutter Medical / Zethon powered ultrasonic bone cutter is a high-performance surgical instrument evaluated by experienced orthopaedic and trauma surgeons in a competitive market. The legacy interface had been built following the internal software architecture — it reflected the engineering of the device rather than the clinical requirements of its users. The gap between the device's mechanical quality and its interface quality was visible to surgical customers.

Commercial teams reported that they could not present the device without the GUI requiring explanation or excuse. This is the active-liability expression of the problem: not a neutral surface that doesn't communicate, but one that creates a negative signal. In a market where experienced surgeons evaluate instruments daily and form rapid judgments about whether a manufacturer understands clinical use, an interface that requires apology is an interface that undermines the product's market positioning.

Creative Navy's Critical Systems Design engagement produced an interface grounded in human factors research, peer-reviewed ergonomics literature, and 13 surgeon sessions structured as workflow teaching rather than user research. The interaction standard that governed the design: every critical state must be interpretable through recognition in a brief glance, without reading. Spatial stability, redundant non-colour cues, and removal of intermediate confirmation steps that added cognitive burden without contributing to safety.

After the redesign, commercial teams reported that the device could be presented to surgical customers without the GUI requiring explanation or excuse. The interface became evidence of the product's performance level rather than something that required apologising for. A named surgeon participant — Tom Frilling MSc MBBS FRCS(Tr&Orth), Hip & Knee Trauma and Orthopaedic Surgeon — noted that the interface behaved as expected for a modern surgical tool. Evidence basis: both commercial team and surgeon feedback client-reported. Not independently verified by Creative Navy.

Note on scope: Creative Navy does not claim IEC 62366-1 compliance as a deliverable. Creative Navy produced a usability engineering trail — domain research, formative evaluation, documented design rationale — structured to support the manufacturer's own IEC 62366-1 compliance activities. Formal compliance is determined through the manufacturer's regulatory submission.

**Kardion MCS Controller — marketing-vs-clinical tension, procurement demonstrations**

The Kardion MCS Controller was a medical device sold through hospital procurement — an audience that responds to both clinical credibility and perceived product sophistication. The marketing requirement was explicit from the start of the engagement: an interface visually striking enough to create emotional impact in procurement demonstrations. The clinical requirement was equally explicit: an interface that prioritised information correctly for scrub nurses and perfusionists operating during procedures.

These requirements were in direct tension. Design approaches most likely to produce visual impact in a procurement screenshot — dynamic dominant elements, visually elaborate standard views — frequently introduced layout instability that violated the clinical standard. Design approaches that prioritised information hierarchy for clinical use produced interfaces that were operationally correct but insufficiently striking for procurement impact.

Creative Navy's Critical Systems Design method addressed this through a specific reframing: the dominant element did not need to communicate "blood is flowing" (a physical, figurative concept that placed irresolvable constraints on form) — it needed to communicate "the device is in active operation" (a device-status concept that could be achieved through means visually distinctive without being physically literal). That reframing unlocked a design direction that satisfied both requirements simultaneously without trading them off. The standard view required 34 directions to be explored before the resolution was found.

Outcome: the Kardion MCS Controller received FDA approval, passing the regulatory evaluation as submitted with no design changes required. Operator feedback post-deployment: multiple doctors told Kardion the controller is one of the best designed tools they had seen in a long time. Evidence basis: FDA approval is documented and verifiable. Operator feedback is client-reported from multiple sources across two timepoints.

## What Creative Navy's Critical Systems Design method addresses here

Creative Navy's Critical Systems Design method addresses the hard-to-sell problem by treating the sales and evaluation context as a design requirement alongside the operational context. The competitive vector identified during Concept Convergence is not only an operational claim — it is the design statement that makes the product's value legible to evaluators at the moment they encounter it.

Domain learning establishes what evaluators are looking for at the moment of assessment. In the Triopsis engagement, understanding the operational mental models of schedulers and operations managers was what made it possible to design an interface that communicated its logic to a prospect in a demo without narration. In the Kardion engagement, understanding both the procurement audience's expectations and the clinical user's requirements was what established that the tension between marketing impact and clinical clarity could be resolved rather than traded off.

The outcome this addresses is not a feature or a layout — it is the commercial consequence of an interface that carries its own argument. A product whose interface communicates its value without requiring mediation can be demoed by more people, to more audiences, in more contexts, at greater scale.
